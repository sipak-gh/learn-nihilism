# Chapter 1 — What Is Nihilism?

"Nihilism" is one of those words everyone uses and few define. A teenager says it with a shrug, a preacher says it with alarm, a critic uses it to dismiss a film, a philosopher uses it to name a thesis about moral facts, and a historian uses it for a group of Russian students in the 1860s who cut their hair short and dissected frogs. This chapter sorts out the meanings. It separates *nihilism as an accusation* (what people call their enemies) from *nihilism as a position* (what someone might actually argue for), lays out the main kinds (existential, moral, epistemological, metaphysical, political, cosmic), introduces Nietzsche's distinction between passive and active nihilism, and marks off nihilism from its neighbors: skepticism, pessimism, relativism, atheism and, importantly, Buddhism. It ends with the question that drives the rest of the course: if nihilism were true, what would follow for how you live? Previous: [Course overview](README.md). Next: [Chapter 2 — Ancient Roots](02-ancient-roots.md).

## A word born as an insult

Start with a breakfast. In the summer of 1859, in Turgenev's novel, young Arkady Kirsanov comes home from university to his father's small estate. He brings a friend, Yevgeny Bazarov, a medical student who cuts up frogs, sneers at poetry, and calls the old men of the house "romantics." At breakfast Arkady's uncle, the dandy Pavel Petrovich, asks what exactly this Bazarov *is*:

> 'He's a nihilist.'
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. 5 (tr. Constance Garnett)

The father, Nikolai, tries to work it out from the Latin:

> 'A nihilist,' said Nikolai Petrovitch. 'That's from the Latin, _nihil_, _nothing_, as far as I can judge; the word must mean a man who ... who accepts nothing?'
>
> 'Say, "who respects nothing,"' put in Pavel Petrovitch, and he set to work on the butter again.
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. 5 (tr. Constance Garnett)

Arkady corrects them both:

> A nihilist is a man who does not bow down before any authority, who does not take any principle on faith, whatever reverence that principle may be enshrined in.
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. 5 (tr. Constance Garnett)

Notice the three definitions in a dozen lines. The father's is *logical*: a nihilist accepts nothing. The uncle's is *moral and social*: a nihilist respects nothing, which is an insult. The nephew's is *epistemic*: a nihilist takes nothing on authority, which sounds like a description of a good scientist. The whole history of the word is already on the breakfast table. The same label can mean "someone who believes there is nothing," "someone who values nothing," and "someone who refuses to believe without evidence." People who use the word against an opponent tend to slide from the third meaning to the second without noticing.

That slide is the first lesson of this course. For most of its history "nihilism" has been an **accusation** rather than a **self-description**. Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi used it in 1799 to accuse Fichte's idealism of dissolving the world and God into the self ([Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md)). Russian conservatives used it against radical students; the students mostly preferred other names ([Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md)). Nietzsche, the thinker most associated with nihilism, used it chiefly as a charge against Christianity, Schopenhauer and modern culture, and claimed to be its diagnostician and its cure ([Chapters 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) and [7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md)). Heidegger later turned the charge against Nietzsche himself. And centuries earlier, Indian critics accused the Buddhist philosopher Nagarjuna of being a nihilist, a charge he explicitly rejected ([Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md)).

So when you meet the word, ask two questions before anything else:

1. **Who is saying it, about whom?** Is a person describing their own view, or labeling someone else's?
2. **Nothing of what?** Nothing exists? Nothing is known? Nothing is valuable? Nothing is worth doing? Nothing in the current order deserves to survive?

The second question is the key to the rest of this chapter.

## A working definition

Philosophers who write about nihilism usually agree on a schema rather than a single definition. Here it is in a form you can reuse:

> **Nihilism about D** is the view that nothing in domain D has property P, where P is a property that people have standardly taken things in D to have, and that seemed to matter.

Fill in the blanks and you get the kinds:

| Kind | Domain D | Property denied P | Slogan |
|------|----------|-------------------|--------|
| Existential | human lives | meaning, point, worth | "Life is meaningless." |
| Moral (ethical) | actions, characters | objective rightness, goodness, obligation | "Nothing is really right or wrong." |
| Epistemological | beliefs | knowledge, justification | "Nothing can be known." |
| Metaphysical / ontological | the world, objects | existence, or a stable reality | "Nothing (really) exists," or "there is no real world behind appearances." |
| Political | institutions | legitimacy, right to exist | "Everything existing should be destroyed." |
| Cosmic | the universe as a whole | purpose, care, intelligibility | "The universe is indifferent." |

Donald Crosby's *The Specter of the Absurd* (1988) gives a five-part version of this map (political, moral, epistemological, cosmic, existential), and Karen Carr's *The Banalization of Nihilism* (1992) gives a slightly different one that splits "nothing is true" (*alethiological* nihilism, from Greek *alētheia*, truth) from "nothing can be known." The labels vary; the schema does not.

The schema makes three things clear at once.

**First, nihilism is local before it is global.** You can be a nihilist about one domain and not another. A moral error theorist like J. L. Mackie denied objective moral values but had no doubt that tables and electrons exist and that science gives knowledge ([Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md)). A cosmic nihilist can think the universe has no purpose while finding her own life full of meaning. "Global nihilism," denying every P in every D at once, is rare and, as you will see, hard to state coherently.

**Second, nihilism is denial, not doubt.** The nihilist says "there is no P," not "I am unsure whether there is P." That separates nihilism from **skepticism**, which suspends judgment. The ancient Pyrrhonist skeptics refused to say even that nothing can be known, since that too would be a dogmatic claim ([Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md)). Epistemological nihilism, by contrast, *asserts* that knowledge is impossible, and thereby walks into an obvious trap: does it know that?

**Third, nihilism is a claim about a lack, not a negative evaluation.** The nihilist says the world lacks value, not that it has a lot of *negative* value. That separates nihilism from **pessimism**, the view that existence is, on balance, bad. Schopenhauer was a pessimist, not a nihilist: he thought suffering is real, that compassion is really good, and that some states (aesthetic contemplation, saintly resignation) are really better than others ([Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md)). A thoroughgoing nihilist could not even say life is *bad*, because "bad" is a value term too.

## Existential nihilism: "What profit hath a man?"

The oldest and most felt form is the claim that human life has no point. Its classic text was written perhaps in the third century BCE by a Hebrew sage who calls himself *Qoheleth*, "the Preacher" or "the Gatherer":

> Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity. What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the sun?
>
> — *Ecclesiastes* 1:2–3 (King James Version)

The Hebrew word translated "vanity" is *hevel*, literally "vapor" or "breath": something real but fleeting and ungraspable, like your breath on a cold morning. Qoheleth's complaint is not that nothing exists, but that nothing *lasts* and nothing *adds up*:

> The thing that hath been, it is that which shall be; and that which is done is that which shall be done: and there is no new thing under the sun.
>
> — *Ecclesiastes* 1:9 (King James Version)

You can turn the existential worry into an argument. One common version runs:

1. A life is meaningful only if it makes some lasting difference, or serves some purpose beyond itself.
2. Nothing you do makes a lasting difference: you will die, those who remember you will die, the species will end, the sun will burn out.
3. There is no purpose beyond human lives that your life could serve (no God, no cosmic plan).
4. Therefore your life is not meaningful.

Every premise is contestable, and much of this course is about contesting them. Premise 1 assumes that meaning requires permanence or transcendence. Thomas Nagel's essay "The Absurd" (1971) argues that permanence would not help: if a life of seventy years is pointless, a life of seventy million years would be just as pointless, since the question "but what is it all *for*?" can be asked of any span. Susan Wolf, in *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters* (2010), argues that meaning arises "when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness": when you love something that is genuinely worth loving, regardless of how long it lasts. Premise 3 is precisely what religious traditions deny. These replies get their full hearing in [Chapter 15](15-beyond-nihilism.md).

Notice something about Qoheleth, though. He is not a philosopher defending a thesis. He is a man reporting an experience: he tried pleasure, wisdom, building, wealth, and found each one "vexation of spirit." That *experiential* side of existential nihilism matters. Tolstoy's *A Confession* (1882) describes a famous, rich, healthy writer of about fifty who suddenly cannot answer the simplest question (what will come of everything I am doing?), hides a cord so as not to hang himself with it, and stops taking his gun hunting for fear of turning it on himself. Existential nihilism is often a *mood* before it is a *view*, and part of what philosophers argue about is whether the mood is a perception of the truth or a symptom of something else (fatigue, depression, a culture losing its bearings). Nietzsche, as you will see, thought it was both.

## Moral nihilism: "Everything is lawful"

Moral nihilism denies that there are objective moral facts: nothing is really, mind-independently, right or wrong, good or evil. It comes in at least three varieties, which are easily confused:

- **Error theory**: moral statements try to state objective facts ("torturing for fun is wrong" aims to describe a feature of the world), but there are no such facts, so all positive moral statements are false. This is Mackie's position in *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977).
- **Noncognitivism** (emotivism, expressivism): moral statements never tried to state facts; "torture is wrong" expresses an attitude, roughly "Boo, torture!" On this view there is nothing to be in error about. A. J. Ayer held a version of this in *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936). Whether noncognitivism counts as "nihilism" is disputed: many expressivists, like Simon Blackburn and Allan Gibbard, insist they can say everything a moral realist says.
- **Amoralism** or practical nihilism: a person who simply does not care about morality, whatever its metaphysical status. This is a character, not a theory. An error theorist can be scrupulously honest; a moral realist can be a scoundrel.

The popular picture of moral nihilism comes from Dostoevsky's *The Brothers Karamazov* (1879–80). You may have seen the line "If God does not exist, everything is permitted" attributed to Ivan Karamazov. It is a paraphrase: no character says exactly that in the novel. What the text gives you, in Constance Garnett's translation, is the seminarian Rakitin sneering at Ivan's "theory":

> And did you hear his stupid theory just now: if there's no immortality of the soul, then there's no virtue, and everything is lawful.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book II, ch. 7 (tr. Constance Garnett)

Notice that Rakitin immediately rejects the theory: "Humanity will find in itself the power to live for virtue even without believing in immortality." Dostoevsky stages the debate; he does not simply assert the conditional. You will examine the conditional in [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md). For now, separate two claims that the slogan blurs:

1. **The metaphysical claim**: without God (or immortality), there are no objective moral facts.
2. **The practical claim**: without God (or immortality), people will behave badly.

The first is a thesis in metaethics. Plato's *Euthyphro* already raised a problem for it: if the good is good *because* God commands it, morality looks arbitrary; if God commands it *because* it is good, the good does not depend on God after all (see [the Ethics course, Chapter 4](../learn-ethics/04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md)). The second is an empirical prediction about human psychology, and it is not obvious that it is true. Moral nihilism, properly speaking, is only the first kind of claim, minus the theology: there are no objective moral facts, period.

## Epistemological nihilism: "Nothing can be known"

The fifth-century BCE orator Gorgias of Leontini wrote a treatise, *On Not-Being*, whose argument survives in two later summaries (one in Sextus Empiricus, one in a short work attributed to Aristotle's school). Its three theses, roughly paraphrased, are: nothing exists; if anything existed, it could not be known; if it could be known, it could not be communicated. Scholars still argue whether Gorgias meant this seriously, as a parody of Parmenides' arguments about Being, or as a rhetorical showpiece ([Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md)). Either way, the second and third theses are the template of epistemological nihilism.

The standard objection is self-refutation. If someone asserts "nothing can be known," we can ask: do you know that? If yes, the claim is false. If no, why should anyone accept it? The objection is strong against the flat-footed assertion. It is weaker against more careful versions: "no belief is *justified* in the strong sense philosophers have demanded" does not obviously undermine itself, and the radical skeptic can present her arguments as a ladder to be kicked away once climbed. The ancient Pyrrhonists did exactly this, and that is why they were *not* epistemological nihilists. They suspended judgment even about whether knowledge is possible.

Modern versions of epistemological nihilism tend to be about *truth* rather than knowledge. Nietzsche's notebooks contain the line, often quoted, that there are no facts, only interpretations. Whether Nietzsche held a global "nothing is true" view, or only denied a particular metaphysical picture of truth as correspondence to a world-in-itself, is one of the central disputes in Nietzsche scholarship (Maudemarie Clark's *Nietzsche on Truth and Philosophy*, 1990, argues for the second reading). You will meet it again in [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md).

## Metaphysical nihilism: from "nothing exists" to "no tables"

Metaphysical (or ontological) nihilism covers several distinct claims, and the word is used in strikingly different ways in different parts of philosophy.

1. **The real world is nothing.** In German philosophy around 1800, "nihilism" meant the charge that a philosophy dissolves reality into appearances or into the activity of the thinking self. Jacobi's complaint against Fichte was that if everything is a product of the "I," then everything outside the I is nothing, and the I itself, being pure activity, is empty too ([Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md)).
2. **No real world behind appearances.** Nietzsche's version: the "true world" of Plato and Christianity is a fiction, and once that is seen, the "apparent world" loses its standard of comparison too. His late fragments describe this as nihilism's "final form," which "comprises _a denial of a metaphysical world,_ and which forbids itself all belief in a _real_ world" (*Will to Power* §12).
3. **It is possible that nothing exists.** In analytic metaphysics, "metaphysical nihilism" sometimes names the thesis that there is a possible world with no concrete objects at all (Thomas Baldwin's "subtraction argument," 1996).
4. **Mereological nihilism.** The thesis that there are no composite objects: there are particles "arranged tablewise," but no tables. Theodore Sider defends it in "Against Parthood" (2013); Cian Dorr and Gideon Rosen explore a fictionalist version in "Composition as a Fiction" (2002); Peter van Inwagen's *Material Beings* (1990) comes close, but allows living organisms.

None of these is about the meaning of life, and the mereological nihilist may be a cheerful person who simply has views about parts and wholes. That is a useful reminder: the word "nihilism" alone tells you almost nothing until you know the domain.

## Political nihilism: clearing the ground

In the public imagination of the 1870s and 1880s, a "nihilist" was a bomb-thrower. The assassination of Tsar Alexander II in 1881 by the revolutionary group *Narodnaya Volya* ("People's Will") was reported across Europe as a nihilist plot. Political nihilism, in this sense, holds that existing institutions (state, church, family, property) have no legitimacy and should be destroyed, with little said about what should replace them.

Bazarov's own statement of the program is a model of the genre:

> 'We act by virtue of what we recognise as beneficial,' observed Bazarov. 'At the present time, negation is the most beneficial of all--and we deny----'
>
> 'Everything?'
>
> 'Everything!'
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. 10 (tr. Constance Garnett)

When Nikolai Petrovich objects that one must also build, Bazarov answers: "That's not our business now.... The ground wants clearing first."

But listen to someone who was there. Peter Kropotkin, a prince turned geographer turned anarchist, lived through the 1860s as a young man. His *Memoirs of a Revolutionist* (1899) insists that Western Europe got the word wrong:

> To confuse Nihilism with terrorism is as wrong as to confuse a philosophical movement like Stoicism or Positivism with a political movement, such as, for example, republicanism.
>
> — Peter Kropotkin, *Memoirs of a Revolutionist*, Part Fourth, ch. XII

For Kropotkin, the core of Russian nihilism was not a doctrine about nothing but a demand for sincerity:

> First of all, the Nihilist declared war upon what may be described as the 'conventional lies of civilized mankind.' Absolute sincerity was his distinctive feature, and in the name of that sincerity he gave up, and asked others to give up, those superstitions, prejudices, habits, and customs which their own reason could not justify.
>
> — Peter Kropotkin, *Memoirs of a Revolutionist*, Part Fourth, ch. XII

This is a striking twist. The Russian "nihilist" was, by his own lights, intensely committed to values: truthfulness, science, the emancipation of women, the welfare of peasants. What he denied was the authority of *inherited* values that could not justify themselves. The label "nihilist" was applied from outside, by people who could not imagine values without their own traditional foundations. Hold onto this pattern. It recurs.

## Cosmic nihilism: the silence of infinite spaces

Cosmic nihilism says the universe as a whole has no purpose, no concern for us, and perhaps no intelligible order that our minds are fitted to grasp. It is less a thesis about human values than a picture of our situation. The scientific revolution gave it a new vividness. Pascal, a brilliant mathematician and a devout Christian, put the experience in a single line:

> The eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* 206 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

Pascal's point is not that the universe is evil, nor that values are unreal. It is that the cosmos, measured by the new astronomy, is vast and mute; it does not speak to us, does not answer, does not notice. Nietzsche put the same thought as a historical observation in his notes:

> Since Copernicus man has been rolling away from the centre towards _x_.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, "A Plan," 5 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Cosmic nihilism is compatible with many attitudes. Lucretius, the Roman Epicurean poet, believed the universe was a purposeless swirl of atoms and found that thought *consoling*: no angry gods, nothing to fear after death ([Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md)). The twentieth-century horror writer H. P. Lovecraft found the same thought terrifying. Bertrand Russell, in "A Free Man's Worship" (1903), found it a reason for defiant, tender solidarity among mortals. The facts, if they are facts, do not dictate the mood. That gap between fact and mood will turn out to be where most of the philosophical action is.

## Passive and active nihilism

So far you have a typology by domain. Nietzsche adds a second axis: not *what* is denied, but *how* the denial is lived. His late notebooks, published after his death as *The Will to Power*, open with a dramatic sentence:

> Nihilism is at our door: whence comes this most gruesome of all guests to us?
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, "A Plan," 1 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

(Other translators render the German *der unheimlichste aller Gäste* as "the uncanniest of all guests"; *unheimlich* means eerie, literally "un-homely.") Nietzsche's definition is brief:

> What does Nihilism mean?--_That the highest values are losing their value._ There is no bourne. There is no answer to the question: "to what purpose?"
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power* §2 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

("Bourne" is an old word for a goal or boundary; Kaufmann's later translation has "The aim is lacking.") Notice two things. Nihilism here is not a theory someone invents; it is an *event*, something that happens to a culture when its highest values (God, truth, the moral order) lose their grip. And the values devalue *themselves*: Nietzsche argues that Christian morality cultivated truthfulness so intensely that truthfulness finally turned on Christianity and exposed it as a fiction (§§3–5).

Then comes the distinction:

> Nihilism. It may be _two things_:--
>
> A. Nihilism as a sign of _enhanced spiritual strength_: active Nihilism.
>
> B. Nihilism as a sign of the _collapse_ and _decline_ of spiritual _strength_: passive Nihilism.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power* §22 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

**Passive nihilism** is the weary response: the old values no longer convince, no new ones appear, and the person or culture retreats into whatever "relieving, which heals, becalms, or stupefies" (§23): entertainment, comfort, sedatives of every kind. Nietzsche's later image for this condition is the "last man" of *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, who has invented happiness and blinks ([Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)).

**Active nihilism** is the strong response: it does not merely lament that the old values are hollow, it tears them down to make room.

> Nihilism is not only a meditating over the "in vain!"--not only the belief that everything deserves to perish; but one actually puts one's shoulder to the plough; _one destroys._
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power* §24 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Active nihilism is not Nietzsche's final goal. It is a transitional stage, a demolition crew. What lies beyond it, the "revaluation of all values," is the subject of [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md).

Bernard Reginster's *The Affirmation of Life* (2006) adds a sharp analytical distinction within Nietzsche's notion. **Nihilism as disorientation**: the belief that there are no objective values, so you have nothing to steer by. **Nihilism as despair**: the belief that your highest values are real but *cannot be realized* in this world (for example, if you value a happiness free of all suffering, and suffering is unavoidable). The cures are different. Disorientation needs a new account of where values come from; despair needs a revision of which values you hold.

## What nihilism is not

Because "nihilism" is so often a charge, it has been pinned on many views that do not deserve it. Here are the main confusions.

**Atheism is not nihilism.** An atheist denies that God exists. Whether anything follows about meaning or morality depends on further premises, the ones disputed in the Dostoevsky section above. Many atheists (Epicurus, Hume, Mill, Russell) held robust moral views.

**Relativism is not nihilism.** The relativist says moral truths exist but are relative to cultures or individuals ("slavery is wrong-for-us"). The nihilist says there are no moral truths at all. The two often travel together in arguments, but they are distinct theses ([the Ethics course, Chapter 3](../learn-ethics/03-relativism-and-egoism.md)).

**Pessimism is not nihilism**, as noted above: the pessimist evaluates; the nihilist denies that there is anything to evaluate.

**Cynicism, in the modern sense, is not nihilism.** The modern cynic believes people are selfish and ideals are masks. That is a view about human motives, not about value. Ironically the ancient Cynics, Diogenes and his barrel, were fierce moralists ([the Stoicism course, Chapter 2](../learn-stoicism/02-before-the-stoa.md)).

**Buddhism is not nihilism.** This needs care, because the charge has a long pedigree. Nietzsche himself called Buddhism the "most renowned form" of passive nihilism (*Will to Power* §23), a "yearning for nonentity" (§3). Nineteenth-century European scholars, reading early translations, often took *nirvāṇa* (literally "blowing out," as of a flame) to mean annihilation, and Roger-Pol Droit's *The Cult of Nothingness* (1997, English 2003) documents how Europe invented a "Buddhism of nothingness" to project its own anxieties onto. But the Buddhist texts themselves explicitly reject nihilism. The early discourses present the Buddha's teaching as a "middle way" between two extremes: eternalism (*śāśvatavāda*, the view that there is a permanent self) and annihilationism (*ucchedavāda*, the view that the self is destroyed at death and nothing matters). In one discourse the Buddha complains that some ascetics misrepresent him as a *venayika*, "one who leads astray" or "nihilist," who teaches the annihilation of an existing being, and denies the charge. The second-century philosopher Nagarjuna, whose doctrine of emptiness (*śūnyatā*) sounds most nihilistic to Western ears, argues that emptiness means that things lack *intrinsic, independent existence* (*svabhāva*), not that they do not exist at all; things exist dependently, and that is precisely why ethics, practice and the path make sense. You will read his argument in [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md). For now, register the pattern: a tradition whose central concern is the end of suffering, with a rich ethics of compassion, gets called "nihilist" by outsiders who assume that without a permanent self or a creator God, nothing can matter. That assumption is exactly what is in question.

## Why it matters

Why spend a whole course on a word whose meaning keeps shifting?

**Because the questions are real.** Whatever you call them, the questions "Does anything matter?", "Are moral claims ever true?", "Can I know anything?" and "Is there a point to the whole?" are among the oldest and most persistent in philosophy. Every serious system of thought has to give some answer, and many systems can be read as *responses* to the nihilist threat. Plato's Forms, the Stoics' providential *logos*, Kant's moral law, Hegel's history, Marx's revolution, Sartre's freedom: each is, among other things, a way of showing that the abyss is not the last word.

**Because nihilism is a diagnosis of modernity.** From Jacobi to Nietzsche to Heidegger to contemporary writers, a recurring claim is that the modern world (science, secularization, markets, bureaucracy) produces nihilism as a side effect, by stripping the world of inherent meaning while leaving people who still need meaning. Whether this diagnosis is right is a historical and philosophical question you will be able to judge for yourself by [Chapter 14](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md).

**Because the arguments for nihilism are instructive even if they fail.** Mackie's argument from "queerness" forces you to say what a moral fact would be. Nagel's absurd forces you to say what you mean by "point." Nietzsche's genealogy forces you to ask where your values came from and whether that history undermines them. You do not have to become a nihilist to learn from the nihilist; you have to take the nihilist seriously enough to answer.

**Because it bears on how you live.** Here is the puzzle that will follow you through the course. Suppose you came to believe that nothing has objective value. Would anything in your life *have* to change? You would still feel hunger, love, curiosity, pain. You would still prefer some outcomes to others. Hume would say your passions would go on exactly as before; reason never moved you anyway ([Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md)). Nietzsche would say that a great deal would change, but over centuries, not over breakfast. Dostoevsky would say you would eventually find yourself in Raskolnikov's garret with an axe. Camus would say you would face the question of suicide and could answer it with revolt ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)). Which of these predictions is right is not only an academic question. Keep a notebook as you work through the course: at the end of each chapter, write down whether you were moved toward or away from any form of nihilism, and why.

## Living it

Try a two-week experiment based on the schema above.

1. **Map your own "nothing."** On a single page, write the six domains from the table (life, morality, knowledge, reality, institutions, the cosmos). For each, write one sentence stating the nihilist thesis in your own words, then rate how plausible it seems to you from 0 to 10. Most people find they are nihilists about one or two domains and not the others. Notice which ones, and notice whether your ratings hang together. (If you rate moral nihilism at 9, can you also rate "the Russian state was illegitimate" at 9? That is a moral claim.)
2. **Catch the accusation.** For the next two weeks, every time you hear or read the word "nihilist" or "nihilistic" (in reviews, politics, social media), write down who used it, about whom, and which "nothing" was meant. You will quickly see Pavel Petrovich's move, from "takes nothing on authority" to "respects nothing," made again and again.
3. **Test Qoheleth on a single day.** At the end of one ordinary day, ask of each main activity his question: "What profit hath a man of all his labour?" Then ask Wolf's question: "Was I engaged with something worth engaging with?" Keep both answers. They are not the same question, and the difference between them is a large part of what this course is about.
4. **Kropotkin's sincerity audit.** Kropotkin's nihilists refused "conventional lies." For one day, notice each small conventional lie you tell (the smile you don't mean, the praise you don't feel). Do not necessarily stop telling them; Bazarov's rudeness did him no credit. But ask of each one whether your reason could justify it. This is "nihilism" in the Russian sense: not a denial of value, but a refusal to accept value on authority alone.

## Summary

- "Nihilism" (Latin *nihil*, "nothing") has been more often an **accusation** than a **self-description**. Always ask who is calling whom a nihilist, and "nothing of what?"
- A useful schema: nihilism about a domain D denies that things in D have a property P that seemed to matter. This yields **existential, moral, epistemological, metaphysical, political and cosmic** nihilism. Typologies by Crosby (1988) and Carr (1992) refine it.
- Nihilism is **denial**, not doubt (unlike skepticism), and a claim of **absence**, not negative value (unlike pessimism). It is also distinct from atheism, relativism and cynicism.
- **Moral nihilism** has varieties: error theory (Mackie), noncognitivism (Ayer; disputed as "nihilism"), and practical amoralism. "If God does not exist, everything is permitted" is a paraphrase, not a line in *The Brothers Karamazov*.
- **Russian nihilism**, by an insider's account (Kropotkin), was a movement of radical sincerity and scientific materialism, not of believing in nothing, and not identical with terrorism.
- Nietzsche defines nihilism as the event in which "the highest values are losing their value," and distinguishes **passive** (weary, sedative) from **active** (destructive, a sign of strength) nihilism. Reginster distinguishes nihilism as **disorientation** from nihilism as **despair**.
- **Buddhism is not nihilism.** Its texts reject annihilationism explicitly; Nagarjuna's emptiness denies intrinsic existence, not existence. The European "cult of nothingness" was largely a projection.
- The arguments for nihilism are worth studying even if they fail, because answering them forces you to say what meaning, value and knowledge are.

## Dialogues

### Dialogue 1 — On whether "nothing matters" can be said sincerely

**Socrates:** A friend tells you, after a bad month, "Honestly, nothing matters." You nod. Did you agree with her?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I suppose I did. When you look at it from far enough away, none of our worries last. In a hundred years nobody will remember her job or her breakup.

**Socrates:** Then let me ask about her, not about the hundred years. Did she seem indifferent when she said it?

**You:** No. She seemed upset.

**Socrates:** Upset about what, if nothing matters?

**You:** About the job and the breakup, I suppose.

**Socrates:** So the sentence "nothing matters" was spoken by someone to whom at least two things mattered a great deal. Is that a contradiction, or something else?

**You:** Maybe it isn't a contradiction. Maybe she meant that those things *shouldn't* matter, that it is irrational to care about them since they don't last.

**Socrates:** Good; now she has a thesis. Let us state it: "Whatever does not last is not worth caring about." Is that the premise the Preacher of Ecclesiastes uses when he asks, "What profit hath a man of all his labour"?

**You:** It seems close. He keeps saying that one generation passes and another comes, and that there is no remembrance.

**Socrates:** And does the Preacher, having said this, stop caring about anything?

**You:** No. Later in the book he tells people to eat their bread with joy and to enjoy life with the person they love. So he seems to care about some things anyway.

**Socrates:** Then either he is inconsistent, or his premise was not "whatever does not last is worthless" but something milder. What milder thing could he mean?

**You:** Maybe: "Nothing you do will add up to a permanent gain, so stop trying to secure one, and enjoy what is given while it lasts."

**Socrates:** Is that nihilism?

**You:** Not by the schema in the chapter. It doesn't deny that the bread and the joy have value. It denies that anything has *lasting* value, and maybe that we can control outcomes.

**Socrates:** Now return to your friend. Is she an existential nihilist, or a person who has learned, painfully, that what she valued can be lost?

**You:** The second, probably. But I am not sure the first is false. Maybe her feelings are just stubborn, and the truth is that nothing matters.

**Socrates:** Then tell me what you would need to know to decide. Must something last forever to matter? Thomas Nagel suggests that if seventy years are pointless, seventy million would be too. If permanence does not supply meaning, can its absence take meaning away?

**You:** If permanence doesn't help, then the argument from impermanence fails. That doesn't prove things matter, though. It just removes one reason for thinking they don't.

**Socrates:** That is a real gain. We have not found what makes things matter, but we have found that one popular road to "nothing matters" is blocked. And we noticed something about the sentence itself: it is usually spoken by people who care a great deal. Keep that in mind; Nietzsche will call it a sign.

</details>

### Dialogue 2 — On being called a nihilist

**Pavel Petrovich:** Young man, my nephew tells me your friend Bazarov is a "nihilist," a man who respects nothing. You seem to admire him. Are you one too?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Pavel Petrovich in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I'm not sure I accept your definition. Arkady said a nihilist takes no principle on faith. That's different from respecting nothing.

**Pavel Petrovich:** Is it? Every principle I hold I took on faith first: from my father, from my church, from the code of a gentleman. If you refuse to take principles on faith, you will have none. We shall see how you exist in a vacuum.

**Kropotkin:** If I may interrupt, sir. I knew many of these young people. They took a great deal very seriously: science, the education of women, the condition of the peasants, and above all sincerity. They declared war on the "conventional lies of civilized mankind." Is that respecting nothing?

**Pavel Petrovich:** It is respecting only what they themselves choose to respect. Which is to say, respecting themselves.

**Kropotkin:** It is respecting what reason can justify. You call it pride. They called it honesty.

**Pavel Petrovich:** (to you) And you? Can your reason justify every principle you live by?

**You:** No. I keep promises partly because I was raised to, and I couldn't give you a complete argument for why promises bind.

**Pavel Petrovich:** Then by your friend's standard, you should stop keeping them.

**You:** That doesn't follow. Not having a complete argument isn't the same as having a reason to reject something. A scientist without a complete theory of gravity doesn't stop believing that things fall.

**Kropotkin:** Just so. The nihilist I knew refused principles that could not *survive* examination, not every principle that had not *yet* been examined.

**Pavel Petrovich:** A fine distinction. Did Bazarov observe it, when he told me he denied "everything"?

**You:** Probably not. Bazarov seems to slide from "examine everything" to "deny everything." Those are different, and the second is as dogmatic as anything you believe.

**Pavel Petrovich:** So you concede he is a nihilist in my sense?

**You:** I concede that Bazarov, at that moment, was making a sweeping denial he couldn't support. But the word "nihilist" was doing two jobs in our conversation: naming a method (critical examination) and naming a vice (contempt). I think you used the second to discredit the first.

**Kropotkin:** And Bazarov used the first to excuse the second. Neither of you is innocent.

**You:** Then the useful question isn't "Are you a nihilist?" It's "Which of your principles can survive examination, and what do you do with the ones that can't?"

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On which kind of nihilist you are

**Nietzsche:** You have read my definition: the highest values lose their value. Tell me, then, which values have lost their value for you?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Nietzsche in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I don't believe in a God who guarantees a moral order, so I suppose the idea of a cosmic purpose has lost its value for me. But I still value honesty, kindness and understanding.

**Nietzsche:** You value honesty. Where did you learn to value it so much?

**You:** From my parents, my schooling. Probably from a culture shaped by Christianity, if that is where you are going.

**Nietzsche:** That is exactly where I am going. In my notes I say that among the forces morality reared was truthfulness, and that truthfulness in the end turns against morality itself. Your honesty killed your God. Will it stop there?

**You:** Why shouldn't it? I can be honest that there is no God and still value kindness.

**Nietzsche:** Can you say *why* kindness is valuable, without borrowing the old reasons? "Every soul is precious in the sight of God"? "Love thy neighbor"?

**You:** Kindness reduces suffering, and suffering is bad.

**Nietzsche:** Is it? Is all suffering bad? Does not every great thing (a work of art, a mastered skill, a hard-won insight) cost suffering? Your "suffering is bad" may be another shadow of the dead God.

**You:** Maybe some suffering is worth it. But that is a claim about value too. You're not denying all value; you're questioning which values are mine and which are inherited.

**Nietzsche:** Good. Now the other question. When you notice that your inherited values are hollow, what do you feel: weariness or appetite?

**You:** Some of each. Sometimes I just want to stop thinking about it and watch something.

**Nietzsche:** That is the passive response: whatever "heals, becalms, or stupefies." And the other part?

**You:** Sometimes it feels like freedom: if nothing is handed down, I have to work out what I care about.

**Nietzsche:** That is the beginning of the active response. But beware: destruction is easy, and it is not the goal.

**You:** Then what is the goal? Making up new values? That sounds arbitrary.

**Nietzsche:** That is the right objection, and you will not answer it this week. Here is what we have: you are not a nihilist about value in general; you are a nihilist about some inherited justifications for value. And you have noticed that the loss can feel like exhaustion or like freedom. Which it becomes depends partly on you.

**You:** I can accept that much. But I want to know whether "partly on me" means values are just whatever I choose. If so, I'm not sure I've escaped nihilism at all.

**Nietzsche:** Keep that worry. It is the best thing you will take into my chapters.

</details>

# Chapter 2 — Ancient Roots

The word "nihilism" is barely two hundred years old, but the thoughts it names are ancient. This chapter visits five places where someone stared into one of the "nothings" of [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md) long before the word existed: the Hebrew sage of *Ecclesiastes*, who found everything to be "vapor"; the sophist Gorgias, who argued that nothing exists; the Pyrrhonist skeptics, who suspended judgment on everything and claimed to find peace; the Cyrenaics, one of whom lectured so persuasively on the misery of life that a king banned him; and Lucretius, who told his readers that death is nothing to them. The chapter ends in India with Nagarjuna, whose doctrine of emptiness was attacked as nihilism by his opponents, and who turned the charge back on them. The goal is twofold: to see that nihilist thoughts are perennial, and to see that ancient thinkers who entertained them almost never ended where modern caricatures of "nihilism" end. Previous: [Chapter 1 — What Is Nihilism?](01-what-is-nihilism.md). Next: [Chapter 3 — The Word and Its Birth](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md).

## A warning about anachronism

Before the tour, a caution. When you read ancient texts looking for "nihilism," you risk finding what you brought. The Greeks had no word for it; the closest Greek terms are *ouden* ("nothing") and *to mē on* ("what is not"), which belong to metaphysics, not to moods. The Hebrew sage had no concept of "values" in the modern sense. Indian philosophers did have a relevant term, *nāstika* ("one who says 'it is not'"), but it meant someone who rejected the authority of the Vedas or the reality of karma and rebirth, which made Buddhists *nāstikas* by definition in some Hindu classifications, whatever they believed about value.

So the question this chapter asks is not "Who were the ancient nihilists?" but: **Which ancient arguments and experiences anticipate the denials catalogued in Chapter 1, and what did their authors do with them?** The answer is surprising. Almost every ancient thinker who pressed hard on "nothing" used it as a *route to something*: joy, tranquillity, freedom from fear, liberation. The ancient world has plenty of negation and very few nihilists.

## The wisdom of Silenus

Start with the darkest Greek thought, because it sets the stakes. Nietzsche, in his first book, retells a folk tale he found in ancient sources (it is preserved in a fragment of Aristotle's lost dialogue *Eudemus*, quoted by Plutarch). King Midas hunts down Silenus, the drunken companion of Dionysus, and asks him what is best for human beings:

> "Oh, wretched race of a day, children of chance and misery, why do ye compel me to say to you what it were most expedient for you not to hear? What is best of all is for ever beyond your reach: not to be born, not to _be_, to be _nothing._ The second best for you, however, is soon to die."
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Birth of Tragedy* §3 (tr. William A. Haussmann)

The same sentiment appears in the poet Theognis (lines 425–428) and in Sophocles' *Oedipus at Colonus* (lines 1224–1227).

Notice what kind of claim this is. It is not nihilism in the strict sense of [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md). Silenus does not deny that anything has value; he makes a ferociously *evaluative* claim: existence is worse than nonexistence. That is pessimism, the root of the tradition that runs through Schopenhauer ([Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md)) to contemporary antinatalism ([Chapter 13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md)). Nietzsche's own point in telling the story is that the Greeks *knew* this wisdom and built their gods, their art and their tragedy as a way of living with it: "The Greek knew and felt the terrors and horrors of existence." He calls the result "pessimism of strength." Keep that idea in reserve; it is the seed of everything in [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md).

## Ecclesiastes: the Preacher and the vapor

*Ecclesiastes* is the strangest book in the Hebrew Bible. Its narrator calls himself *Qoheleth*, a Hebrew word for someone who gathers an assembly; the Greek translators rendered it *Ekklēsiastēs*, "member of the assembly," whence the English "Preacher." He presents himself as a son of David and king in Jerusalem, which tradition took to mean Solomon. Most modern scholars date the book to the third century BCE, centuries after Solomon, on grounds of language (including Persian loanwords) and thought; the royal persona is a literary device.

The book's thesis is announced in the first lines and repeated like a drumbeat: *hevel havalim*, "vanity of vanities." *Hevel* means "vapor," "breath," "mist": the cloud of your breath in winter, there and then gone. Michael V. Fox, in *A Time to Tear Down and a Time to Build Up* (1999), argues that the best English equivalent is "absurd," in roughly Camus' sense: a disparity between what should be and what is, an affront to reason. Other scholars prefer "transient" (emphasizing brevity) or "futile" (emphasizing lack of result). The choice matters: "everything is transient" is a lament; "everything is absurd" is an accusation against the order of things.

### The experiment

Qoheleth does not argue from first principles. He runs an experiment on himself and reports the results. He tries wisdom, and finds that "in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow" (1:18). He tries pleasure, wine, building projects, gardens, slaves, silver, singers. Then he reviews the whole:

> Then I looked on all the works that my hands had wrought, and on the labour that I had laboured to do: and, behold, all was vanity and vexation of spirit, and there was no profit under the sun.
>
> — *Ecclesiastes* 2:11 (King James Version)

The word "profit" (Hebrew *yitron*, "what is left over," a surplus) is the key. Qoheleth asks an accountant's question: after all the labor, what remains on the balance sheet? Nothing you can keep. Death cancels the account, and it is no respecter of persons, or even of species:

> For that which befalleth the sons of men befalleth beasts; even one thing befalleth them: as the one dieth, so dieth the other; yea, they have all one breath; so that a man hath no preeminence above a beast: for all is vanity. All go unto one place; all are of the dust, and all turn to dust again.
>
> — *Ecclesiastes* 3:19–20 (King James Version)

Here is the argument implicit in the book, laid out:

1. A pursuit is worthwhile only if it yields a lasting surplus (*yitron*).
2. Death erases every surplus: wealth goes to heirs who may be fools (2:18–19), wisdom dies with the wise, and "the memory of them is forgotten" (9:5).
3. Therefore no pursuit is worthwhile.

And at this point Qoheleth hated life: "Therefore I hated life; because the work that is wrought under the sun is grievous unto me" (2:17). If the book ended here it would be a manifesto of existential nihilism.

### The turn to joy

It does not end there. Seven times the book returns to a refrain that seems to contradict its thesis:

> Go thy way, eat thy bread with joy, and drink thy wine with a merry heart; for God now accepteth thy works.
>
> — *Ecclesiastes* 9:7 (King James Version)

> Live joyfully with the wife whom thou lovest all the days of the life of thy vanity, which he hath given thee under the sun, all the days of thy vanity: for that is thy portion in this life, and in thy labour which thou takest under the sun. Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest.
>
> — *Ecclesiastes* 9:9–10 (King James Version)

How do the two strands fit? Scholars give three main answers.

1. **Contradiction, honestly left standing.** The book is a notebook of conflicting observations, not a treatise.
2. **The "preacher of joy" reading.** R. N. Whybray ("Qoheleth, Preacher of Joy," 1982) argues that the refrains are the book's real message: since no lasting profit is available, take the *portion* (*ḥeleq*) that is available, the daily gift of bread, wine, love and work. The argument is then not nihilist but a rejection of premise 1: worth does not require surplus.
3. **The pious frame.** The last verses, "Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man" (12:13), are widely judged to be an editor's addition (the voice shifts to third person in 12:9). On this reading, a later hand tamed a radical book to get it into the canon.

The second reading is the philosophically interesting one. It suggests a distinction you will meet again in Camus and in contemporary work on meaning ([Chapter 15](15-beyond-nihilism.md)): the difference between **meaning as achievement** (what you produce and leave behind) and **meaning as participation** (what you do and enjoy while you are here). Qoheleth's nihilism is about the first. His counsel is about the second. Notice too that *hevel* is not "nothing." Breath is real; it just does not last. The book is a long meditation on what you can rightly value in a world where nothing lasts, which is a different question from whether anything has value at all.

## Gorgias: "nothing exists"

Now cross to Greece in the fifth century BCE. Gorgias of Leontini, a Sicilian orator, arrived in Athens in 427 BCE as an ambassador and dazzled the city with a prose style so ornamented that "Gorgianic" became a byword. Plato named a dialogue after him. He also wrote a treatise with a provocative double title: *On Not-Being*, or *On Nature*. The original is lost. Two summaries survive: one in Sextus Empiricus (*Against the Logicians* I, also numbered *Against the Mathematicians* VII.65–87) and one in an anonymous treatise transmitted with Aristotle's works, *On Melissus, Xenophanes and Gorgias* (979a–980b). They differ in details, but agree on three theses:

1. Nothing exists.
2. Even if something existed, it could not be known (or thought).
3. Even if it could be known, it could not be communicated to another.

The arguments are clever. The first, in compressed paraphrase: if anything exists, it is either what-is or what-is-not. What-is-not does not exist, or it would both be and not be. What-is does not exist either: it would have to be eternal (hence unlimited, hence nowhere, hence nothing) or generated (from what-is, so it already existed, or from what-is-not, which is impossible). So nothing exists. The third thesis has an argument that still has teeth: we communicate by words, but words are not the things they name. A color is seen, a word is heard; how can what is heard convey what is seen? So what we pass to each other is never the thing itself, only *logos*, speech.

**How seriously did Gorgias mean it?** Three interpretations compete.

- **Parody.** The arguments imitate, step for step, the reasoning of Parmenides and Melissus, the Eleatic philosophers who argued that Being is one, eternal and unchanging and that not-being cannot be thought. Gorgias uses their own methods to reach the opposite conclusion, showing that Eleatic argument can prove anything. On this view the treatise is a *reductio* of a philosophical method, not a statement of belief.
- **Rhetorical showpiece.** Gorgias was a teacher of persuasion. His surviving *Encomium of Helen* ends by calling itself a *paignion*, a "plaything." *On Not-Being* may be a demonstration that a skilled speaker can make the weakest case compelling.
- **Serious philosophy.** G. B. Kerferd (*The Sophistic Movement*, 1981) and others argue that the third thesis, about the gap between language and reality, expresses a real view that underpins Gorgias' whole theory of rhetoric: since speech never transmits reality, persuasion works on the soul directly, like a drug.

Whatever Gorgias intended, he gave the West its first systematic argument for **ontological and epistemological nihilism**, and he did it as a performance. That pairing is not an accident. Global nihilism is hard to *state* without self-refutation (if nothing can be communicated, what has Gorgias just done?), so it tends to appear in ironic, playful or literary modes. You will see the same pattern in Stirner ([Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md)), in Dada and in Beckett ([Chapter 14](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md)).

## Pyrrhonism: the art of not concluding

### Pyrrho the painter

Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE) began as a painter, joined Alexander the Great's expedition to India with his teacher Anaxarchus, and came home with a way of life that astonished his contemporaries. He wrote nothing. Our main source, Diogenes Laertius (third century CE), gives a vivid and partly scandalous picture:

> For he used to say that nothing was honourable, or disgraceful, or just, or unjust. And on the same principle he asserted that there was no such thing as downright truth; but that men did everything in consequence of custom and law. For that nothing was any more this than that.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* IX.61 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

On its face this is moral and epistemological nihilism in one breath. And the anecdotes that follow are the stuff of caricature:

> And his life corresponded to his principles; for he never shunned anything, and never guarded against anything; encountering everything, even waggons for instance, and precipices, and dogs, and everything of that sort; committing nothing whatever to his senses. So that he used to be saved, as Antigonus the Carystian tells us, by his friends who accompanied him.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* IX.62 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Diogenes immediately adds a dissenting report from the later Pyrrhonist Aenesidemus: Pyrrho suspended judgment "without however, on any occasion acting in an imprudent manner." He lived to nearly ninety, which is not what you would expect of a man who walked into traffic. When a dog attacked him and he flinched, he admitted "That it was a difficult thing entirely to put off humanity" (IX.66); in a storm at sea he pointed to a pig calmly eating on deck as a model of the untroubled mind (IX.68).

### Sextus' method

Pyrrho's way of life was turned into a philosophical method five centuries later by Sextus Empiricus (late second century CE), a doctor whose *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* (*Pyrrhōneioi hypotypōseis*) is the fullest surviving account of ancient skepticism. Sextus begins by sorting philosophers into three groups: those who think they have found the truth (Dogmatists), those who say it cannot be found (the Academics), and those who are still looking. The Pyrrhonists are the third group. That is the first crucial point: **the Pyrrhonist does not say "nothing can be known."** That would be a dogma.

His definition of skepticism is technical and worth having in Patrick's translation:

> The _dynamis_ of the Sceptical School is to place the phenomenal in opposition to the intellectual "in any way whatever," and thus through the equilibrium of the reasons and things (_isostheneia tôn logôn_) opposed to each other, to reach, first the state of suspension of judgment, _epochê_ and afterwards that of imperturbability, _ataraxia_.
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* I.8 (tr. Mary Mills Patrick, *Pyrrhonic Sketches*)

Three Greek terms do the work. *Isostheneia*, equal strength: for any argument, an equally persuasive counter-argument can be found. *Epochē*, suspension: since neither side wins, you hold back assent. *Ataraxia*, tranquillity: the peace that follows. Sextus describes the discovery of this peace with a story about the painter Apelles, who was trying to paint the foam on a horse's mouth, failed, and in frustration threw his sponge at the picture:

> As soon, however, as it touched the picture it produced a good copy of the foam. The Sceptics likewise hoped to gain _ataraxia_ by forming judgments in regard to the anomaly between phenomena and the things of thought, but they were unable to do this, and so they suspended their judgment; and while their judgment was in suspension _ataraxia_ followed, as if by chance, as the shadow follows a body.
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* I.28–29 (tr. Mary Mills Patrick, *Pyrrhonic Sketches*)

Why should suspension bring peace? Sextus' answer is a piece of moral psychology that bears directly on nihilism:

> For he who is of the opinion that anything is either good or bad by nature is always troubled, and when he does not possess those things that seem to him good he thinks that he is tortured by the things which are by nature bad, and pursues those that he thinks to be good.
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* I.27 (tr. Mary Mills Patrick, *Pyrrhonic Sketches*)

This is the ancient source of a thought that runs through the whole history of nihilism: that *belief in objective value* is itself a source of suffering, and that letting it go might be liberating rather than devastating. The Stoics drew the opposite conclusion (only virtue is good, so nothing else can hurt you; see [the Stoicism course, Chapter 6](../learn-stoicism/06-virtue-and-the-good.md)). Sextus goes further: suspend judgment even on whether virtue is good.

### Pyrrhonism is not nihilism

It is easy to read Sextus as a moral nihilist. He is not, and seeing why sharpens the definition from [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md). First, he insists the skeptic *does not deny* that things are good or bad; he suspends judgment. Second, he explicitly distinguishes himself from the New Academy, who did assert that nothing can be apprehended:

> Those of the New Academy, although they say that all things are incomprehensible, differ from the Sceptics, perhaps even in saying that all things are incomprehensible (for they assert decidedly in regard to this, but the Sceptic thinks it possible that some things may be comprehended)…
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* I.226 (tr. Mary Mills Patrick, *Pyrrhonic Sketches*)

Third, he handles the self-refutation objection with an image that has become famous. Skeptical slogans like "Nothing more" (*ouden mallon*, "no more this than that") are not assertions; they apply to themselves and cancel themselves along with everything else, "just as cathartic medicines not only purge the body of humors, but carry off themselves with the humors" (I.206). The skeptic says what appears to him, as a report of his own state, "without asserting anything in regard to external objects" (I.15).

### Can the skeptic live?

The oldest objection to Pyrrhonism is the *apraxia* (inaction) charge: if you believe nothing, you cannot act. The Pyrrho anecdotes caricature exactly this objection. Sextus replies that the skeptic lives by appearances, following a fourfold guide: nature, the compulsion of feelings (hunger leads to food), the traditions of laws and customs ("according to them we consider piety a good in daily life, and impiety an evil"), and the teaching of the crafts, all "without expressing a decided opinion" (I.23–24).

Whether this works is much debated. Myles Burnyeat ("Can the Sceptic Live His Scepticism?", 1980) argued that the Pyrrhonist suspends judgment even on everyday matters and cannot coherently live that way; Michael Frede ("The Sceptic's Beliefs," 1979) argued that he rejects only *theoretical* beliefs about hidden natures. Jonathan Barnes ("The Beliefs of a Pyrrhonist," 1982) named the two readings "rustic" and "urbane."

The nihilism question cuts across this debate. On either reading, the Pyrrhonist lives by custom, follows the laws of his city, considers piety good "in daily life," and does so without believing that any of it is *by nature* good. That is a remarkable model of what life might look like after the loss of belief in objective value: not chaos, not despair, but a gentle conformity. Whether that is wisdom or a kind of passive nihilism, in Nietzsche's sense, is a question to bring to [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md).

## The Cyrenaics: pleasure, now, and the "death-persuader"

While the Pyrrhonists suspended judgment, another school took a more aggressive line. The Cyrenaics, named after the North African city of Cyrene, traced themselves to Aristippus, a companion of Socrates notorious for enjoying luxury. Their doctrines, as Diogenes Laertius reports them, combine three radical theses.

**Hedonism of the moment.** The only good is pleasure, specifically present, bodily, "smooth motion" pleasure; past and future pleasures are not good for you now. Absence of pain, which Epicurus would call the highest pleasure, they compared to the condition of a person asleep.

**Subjectivism about knowledge.** We can know our own affections (*pathē*) with certainty, but not their external causes:

> That the sensations are things which can be comprehended; but they limited this assertion to the sensations themselves, and did not extend it to the causes which produce them.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* II.92 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

(Sextus elsewhere reports their idea vividly: I know that I am *being whitened* (Greek *leukainesthai*), but not that the wall is white (*Against the Logicians* I.191–192).)

**Conventionalism about morality:**

> They also taught that there was nothing naturally and intrinsically just, or honourable, or disgraceful; but that things were considered so because of law and fashion.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* II.93 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

This is the closest any ancient school came to stating moral nihilism outright. Yet the same report adds that "the good man will do nothing out of the way," for fear of punishment and disgrace. The Cyrenaic sage knows that justice is a convention and keeps it anyway.

Then, around 300 BCE, a Cyrenaic named Hegesias drew the dark conclusion. If the good is pleasure, and pleasure is fleeting while pain is constant, the arithmetic of life is grim:

> But they teach that complete happiness cannot possibly exist; for that the body is full of many sensations, and that the mind sympathizes with the body, and is troubled when that is troubled, and also that fortune prevents many things which we cherished in anticipation; so that for all these reasons, perfect happiness eludes our grasp. Moreover, that both life and death are desirable.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* II.94 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Hegesias' followers held that "for the foolish man it is expedient to live, but to the wise man it is a matter of indifference" (II.95). According to Cicero (*Tusculan Disputations* I.83–84), Hegesias wrote a book called *The Man Who Starves Himself to Death* (*Apokarterōn*), and lectured so eloquently on the evils of life that King Ptolemy of Egypt forbade him to teach, because some of his listeners killed themselves. Later writers gave him the nickname *Peisithanatos*, "death-persuader." He is the first Western philosopher whose teaching was suppressed for being too persuasively bleak ([Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md) meets his German successors).

Yet notice even here: Hegesias' followers taught that "errors ought to meet with pardon" and that "one ought not to hate a person who has erred, but only to teach him better" (II.95). The bleakest school of antiquity was also a school of forgiveness. And the next Cyrenaic generation, the Annicereans, restored friendship, gratitude and patriotism to the list of things worth having. The ancient pattern holds: negation, then reconstruction.

## Lucretius: the consolations of a purposeless cosmos

The Epicureans gave the ancient world its most thoroughly purposeless universe. Following Democritus, Epicurus (341–270 BCE) taught that everything is atoms moving in the void; the world was not made, has no plan, and will dissolve. The gods exist but do not care about us. The soul is made of fine atoms and scatters at death. If cosmic nihilism means a universe without purpose, care or afterlife, Epicurus was a cosmic nihilist.

And he thought this was the best news anyone could hear. His *Letter to Menoeceus*, preserved by Diogenes Laertius, gives the core argument:

> Therefore, the most formidable of all evils, death, is nothing to us, since, when we exist, death is not present to us; and when death is present, then we have no existence.
>
> — Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus*, in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* X.125 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Two centuries later the Roman poet Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99–55 BCE) turned Epicurean physics into a six-book poem, *De rerum natura*. Its purpose, stated at the start, is to free the reader from religious terror. After telling how Agamemnon sacrificed his own daughter Iphigenia to get fair winds for Troy, Lucretius delivers his verdict in a single line:

> Such are the crimes to which Religion leads.
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* I.101 (tr. William Ellery Leonard)

Book III is the climax: a long proof that the soul is mortal, followed by the conclusion:

> Therefore death to us\
> Is nothing, nor concerns us in the least,\
> Since nature of mind is mortal evermore.
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* III.830–831 (tr. William Ellery Leonard)

Then Lucretius does something no philosopher had done so vividly. He gives Nature herself a voice, and she scolds the person who whines about dying:

> "Mortal, what hast thou of such grave concern\
> That thou indulgest in too sickly plaints?\
> Why this bemoaning and beweeping death?\
> For if thy life aforetime and behind\
> To thee was grateful, and not all thy good\
> Was heaped as in sieve to flow away\
> And perish unavailingly, why not,\
> Even like a banqueter, depart the halls,\
> Laden with life?
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* III.933–938 (tr. William Ellery Leonard)

And she adds the line that is the Epicurean answer to Qoheleth's "profit": you never owned your life in the first place.

> And in fee-simple life is given to none,\
> But unto all mere usufruct.
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* III.971 (tr. William Ellery Leonard)

A *usufruct* is a legal right to use and enjoy property that belongs to someone else. Qoheleth wanted a surplus he could keep; Lucretius says that wanting to keep is the mistake. Life is a loan, and the right response is to enjoy it and give it back gracefully.

### The symmetry argument

Lucretius adds a second argument, the most debated in the philosophy of death:

> Look back:\
> Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld\
> Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth.\
> And Nature holds this like a mirror up\
> Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone.\
> And what is there so horrible appears?
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* III.972–975 (tr. William Ellery Leonard)

In premise form:

1. Your nonexistence before birth was not bad for you.
2. Your nonexistence after death is relevantly like your nonexistence before birth.
3. Therefore your nonexistence after death is not bad for you.

The modern debate turns on premise 2. Thomas Nagel ("Death," 1970) argues that death is bad as a **deprivation** of goods you would otherwise have had, while prenatal nonexistence deprives you of nothing, since you could not have been born much earlier and still been you; Fred Feldman (*Confrontations with the Reaper*, 1992) develops the deprivation account. Derek Parfit (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984) suggests the asymmetry in our attitudes reflects a "bias toward the future" that may or may not be rational.

What matters for this course is the overall shape. Epicureanism accepts nearly every premise of cosmic nihilism: no purpose, no providence, no afterlife, a universe indifferent to us. It denies that any nihilist *conclusion* about value follows. Pleasure is really good; pain is really bad; friendship is among the greatest goods; and fear of death is the main obstacle to a happy life. The Epicurean is a cosmic nihilist and an ethical realist at once. That combination is one of the most important options on the map, and it will come back in modern dress as "optimistic nihilism" ([Chapter 14](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md)).

## Nagarjuna: emptiness and the charge of nihilism

### The setting

Move now to India, around the second century CE. Buddhism had by then a six-century history and a sophisticated scholastic philosophy, the *Abhidharma*, which analyzed persons and things into momentary elements called *dharmas*. The Buddha had taught *anātman*, "no-self": there is no permanent, unchanging soul behind the stream of experience. He had also taught *pratītyasamutpāda*, "dependent origination": everything arises in dependence on causes and conditions. The Abhidharma philosophers reconciled these by saying that while persons and tables are mere conventional constructions, the ultimate *dharmas* (moments of color, feeling, consciousness and so on) are real, each with its own intrinsic nature, *svabhāva* ("own-being").

Nāgārjuna, about whose life almost nothing reliable is known, attacked this compromise in his *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (*Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way*), founding the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school. His thesis is that *nothing* has *svabhāva*, not even the dharmas. Everything is *śūnya*, "empty": empty of intrinsic, independent existence. This is the doctrine of emptiness, *śūnyatā*. (There is no public-domain English translation of the *Kārikā* on Project Gutenberg; what follows paraphrases it, and Garfield's translation is the required reading.)

### The objection

If everything is empty, does anything exist? Nāgārjuna's opponents thought his view destroyed everything Buddhism stands for. In chapter 24 of the *Kārikā*, he states their objection himself, at full strength: if all is empty, nothing really arises or ceases; then the Four Noble Truths (suffering, its origin, its cessation, the path) cannot be real; then there is no practice, no attainment, no community of practitioners, no teaching, and no Buddha. Emptiness, the objector concludes, is a nihilism that saws off the branch the Buddhist sits on.

Nyāya critics pressed a logical version: is the statement "all things are empty" itself empty (and so powerless) or not (and so self-refuting)? Nāgārjuna's *Vigrahavyāvartanī* (*Dispeller of Disputes*) answers in a way that echoes Sextus' cathartic: his statement is empty too, yet works, like a magically created person who stops another; and (verse 29) he has no thesis of his own for the fault to attach to.

### Three replies

In chapter 24, Nāgārjuna answers the opponent in three moves.

**First, you have misunderstood emptiness.** "Empty" does not mean "nonexistent." It means "dependently arisen." In the verse that is often taken as the key to the whole work (24.18), he says that whatever arises dependently is what he calls emptiness, that emptiness is itself a dependent designation, and that this is the middle way. A chair is empty because it depends on its parts, its causes, and the conceptual scheme that picks it out as a chair. It is not nothing; it is just not a self-standing *something*.

**Second, there are two truths.** The Buddhas teach by means of two truths, the conventional (*saṃvṛti-satya*) and the ultimate (*paramārtha-satya*) (24.8–10). Conventionally, chairs, persons, suffering and the path are perfectly real, and ethical practice matters. Ultimately, none of them has intrinsic nature. And, crucially, the ultimate truth is not a hidden second world behind the conventional one; it is the truth *about* the conventional world, namely that it is empty. Without relying on convention, the ultimate cannot be taught at all.

**Third, the charge is reversed.** This is the brilliant move. Nāgārjuna argues (24.20 onward) that it is the *essentialist*, the person who believes in *svabhāva*, who destroys the Buddhist path. If things had fixed intrinsic natures, they could not change; suffering, if it had its own intrinsic nature, could never arise or cease; the path could never be traveled, since the unawakened could never become awakened. Only because things are empty (dependent, changeable) can anything happen at all. Verse 24.14, in substance: for whom emptiness is possible, everything is possible; for whom emptiness is not possible, nothing is possible. The real nihilist is the one who freezes the world into essences.

Elsewhere (15.10) he names the two extremes explicitly: to say "it exists" is to grasp at permanence (eternalism); to say "it does not exist" is to fall into annihilationism (*uccheda*). The wise rest in neither. And he warns (24.11) that emptiness wrongly grasped destroys the slow-witted, like a snake seized by the wrong end. Emptiness misunderstood as nothingness is precisely the danger he identifies.

### The scholarly debate

Is Nāgārjuna nonetheless a nihilist, despite himself? The question is live.

- **Nihilist readings.** Thomas E. Wood, in *Nāgārjunian Disputations: A Philosophical Journey through an Indian Looking-Glass* (1994), argues that the most coherent reading of Madhyamaka is that nothing exists, conventionally or ultimately. David Burton, in *Emptiness Appraised: A Critical Study of Nāgārjuna's Philosophy* (1999), argues that if everything is a conceptual construction, and nothing unconstructed exists for constructions to be built from, Madhyamaka collapses into nihilism whatever its intentions.
- **Anti-nihilist readings.** Jay Garfield (*The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way*, 1995; *Engaging Buddhism*, 2015) reads emptiness as a radically relational ontology: to exist is to exist dependently, and emptiness is the denial of a particular metaphysical fantasy, not of the world. Mark Siderits (*Buddhism as Philosophy*, 2nd ed. 2021) reads Madhyamaka as a kind of "semantic anti-realism": there is no ultimate truth in the sense of a final, mind-independent description of reality, which is not the same as saying nothing is true. Jan Westerhoff (*Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka*, 2009) gives a careful analysis of the different senses of *svabhāva* and argues that Nāgārjuna's target is precise and his position stable.

Note the historical irony: nineteenth-century Europe filed Buddhism under "nothingness," while Buddhist philosophers had spent fifteen centuries arguing precisely about how *not* to be nihilists. Whatever you conclude about Wood and Burton, the tradition defines itself *against* nihilism, and holds that compassion is grounded by emptiness: if beings have no fixed selves, the walls between your suffering and mine are not ultimate either. For the wider setting of Buddhist thought, see [the Philosophy course, Chapter 8](../learn-philosophy/08-classical-india-and-china.md); for Buddhist ethics, [the Ethics course, Chapter 7](../learn-ethics/07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md).

## What the ancients teach about nothing

Lay the six cases side by side:

| Thinker | Negation | What follows |
|---------|----------|--------------|
| Silenus (folk wisdom) | Existence is worse than nonexistence | Pessimism; for the Greeks, art and tragedy as ways to bear it |
| Qoheleth | No lasting profit; all is *hevel* | Enjoy your portion; act with your might now |
| Gorgias | Nothing exists, is knowable or communicable | A rhetorical or critical performance; persuasion as the real power |
| Pyrrhonists | Suspend judgment on all claims about natures, including value | *Ataraxia*; live by appearance and custom |
| Cyrenaics, Hegesias | Nothing just by nature; happiness impossible | Prudent conformity; for Hegesias, indifference to life and death |
| Epicurus, Lucretius | No purpose, providence or afterlife | Freedom from fear; pleasure and friendship |
| Nāgārjuna | Nothing has intrinsic existence | The middle way; liberation; compassion |

Three lessons stand out. First, **negation was almost always a means** to tranquillity, joy or liberation. Second, **global nihilism is performed, not asserted**: Gorgias' playful treatise and the Pyrrhonist's self-purging slogans are the forms it takes when it tries to go all the way. Third, **the charge of nihilism was already being made and answered**, and both Pyrrho's and Nāgārjuna's answers distinguish *what appears and functions* from *what exists by its own nature*.

What the ancients lacked, and what made modern nihilism a crisis rather than a school, was a world in which the *highest* values (a creator God, a moral order, the soul's immortality) had been believed by nearly everyone for centuries and then began to fail. That is the story of [Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md).

## Living it

Three ancient exercises, each tied to a text.

1. **Qoheleth's ledger.** Make two columns. In the first, list three things you are working on whose value depends on a lasting result (a career milestone, a reputation, a legacy). In the second, list three things whose value is in the doing (a meal with someone, a walk, a piece of work done well for its own sake). For one week, each evening, note which column the day's satisfactions came from. Qoheleth's claim is that only the second column survives scrutiny. Test it on your own evidence.
2. **Sextus' counter-argument drill.** Pick a belief you hold about value that you consider obvious (for instance, "a successful career is important"). Spend fifteen minutes building the strongest argument you can *against* it, then fifteen *for* it. Do not try to decide. Afterwards, notice how you feel about the issue. Sextus claims that *ataraxia* follows suspension "as the shadow follows a body." Is he right about you? Does suspension feel like peace, or like vertigo?
3. **Lucretius' usufruct.** Pick one thing you consider "yours" (a possession, a role, a relationship) and spend a day thinking of it as a usufruct: held on loan, to be used and enjoyed and eventually returned. Note what changes in how you treat it. Does it become less valuable, or more?

## Summary

- Ancient texts anticipate modern nihilism, but the word and the crisis are modern; beware anachronism. *Nāstika* in Indian debate meant rejecting Vedic authority, not "nihilist."
- The **wisdom of Silenus** (better never to be born) is pessimism, not strict nihilism; Nietzsche reads Greek art as a way of living with it.
- **Ecclesiastes** declares everything *hevel* ("vapor," on Fox's reading "absurd") because death cancels any lasting profit; but it repeatedly counsels joy in one's daily portion. Scholars divide over contradiction, "preacher of joy" (Whybray), and a pious editorial frame (12:13).
- **Gorgias'** *On Not-Being* argues that nothing exists, is knowable or communicable; it may be parody of the Eleatics, rhetorical play, or a serious theory of language (Kerferd).
- **Pyrrhonism** suspends judgment (*epochē*) through equal-strength arguments (*isostheneia*) to reach tranquillity (*ataraxia*). It is not nihilism: it denies nothing and distinguishes itself from the Academy's negative dogmatism. The rustic/urbane debate (Burnyeat, Frede, Barnes) concerns how the skeptic lives.
- The **Cyrenaics** held that nothing is just by nature; **Hegesias** argued happiness is impossible and was reportedly banned for causing suicides. Yet they counseled forgiveness and prudent conformity.
- **Epicurus and Lucretius** combine cosmic nihilism (no purpose, providence or afterlife) with ethical realism about pleasure and friendship. "Death is nothing to us"; life is a usufruct. The symmetry argument is contested by deprivation accounts (Nagel, Feldman).
- **Nāgārjuna's** emptiness (*śūnyatā*) denies intrinsic existence (*svabhāva*), not existence. He answers the charge of nihilism with dependent origination, the two truths, and the reversal that essentialism, not emptiness, makes change and liberation impossible. Wood and Burton read him as a nihilist; Garfield, Siderits and Westerhoff do not.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether suspending judgment about value brings peace

**Sextus Empiricus:** You say you believe that some things are good by nature: health, friendship, justice. Tell me, does this belief make you calmer or more anxious?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Sextus in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** More anxious, honestly. If health is really good, then losing it is really bad, and I worry about losing it.

**Sextus:** Just so. "He who is of the opinion that anything is either good or bad by nature is always troubled." Would you not be calmer if you held no opinion about it?

**You:** Maybe. But if I didn't think health was good, why would I go to the doctor?

**Sextus:** Because you are hungry, you eat; because you are in pain, you seek relief. The compulsion of feelings guides you. I do not ask you to stop acting, only to stop adding "and this is good by nature" to your actions.

**You:** But isn't that addition doing something? Suppose a friend is being treated unjustly. If I don't believe injustice is really bad, why would I risk anything to help?

**Sextus:** Because the customs and laws of your country teach that one should help friends, and you follow them in daily life, as I do.

**You:** And if the customs of my country taught that I should *not* help, say, because my friend belonged to a despised group?

**Sextus:** Then the customs would teach that. I would place against them the customs of other peoples, and find the arguments equal.

**You:** That troubles me. Your peace seems to depend on going along with whatever is customary. In a bad society, wouldn't the skeptic just be a quiet collaborator?

**Sextus:** You speak as though you knew that collaboration is bad by nature.

**You:** I'm not sure I *know* it. But I'm not willing to suspend judgment about it either. Some things seem worth being troubled for.

**Sextus:** Then you accept trouble as the price of your convictions. I do not judge you; it appears to me that you have chosen a different path.

**You:** Here's what I've learned, then. You're right that believing in objective value has a cost in anxiety. But giving it up has a cost too: it may leave me with nothing to stand on against my own society's errors. I'm not sure the trade is worth it.

**Sextus:** And I am not sure it is not. We have reached equal weights. Is that so bad a place to be?

### Dialogue 2 — On whether emptiness is nothingness

**Nāgārjuna:** You have heard that I teach that all things are empty. Many say this means nothing exists and nothing matters. What do you think "empty" means?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Nāgārjuna in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I think it means that things don't really exist, that they're like illusions. And if that's so, it's hard to see why suffering or kindness would matter.

**Nāgārjuna:** Consider this cup. Does it depend on anything?

**You:** On the clay, the potter, the kiln, the people who call it a cup instead of a bowl.

**Nāgārjuna:** If all those were taken away, would the cup remain?

**You:** No.

**Nāgārjuna:** Then the cup does not exist by its own power, independently of everything else. That is all "empty" means: empty of *svabhāva*, of self-standing existence. Is the cup therefore unable to hold tea?

**You:** No, it still holds tea.

**Nāgārjuna:** Then emptiness has not destroyed the cup. It has destroyed a mistaken idea of the cup.

**You:** But you also say the ultimate truth is that nothing has intrinsic existence. Isn't the conventional world, where cups hold tea, just a lower, less real level?

**Nāgārjuna:** Is there a second world, behind this one, where the "real" cup lives?

**You:** I suppose that's the idea of an ultimate reality.

**Nāgārjuna:** I deny it. The ultimate truth is only that this world, the one where cups hold tea and people suffer, is dependent all the way down. There is no other world for it to be less real than.

**You:** Then why do critics like Burton say you're a nihilist anyway?

**Nāgārjuna:** Because they think that if everything is constructed, there must be something unconstructed from which constructions are made, and if there is not, then there is nothing. Tell me: why must there be a foundation?

**You:** Because otherwise it's turtles all the way down, and nothing ultimately holds anything up.

**Nāgārjuna:** And yet the cup holds the tea. Perhaps the demand for a foundation is the illusion.

**You:** I'm not sure I can accept that. But I see that "nothing has a foundation" is a different claim from "nothing exists" or "nothing matters."

**Nāgārjuna:** Now consider suffering. If suffering had its own fixed nature, could it ever end?

**You:** No. It would be what it is permanently.

**Nāgārjuna:** Then it is because suffering is empty that liberation is possible. Who is the nihilist: the one who says suffering can end, or the one who makes it eternal?

**You:** On your terms, the essentialist. Though I suspect your critics would say you've redefined "nihilism" to win the argument.

**Nāgārjuna:** Perhaps. Then let us agree on this much: whoever calls me a nihilist must show that dependence is the same as nonexistence. That is the burden. It has not yet been carried.

</details>

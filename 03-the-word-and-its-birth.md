# Chapter 3 — The Word and Its Birth

Between the ancient worlds of [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md) and the Russian students of [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md) lies the moment when "nihilism" became a word. It happened in 1799, in an open letter from the philosopher Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi to Johann Gottlieb Fichte, and the word was born as an insult. To understand why Jacobi needed it, you have to see what had happened to European thought in the century and a half before him: Descartes had shown how to doubt everything and rebuild the world on the thinking self; Pascal had looked past the new science into an abyss of infinite space; Hume had followed reason to a point where it seemed to dissolve the self, causation and morality, and then gone off to play backgammon. This chapter tells that story, then follows the word into Jean Paul's nightmare of a dead Christ preaching that there is no God, and into Max Stirner's gleeful book that set its "affair on nothing." The thread running through it is a single worry: if the thinking self is the foundation of everything, what holds the self up? Previous: [Chapter 2 — Ancient Roots](02-ancient-roots.md). Next: [Chapter 4 — Russian Nihilism](04-russian-nihilism.md).

## From ancient doubt to modern doubt

The Pyrrhonism of [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md) slept for a thousand years. Sextus Empiricus was barely read in the Latin Middle Ages. Then in 1562 the printer Henri Estienne published a Latin translation of the *Outlines*, and skepticism became fashionable again. Michel de Montaigne had "*Que sais-je?*" ("What do I know?") struck on a medal and wrote his longest essay, the "Apology for Raymond Sebond," as an exercise in Pyrrhonian counter-argument. The Reformation made the question urgent: Catholics and Protestants each accused the other of having no criterion of truth, and each was right about the other. Richard Popkin called this the "skeptical crisis" of early modern Europe.

The difference from antiquity is what was at stake. Sextus could suspend judgment about the gods and still follow the customs of his city. Early modern Europeans had staked everything (salvation, political order, the meaning of life) on a single revealed truth. If that truth could not be secured, the loss was not a comfortable *ataraxia* but a hole at the center of the world. Three thinkers of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries stared into that hole and described it with a precision that would shape every later discussion of nihilism.

## Descartes: the demon and the floor

### The method of doubt

René Descartes (1596–1650) wanted to beat the skeptics at their own game. His plan, in the *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641), was to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted, and see whether anything survived. The senses sometimes deceive; perhaps they always do. You might be dreaming now. And then, in the most famous thought experiment in modern philosophy, he imagines a deceiver of unlimited power:

> To Which end I will suppose, not an _Infinitely perfect God_, the _Fountain_ of _truth_, but that some _Evil Spirit_ which is very _Powerful_ and _crafty_ has used all his endeavours to _deceive_ me; I will conceive, the Heavens, Air, Earth, Colours, Figures, Sounds, and all outward things are nothing else but the delusions of Dreams, by which he has laid snares to catch my easy beleif…
>
> — René Descartes, *Meditations* I (tr. William Molyneux, *Six Metaphysical Meditations*, 1680)

This is the "evil demon" (*genius malignus*). Pyrrhonian suspension left the appearances alone; Descartes' demon threatens the world itself: maybe there is nothing outside your mind at all. The Second Meditation opens with the feeling this produces:

> By yesterdays Meditation I am cast into so great _Doubts_, that I shall never forget them, and yet I know not how to answer them, but being plunged on a suddain into a deep Gulf, I am so amazed that I can neither touch the bottome, nor swim at the top.
>
> — René Descartes, *Meditations* II (tr. William Molyneux, *Six Metaphysical Meditations*, 1680)

He resolves to go on "till I find out some _certainty_, or if nothing else, yet at least this _certainty, That there is nothing sure_." That last phrase is a complete statement of epistemological nihilism as defined in [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md), offered as a possible destination.

### The floor

Descartes does not end there. Even if the demon deceives him about everything, the demon cannot deceive him about the fact that he is being deceived:

> But there is _I_ know not what sort of _Deceivour_ very _powerful_ and very _crafty_, who always strives to _deceive_ Me; without Doubt therefore _I am_, if he can _decieve me_; And let him _Deceive_ me as much as he can, yet he can never make me _not to Be_, whilst _I think that I am_.
>
> — René Descartes, *Meditations* II (tr. William Molyneux, *Six Metaphysical Meditations*, 1680)

In the earlier *Discourse on Method* (1637, Part IV) he had put it in the formula everyone knows: "I think, therefore I am (COGITO ERGO SUM)." From this floor Descartes rebuilds: he proves (to his satisfaction) that a perfect God exists, that God is no deceiver, and therefore that what he clearly and distinctly perceives is true. The world returns, guaranteed by God.

### Why Descartes matters for nihilism

Descartes was a devout Catholic and no nihilist. But after him, the *thinking subject* is the one thing given; the world, and even God, must be *proved* from inside the mind.

Now subtract the proof of God. Most of Descartes' readers found it weak: the "Cartesian circle" objection, raised by Antoine Arnauld in 1641, points out that Descartes needs God to guarantee clear and distinct perceptions, but uses clear and distinct perceptions to prove God. If the proof fails, you are left with the floor and nothing else: a self, certain of its own thinking, facing a world it can never verify. This is the situation German philosophers would call *subjectivism*, and Jacobi would call *nihilism*. Heidegger, a century later, would read the whole history of modern metaphysics as running from Descartes' *ego cogito* to Nietzsche's will to power (see [Chapter 9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md)).

## Pascal: the abyss between two infinities

### The new cosmos

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was a mathematical prodigy and pioneer of probability theory who, after a mystical experience in 1654, planned a great apology for Christianity. He died before finishing it; the notes, published as the *Pensées*, are some of the most intense pages in French.

Pascal saw what the new science meant for the human place in nature more clearly than its practitioners did. Copernicus had moved the earth from the center; the telescope and the microscope had opened two infinities, one of the vast and one of the small. Pascal asks you to imagine yourself suspended between them:

> For in fact what is man in nature? A Nothing in comparison with the Infinite, an All in comparison with the Nothing, a mean between nothing and everything.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* §72 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

The consequence is epistemological. Because we are "infinitely removed from comprehending the extremes," we cannot know the beginning or the end of anything, and our knowledge floats:

> We sail within a vast sphere, ever drifting in uncertainty, driven from end to end. When we think to attach ourselves to any point and to fasten to it, it wavers and leaves us; and if we follow it, it eludes our grasp, slips past us, and vanishes for ever. Nothing stays for us. This is our natural condition, and yet most contrary to our inclination; we burn with desire to find solid ground and an ultimate sure foundation whereon to build a tower reaching to the Infinite. But our whole groundwork cracks, and the earth opens to abysses.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* §72 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

Notice "solid ground" and "an ultimate sure foundation." This is exactly what Descartes claimed to have found, and Pascal (who knew Descartes and thought him "useless and uncertain") is saying it cannot be had.

### The silence of the spaces

Then comes the existential version, the one quoted in [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md):

> When I consider the short duration of my life, swallowed up in the eternity before and after, the little space which I fill, and even can see, engulfed in the infinite immensity of spaces of which I am ignorant, and which know me not, I am frightened, and am astonished at being here rather than there; for there is no reason why here rather than there, why now rather than then. Who has put me here? By whose order and direction have this place and time been allotted to me?
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* §205 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

And the next fragment, a single line: "The eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me." Two features make this a turning point. First, the problem is **contingency**: "there is no reason why here rather than there." Your existence is not explained by anything in it. Second, the universe is **indifferent**: the spaces "know me not." The Lucretian cosmos of [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md) was also vast and purposeless, but Lucretius found that liberating. Pascal finds it terrifying, because he assumes that a human life *needs* to be known and placed by something greater to mean anything.

### Diversion

How do people live with this? By not looking. Pascal's analysis of *divertissement* (diversion, distraction) is one of his sharpest:

> I have discovered that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* §139 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

We hunt, gamble, go to war and chatter, not because we want the hare or the prize, but because the activity keeps us from thinking about our condition. Read this beside your phone. Pascal anticipates both Kierkegaard's "present age" ([Chapter 8](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md)) and Heidegger's "das Man" ([Chapter 9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md)): the claim that ordinary busy life is, among other things, a flight from nothingness.

### Neither skeptic nor dogmatist

Pascal is a deliberate double agent between the ancient schools. Against the dogmatists, the skeptics are right: reason cannot prove its own first principles. Against the skeptics, nature is right: nobody can actually doubt whether he is awake. The result is a portrait of the human being as a contradiction:

> What a chimera then is man! What a novelty! What a monster, what a chaos, what a contradiction, what a prodigy! Judge of all things, imbecile worm of the earth; depositary of truth, a sink of uncertainty and error; the pride and refuse of the universe!
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* §434 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

"Nature confutes the sceptics, and reason confutes the dogmatists." Neither can be your home. Pascal's way out is the wager (§233): reason cannot decide, but "you must wager. It is not optional. You are embarked," and on the bet that God is, "If you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing." (The standard objections: which God? Can you believe at will?)

### Why Pascal matters for nihilism

Pascal is the great *anti*-nihilist who described nihilism better than most nihilists. His strategy is to make the godless condition as unbearable as possible so that you will run toward faith:

> When I see the blindness and the wretchedness of man, when I regard the whole silent universe, and man without light, left to himself, and, as it were, lost in this corner of the universe, without knowing who has put him there, what he has come to do, what will become of him at death, and incapable of all knowledge, I become terrified, like a man who should be carried in his sleep to a dreadful desert island, and should awake without knowing where he is, and without means of escape.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* §692 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

This creates a pattern you will meet again and again: **the religious diagnostician of nihilism**. Pascal, Jacobi, Dostoevsky and Kierkegaard all argue that without God the world is a desert island. The atheist can reply that this is a false dilemma: perhaps the island is just the world, and its being undesigned is no more a catastrophe than its being round. Nietzsche admired Pascal as the most honest of Christians and thought him a victim of Christianity, which had taught him to despise the natural. That will be the argument of [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md): it was the *Christian* standard, which demands that life be justified from outside, that made the silent universe seem unbearable in the first place.

## Hume: the philosopher who dined

### Reason against itself

David Hume (1711–1776) published *A Treatise of Human Nature* at twenty-eight. It made almost no stir, as he ruefully recalled in his short autobiography, but it contains three arguments that every later nihilist would borrow.

**Causation.** We never perceive a necessary connection between cause and effect, only constant conjunction; our expectation that the future will resemble the past is a habit, not a deduction (Book I, Part III).

**The self.** Look inward for the "self" that supposedly has your experiences, and you find only experiences:

> But setting aside some metaphysicians of this kind, I may venture to affirm of the rest of mankind, that they are nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* I.iv.6

(Compare the Buddhist *anātman* in [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md).)

**Value.** Reason alone cannot motivate or settle ends. "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions." And in a passage designed to shock:

> It is not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* II.iii.3

Book III adds the "is–ought" observation. Moralists, he notes, proceed for a while with ordinary factual claims, "when of a sudden I am surprized to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not." How can an *ought* be deduced from an *is*? Hume says a reason should be given "for what seems altogether inconceivable."

Put these together and you have the toolkit of modern moral nihilism ([Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md)): values are not facts in the world, reason cannot establish them, and the self that holds them is a bundle. Hume himself drew none of the nihilist conclusions. Moral distinctions, he held, arise from a widely shared and correctable *moral sense*. Whether that makes him a subjectivist, an expressivist or a sophisticated realist is a live debate (see the [Ethics course, Chapter 3](../learn-ethics/03-relativism-and-egoism.md) for the positions).

### The melancholy and the cure

What makes Hume essential for this course is that he tells you how it *felt* to reach these conclusions. The "Conclusion of this Book" at the end of Book I is the first great first-person account of philosophical nihilism as an experience. He compares himself to a sailor who has barely escaped shipwreck and now proposes to sail the ocean in "the same leaky weather-beaten vessel." He feels isolated, "some strange uncouth monster" expelled from human company. And then:

> The intense view of these manifold contradictions and imperfections in human reason has so wrought upon me, and heated my brain, that I am ready to reject all belief and reasoning, and can look upon no opinion even as more probable or likely than another. Where am I, or what? From what causes do I derive my existence, and to what condition shall I return? Whose favour shall I court, and whose anger must I dread? What beings surround me? and on whom have, I any influence, or who have any influence on me? I am confounded with all these questions, and begin to fancy myself in the most deplorable condition imaginable, invironed with the deepest darkness, and utterly deprived of the use of every member and faculty.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* I.iv.7

Compare this with Descartes' "deep Gulf" and Pascal's desert island. Then comes Hume's answer, and it is not an argument:

> Most fortunately it happens, that since reason is incapable of dispelling these clouds, nature herself suffices to that purpose, and cures me of this philosophical melancholy and delirium, either by relaxing this bent of mind, or by some avocation, and lively impression of my senses, which obliterate all these chimeras. I dine, I play a game of backgammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends; and when after three or four hours' amusement, I would return to these speculations, they appear so cold, and strained, and ridiculous, that I cannot find in my heart to enter into them any farther.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* I.iv.7

### Cure or diversion?

Is Hume's backgammon a **cure** or a **diversion** in Pascal's sense? Pascal would say Hume has stopped looking. Hume would reply that the melancholy was the distortion, produced by an overheated brain. And he does not abandon philosophy; he returns to it because he is curious and it is pleasant: "if we are philosophers, it ought only to be upon sceptical principles, and from an inclination, which we feel to the employing ourselves after that manner."

The position accepts nearly every premise of epistemological and moral nihilism and refuses the mood: the Pyrrhonist's answer from [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md), updated for a modern Scot. Some readers find it the sanest page in philosophy; the existentialists find it evasive, since you cannot play backgammon forever. The question of whether nihilism is a *truth* we must face or a *mood* we can outgrow will return in Nietzsche ([Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md)) and Camus ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)).

## Jacobi: the word takes hold

### The Pantheism Controversy

Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi (1743–1819) was a businessman, novelist and philosopher who spent his career arguing that consistent rationalism leads to disaster. His first explosion came in 1785. Jacobi reported that the revered dramatist Gotthold Ephraim Lessing had confessed to him, shortly before dying, that he was a Spinozist: "*Hen kai pan*" ("One and All"), no personal God, no freedom. Jacobi's thesis in *On the Doctrine of Spinoza* was that Spinoza was not an aberration but the *most consistent* philosopher of all: if you demand a sufficient reason for everything, you end with a single necessary system in which there is no free will and no personal God. Rationalism leads to Spinozism; Spinozism is fatalism and atheism. The only escape is a *salto mortale*, a "mortal leap" of faith.

The resulting quarrel, the *Pantheismusstreit* (Pantheism Controversy), drew in Mendelssohn, Herder, Goethe, Kant and others, and made Spinoza the secret center of German philosophy (Beiser's *The Fate of Reason* tells the story). Jacobi kept up the attack on the new Kantian philosophy too. In an appendix on transcendental idealism (1787), he made his famous complaint about Kant's "thing in itself": without that presupposition he could not enter Kant's system, and with it he could not stay inside.

### The Atheism Controversy and the letter

In 1798 Fichte, then the most celebrated philosopher in Germany, published an essay identifying God with the moral order of the world. He was accused of atheism; the Saxon government demanded action; and in 1799 he lost his professorship at Jena. During this *Atheismusstreit* (Atheism Controversy) Fichte hoped for support from Jacobi. Instead, Jacobi wrote him a long letter, dated March 1799, which he published that autumn as *Jacobi an Fichte* (*Jacobi to Fichte*). It is this letter that put the word *Nihilismus* into philosophical circulation (it had been used before, as you will see below, but it had not stuck).

No public-domain English translation exists, so what follows is a paraphrase (George di Giovanni's translation is listed in the required reading).

Jacobi begins with praise. Fichte, he says, is the true Messiah of speculative reason: he has done what Kant failed to do and made idealism fully consistent, deriving everything from the self-positing activity of the I. That is precisely the problem. A philosophy that derives everything from the I has nothing outside the I. The world becomes the I's product; God becomes the I's moral idea; even the I, having no other to stand against, becomes empty activity. Jacobi offers a homely image: such a philosophy is like knitting a stocking and then unraveling it. The pattern was only ever the thread folding back on itself; pull the thread and you find there was never anything but thread. Speculative reason, followed to the end, finds only itself, which is to say *nothing*.

Against this Jacobi sets a stark either/or: either God is a living being who exists outside me, or I am God; there is no third option. And then the sentence that matters for this course: if anyone wants to call his own position, the insistence on a reality beyond the I, "Chimerism" (*Chimärismus*), he will not mind, because he calls Fichte's idealism *Nihilismus*. He prefers his own "non-philosophy" (*Unphilosophie*), which knows that it does not know and rests on faith and feeling, to a philosophy that knows everything and finds it empty.

### What Jacobi meant

Four features of Jacobi's usage shaped everything that followed.

1. **It was an accusation, not a self-description.** No one in 1799 called himself a nihilist. "Nihilism" was the name for where *your opponent's* position secretly led. Jacobi's charge is the ancestor of every later "that way lies nihilism" argument, including Dostoevsky's ([Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md)) and the culture-war use of the word today.
2. **It was aimed at reason, not at vice.** Fichte was a strict moralist. Jacobi's charge was not that idealists behave badly but that their *philosophy* annihilates its objects. Nihilism is the endpoint of a method: the Cartesian project of grounding everything in the thinking subject, carried through without remainder.
3. **It linked metaphysics and religion.** The loss of a reality independent of the self and the loss of God were, for Jacobi, the same loss. This connection between epistemological and religious nihilism runs straight to Nietzsche's "death of God."
4. **It came with a remedy: a leap.** Jacobi's answer is not a counter-argument but a refusal to keep arguing, a leap into faith. Kierkegaard ([Chapter 8](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md)) would take over the idea of the leap, and possibly the phrase.

### Was the word new?

Not quite, and the prehistory shows how a word gets its meaning. Medieval theologians had debated "nihilianism," a Christological thesis attributed to Peter Lombard, that Christ as man is "not something" (*non est aliquid*); Pope Alexander III condemned it in 1177. Historians have found scattered German uses before Jacobi: by Jacob Hermann Obereit (1787) and by Daniel Jenisch (1796), who applied it to Kant's idealism. But these did not catch on. Jacobi's letter, written at the climax of the most public philosophical scandal of the decade, did. Within a few years the word was in use among the German Romantics (Friedrich Schlegel, Novalis, and Jean Paul, who was Jacobi's friend), usually meaning an idealism or aestheticism that dissolves the world into the play of the self.

### Gillespie's thesis

Michael Allen Gillespie's *Nihilism Before Nietzsche* (1995) uses Jacobi to make a bold historical argument. The usual story says nihilism results from the *death* of God: when God goes, values go. Gillespie argues the reverse. Nihilism grows out of a particular *idea* of God, the omnipotent, unconstrained divine will of late medieval nominalism (William of Ockham), which can do anything and is bound by no reason. Descartes' demon is a version of that God; Fichte's absolute I inherits its unlimited will; and Nietzsche's will to power is its last heir. On this reading nihilism is not the absence of the infinite but its transfer into the human will. You do not need to accept Gillespie's whole genealogy to see the force of the point: the "nothing" in nihilism is often the shadow of an "everything."

### Hegel's speculative Good Friday

One more German text belongs here. In *Faith and Knowledge* (1802), the young Hegel discussed Kant, Jacobi and Fichte together as philosophies of subjectivity, and ended with a startling image. The modern age, he wrote, rests on an infinite grief, the feeling that "God himself is dead" (*Gott selbst ist tot*, in our rendering), a line he took from a seventeenth-century Lutheran Good Friday hymn. He also cited Pascal's remark (§441) that nature testifies everywhere "to a lost God and a corrupt nature." Philosophy, Hegel said, must pass through this "speculative Good Friday" toward a resurrection in the concept. Nietzsche's madman will announce the same death eighty years later with no resurrection in view ([Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)).

## Jean Paul: the dead Christ preaches

### The dream

Jean Paul Friedrich Richter (1763–1825), who wrote as "Jean Paul," was the most popular German novelist of his day, a humorist with a weakness for digression and an enormous heart. In his novel *Siebenkäs* (1796–97) he inserted a set piece, *Rede des toten Christus vom Weltgebäude herab, daß kein Gott sei* (Speech of the dead Christ from the top of the universe, that there is no God), which Ewing titles "The Dead Christ Proclaims that There Is No God." It was meant, he said in a preface, to frighten the atheists among his readers, and to shock the philosophy professors who discussed God's existence "as cold-bloodedly and chill-heartedly as though it were a question of the existence of the kraken or the unicorn."

The narrator dreams he wakes in a churchyard at night. The graves are open, the dead gather in the church, and on the dome is a dial of eternity with no figures on it. Then Christ descends:

> And at this point a lofty, noble form, bearing the impress of eternal sorrow, came sinking down towards our group, and rested on the altar; whereupon all the dead cried out, "Christ! Is there no God?"
>
> He answered, "There is none."
>
> — Jean Paul, *Flower, Fruit, and Thorn Pieces* (*Siebenkäs*), First Flower-Piece (tr. Alexander Ewing)

Christ explains that he has searched the universe:

> I have traversed the worlds, I have risen to the suns, with the milky ways I have passed athwart the great waste spaces of the sky; there is no God. And I descended to where the very shadow cast by Being dies out and ends, and I gazed out into the gulf beyond, and cried, 'Father, where art Thou?' But answer came there none, save the eternal storm which rages on, controlled by none; and towards the west, above the chasm, a gleaming rainbow hung, but there was no sun to give it birth, and so it sank and fell by drops into the gulf. And when I looked up to the boundless universe for the Divine eye, behold, it glared at me from out a socket, empty and bottomless.
>
> — Jean Paul, *Flower, Fruit, and Thorn Pieces* (*Siebenkäs*), First Flower-Piece (tr. Alexander Ewing)

Then the most painful moment. The dead children come into the church and ask, "Jesus, have we no Father?" He answers, weeping:

> "We are orphans all, both I and ye. We have no Father."
>
> — Jean Paul, *Flower, Fruit, and Thorn Pieces* (*Siebenkäs*), First Flower-Piece (tr. Alexander Ewing)

### Reading the dream

Several things stand out.

- **The image is cosmic emptiness**: an "empty and bottomless" socket where the eye of God should be. This is Pascal's silent universe made visible, and it anticipates the madman's "Do we not stray, as through infinite nothingness?" in Nietzsche.
- **The loss is relational.** What the dead lose is not an explanation of the world but a Father. Atheism here means orphanhood, an isolation of every soul: "How every soul in this great corpse-trench of an universe is utterly alone?" The preface makes the same point in a remarkable image: atheism shatters the spiritual universe "into innumerable glittering quicksilver globules of individual personalities, running hither and thither at random, coalescing, and parting asunder without unity, coherence, or consistency." Hold that image in mind for Stirner.
- **It is a dream, and the dreamer wakes.** "And my soul wept for joy that it could still worship God." Jean Paul's purpose is apologetic, like Pascal's: show the abyss so you will step back from it.

The piece traveled: Madame de Staël translated it in *De l'Allemagne* (1813), and Gérard de Nerval reworked it in "Le Christ aux Oliviers" (1844), without the waking. A text written to defend faith became a set piece of Romantic despair, a small model of the whole history: the diagnosticians' portraits of the abyss became its best advertisements.

Jean Paul also attacked Fichte directly. His *Clavis Fichtiana* (1800), written as a companion piece to Jacobi's letter, is a satire narrated by Leibgeber, a character from *Siebenkäs*, who takes Fichte's idealism so seriously that he becomes a solipsist who has created the whole world, including Fichte. It is the funniest document of early nihilism, and it makes Jacobi's point by reductio: a philosophy that makes the I everything leaves the I alone with itself.

## Stirner: the creative nothing

### The book that shocked the Young Hegelians

Johann Caspar Schmidt (1806–1856), who wrote as Max Stirner, was a Berlin schoolteacher in a girls' school who drank with the "Free," a circle of radical Young Hegelians that at times included Bruno Bauer, Friedrich Engels and (briefly) Karl Marx. In late 1844 he published *Der Einzige und sein Eigentum* (the title page is dated 1845), literally "The Unique One and His Property," translated by Steven T. Byington in 1907 as *The Ego and His Own*. It was attacked by nearly everyone he knew, then largely forgotten until anarchists rediscovered it in the 1890s.

The context is Ludwig Feuerbach's *The Essence of Christianity* (1841). Feuerbach had argued that God is a projection: humans attribute their own best qualities (love, reason, will) to an imaginary being and then bow down to it. The cure is to take these qualities back and worship *Man* instead. Stirner's response: Feuerbach has not abolished religion. He has only replaced one God with another. "Man," "Humanity," "Reason," "Freedom," "the People," "the Good Cause": these are all still ideals set above the actual living individual, to which he is told to sacrifice himself. Stirner calls them *spooks*.

### "All Things are Nothing to Me"

The opening section is titled "All Things are Nothing to Me." Byington's footnote explains that the German, *Ich hab' mein' Sach' auf Nichts gestellt*, is the first line of a poem by Goethe called "*Vanitas! Vanitatum Vanitas!*," and translates it literally: "I have set my affair on nothing." The book thus opens with a nod to Ecclesiastes. It continues:

> What is not supposed, to be my concern! First and foremost, the Good Cause, then God's cause, the cause of mankind, of truth, of freedom, of humanity, of justice; further, the cause of my people, my prince, my fatherland; finally, even the cause of Mind, and a thousand other causes. Only _my_ cause is never to be my concern. "Shame on the egoist who thinks only of himself!"
>
> — Max Stirner, *The Ego and His Own*, "All Things are Nothing to Me" (tr. Steven T. Byington)

Stirner points out that God, according to theologians, serves no cause but his own; so does Humanity, so does the Sultan. "And will you not learn by these brilliant examples that the egoist gets on best?" Then the key passage:

> If God, if mankind, as you affirm, have substance enough in themselves to be all in all to themselves, then I feel that _I_ shall still less lack that, and that I shall have no complaint to make of my "emptiness." I am nothing in the sense of emptiness, but I am the creative nothing, the nothing out of which I myself as creator create everything.
>
> — Max Stirner, *The Ego and His Own*, "All Things are Nothing to Me" (tr. Steven T. Byington)

And then: "The divine is God's concern; the human, man's. My concern is neither the divine nor the human, not the true, good, just, free, etc., but solely what is _mine_."

### Spooks and wheels in the head

Stirner's central diagnostic image is possession. People who serve ideals are like the possessed of old, haunted by ghosts:

> Man, your head is haunted; you have wheels in your head! You imagine great things, and depict to yourself a whole world of gods that has an existence for you, a spirit-realm to which you suppose yourself to be called, an ideal that beckons to you. You have a fixed idea!
>
> — Max Stirner, *The Ego and His Own*, Part First, "Wheels in the Head" (tr. Steven T. Byington)

"Wheels in the head" (*Sparren*, a German idiom for having a screw loose) are fixed ideas that subject the person to themselves. Morality is one; so are Right, the State, Truth and, above all, "Man": "The most oppressive spook is _Man_." Stirner applies the same treatment to truth: "Truths are phrases, ways of speaking, words," tools that the individual uses, not masters he serves.

### Is Stirner a nihilist?

Stirner never uses the word "nihilism," but nearly every feature of the later concept appears in him. He rejects objective moral obligations (moral nihilism). He treats truths as instruments (a form of alethiological nihilism). He rejects the State and every collective claim (political nihilism, in an anarchist key). And he founds the whole thing on "nothing." But look at what kind of nothing. Jacobi accused idealism of dissolving everything into the empty I. Stirner *accepts* the charge and turns it into a boast. The I is indeed nothing, not a fixed essence or nature, but a "creative nothing" that makes and consumes its own world. The book ends:

> I am _owner_ of my might, and I am so when I know myself as _unique_. In the _unique one_ the owner himself returns into his creative nothing, out of which he is born. Every higher essence above me, be it God, be it man, weakens the feeling of my uniqueness, and pales only before the sun of this consciousness. If I concern myself for myself, the unique one, then my concern rests on its transitory, mortal creator, who consumes himself, and I may say:
>
> All things are nothing to me.
>
> — Max Stirner, *The Ego and His Own*, "The Unique One" (tr. Steven T. Byington)

So Stirner is the first thinker in this course to *occupy* the position the word "nihilism" was invented to condemn, and to find it not desolate but exhilarating. That makes him a first sketch of what Nietzsche would call *active* nihilism ([Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)).

### Egoism without cruelty?

The obvious worry is that Stirner licenses anything. Some passages invite that reading: "For me you are nothing but--my food, even as I too am fed upon and turned to use by you." But Stirner's egoism is subtler than a slogan. He does not deny love; he denies that love is a *duty*:

> I love men too,--not merely individuals, but every one. But I love them with the consciousness of egoism; I love them because love makes _me_ happy, I love because loving is natural to me, because it pleases me. I know no "commandment of love." I have a _fellow-feeling_ with every feeling being, and their torment torments, their refreshment refreshes me too…
>
> — Max Stirner, *The Ego and His Own*, Part Second, "My Intercourse" (tr. Steven T. Byington)

In place of State and Society he proposes the "union of egoists" (*Verein*), a voluntary association its members own rather than serve. Whether "fellow-feeling" without obligation can do the work of morality is the central question. Philosophers distinguish Stirner's position from *psychological* egoism (everyone does in fact act only for themselves) and from *ethical* egoism (everyone *ought* to act for themselves; see the [Ethics course, Chapter 3](../learn-ethics/03-relativism-and-egoism.md)). Stirner rejects *every* "ought," including the egoist's; his egoism is better described as a refusal to be owned by any ideal at all.

### Reception

Marx and Engels devoted the longest part of *The German Ideology* (1845–46) to mocking "Saint Max" as the isolated bourgeois individual in philosophical dress. Benjamin Tucker published Byington's translation in 1907, and Stirner became a patron saint of individualist anarchism. Whether Nietzsche read him is disputed (Eduard von Hartmann insinuated plagiarism); Nietzsche never mentions him, and most scholars treat the parallels as convergence. David Leopold's Cambridge edition (1995) treats Stirner as a serious critic of ideology rather than a crank.

## The pattern so far

Line up the figures of this chapter and a pattern appears.

| Thinker | What dissolves | What is offered instead |
|---|---|---|
| Descartes | the external world, under the demon | the thinking self, then God as guarantor |
| Pascal | foundations, place, meaning | faith (the wager; the heart's reasons) |
| Hume | causation, the self, rational value | nature, custom, sentiment, backgammon |
| Jacobi (on Fichte) | everything not produced by the I | the leap of faith; "non-philosophy" |
| Jean Paul | God, and with him all relation | waking from the dream |
| Stirner | God, Man, morality, truth as master | the unique one, the creative nothing |

Three lessons follow. First, **modern nihilism grows out of modern subjectivity**: once the self is the foundation, the question of what grounds the self becomes unavoidable. Second, **the word was born as an accusation** aimed at a philosophy, and most of the thinkers who described the abyss best (Pascal, Jacobi, Jean Paul) did so to send you away from it. Third, **the first person to embrace the position**, Stirner, did not experience it as despair but as liberation. The difference between nihilism as horror and nihilism as freedom is already present here, before Russia gave the word its political meaning in [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md).

## Living it

1. **The demon test.** Pick three beliefs that matter to how you live: one factual, one moral, one about yourself. For each, ask Descartes' question: could a powerful deceiver make me believe this falsely? Then ask Hume's: even if so, can I actually stop believing it? Write down which beliefs survive which test. You will probably find a gap between what you can *doubt* and what you can *give up*. That gap is where Hume lived.
2. **Pascal's chamber.** Sit alone in a quiet room with no phone, book or music for thirty minutes. Note when and how you reach for diversion. Afterwards, write a paragraph on what you were avoiding, if anything. Pascal predicts you will find something you would rather not think about. Does he win?
3. **Stirner's spook audit.** List five causes or ideals you feel obligated to serve (family, career, a political cause, "being a good person," the truth). For each, ask: do I serve this because it serves me (including through love and fellow-feeling), or because I feel I *must*, whatever it costs me? Stirner says only the second kind is a spook. Decide, for each, whether you would keep it if you thought of it as yours rather than as your master.

## Summary

- **Descartes**' evil demon threatened the existence of the world; his *cogito* made the thinking self the foundation of certainty, with God as guarantor. If the guarantor fails, the self is left alone, which is the situation later called subjectivism and nihilism.
- **Pascal** described the human condition between two infinities, without foundation, frightened by the silence of infinite spaces and fleeing into diversion. He is the archetypal religious diagnostician of nihilism.
- **Hume** gave later nihilists their arguments (causation as habit, the self as a bundle, reason as slave of the passions, no *ought* from an *is*) and an account of philosophical despair, but refused the mood: nature, company and backgammon cure the melancholy.
- **Jacobi** made *Nihilismus* a philosophical term in his 1799 letter to Fichte, as an *accusation*: idealism that derives everything from the I annihilates everything, including God. His alternative was a leap of faith. Earlier uses (medieval "nihilianism," Obereit, Jenisch) did not catch on.
- **Jean Paul**'s *Speech of the Dead Christ* dramatized atheism as cosmic orphanhood; written to defend faith, it became a Romantic emblem of despair.
- **Stirner** accepted what Jacobi condemned: the self as a "creative nothing," with God, Man, morality and truth exposed as "spooks." He is the first thinker to inhabit the nihilist position joyfully.

**You:** Yes. I don't think values come from outside me. I decide what matters.

**Jacobi:** And when you decide, do you decide for a reason, or for no reason?

**You:** For a reason, usually. I value honesty because it makes trust possible.

**Jacobi:** And do you value trust because you decided to, or because trust is worth something whether or not you decide?

**You:** I suppose because I decided to. Other people might not.

**Jacobi:** Then honesty is valuable because of trust, and trust because you chose it, and your choice because… what?

**You:** Because it's mine. It doesn't need anything further.

**Jacobi:** Then every value you hold hangs on a choice that hangs on nothing. You knit a beautiful stocking, and when I pull the thread there is nothing but thread. That is what I called nihilism.

**You:** But the stocking still keeps my feet warm. Why does it matter that it's "only" thread?

**Jacobi:** Because you could unravel it tomorrow and knit something else, and nothing would be lost, since nothing was there. Does that not frighten you?

**You:** It would if I actually could. But I can't just decide tomorrow not to care about my friends. Some of my values feel chosen; others feel more like they chose me.

**Jacobi:** Then you admit something outside your will has a claim on you.

**You:** Something outside my *deliberate* will, yes. But it might be my nature, or my upbringing, not God.

**Jacobi:** Then you have traded God for nature, and must ask whether nature is any more than another thread.

**You:** Here's where I've got to. You're right that "I choose my values" is too simple: if everything rests on bare choice, it rests on nothing. But your either/or, God or the empty I, leaves out the middle: values I didn't choose and can't drop, that come from being a social, embodied creature. Whether that middle is solid ground or just a longer thread, I can't yet say.

**Jacobi:** Then you are where I was: unable to prove the ground, unwilling to live without it. I leap. What will you do?

### Dialogue 2 — On whether your ideals are spooks

**Stirner:** You say you are working long hours "for the cause." Whose cause is it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Stirner in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It's a climate organization. The cause is the planet, future generations.

**Stirner:** And does the planet thank you? Do future generations pay your rent?

**You:** No, but that's not the point. It matters whether or not they thank me.

**Stirner:** "It matters." To whom? You speak as though the cause had a voice and gave you orders. That is what I call a spook: an idea that has made you its servant.

**You:** So you'd say I should quit and do whatever benefits me?

**Stirner:** I say nothing about what you *should* do. I ask why you do it. If you work for the cause because you love the forests and it pleases you to fight for them, the cause is yours, and I have no quarrel with you. If you work because you feel you *must*, even when it crushes you, then the cause owns you.

**You:** Honestly, it's both. Some days I love it. Some days I hate it and keep going out of guilt.

**Stirner:** Then on the second kind of day you are possessed.

**You:** But on those days the guilt is what keeps me going. Without the spook I'd have quit, and the forests would be worse off.

**Stirner:** Perhaps. Or perhaps you would rest, and return because you wanted to, and work better as an owner than as a servant.

**You:** That's a nice theory. But some things need doing whether or not anyone enjoys doing them. If everyone waited until a cause "pleased" them, a lot of necessary work would never happen.

**Stirner:** You say "need." Need for whom? You keep reaching for a voice above you.

**You:** Here's what I'll take from this. You've shown me that some of my commitment is guilt, and that guilt may not be the best fuel. But I don't accept that every claim beyond my own pleasure is a ghost. My fellow-feeling for people who'll suffer is real, and you yourself admit that their torment torments you. Maybe the question isn't whether ideals are spooks, but whether I can hold them as mine.

**Stirner:** Then hold them. Only do not let them hold you.

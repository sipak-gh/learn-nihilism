# Chapter 4 — Russian Nihilism

In [Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md) "nihilism" was a technical insult that German philosophers threw at one another. In Russia in the 1860s it became an identity: a haircut, a pair of blue spectacles, a way of speaking to your parents, and finally a political movement whose members killed a Tsar. The word reached Russia's reading public through a novel, Ivan Turgenev's *Fathers and Sons* (1862), whose hero Bazarov calls himself a nihilist and means something quite specific by it: he accepts nothing on authority, and he believes in frogs, chemistry and usefulness. Young radicals took the label up, critics argued over it, and Fyodor Dostoevsky spent the rest of his career answering it in three of the greatest novels ever written. This chapter follows that story. Its central puzzle is that the Russian nihilists were among the most morally earnest people of their century. They denied everything in the name of something. Did that make them nihilists at all, or does their story show how a negation sustained by moral passion can slide, by its own logic, into the void that Bazarov's enemies predicted? Previous: [Chapter 3 — The Word and Its Birth](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md). Next: [Chapter 5 — Schopenhauer and Pessimism](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md).

## Russia in 1860: the setting

The thirty-year reign of Nicholas I, an era of censorship and secret police, ended in defeat in the Crimean War. His successor, Alexander II, concluded that Russia could not compete with industrial Europe while most of its people were the property of landowners. In 1861 he emancipated some twenty-two million serfs and reformed the courts, the army and the universities. For a few years the lid came off.

The universities filled with a new kind of student. The *raznochintsy* ("people of various ranks") were the children of priests, minor officials and impoverished gentry, with no estates to inherit and no place in the old order. They had read Feuerbach, Büchner's *Force and Matter*, Mill and Darwin. Their elders, the "men of the forties," were liberal gentry who quoted Schiller and Hegel and talked beautifully about the people while living on serf labor. The "men of the sixties" found this talk unbearable. Turgenev, born in 1818, was a man of the forties who watched the new generation with admiration and dread, and put it into a novel.

The word itself was not new to Russian. The critic Nikolai Nadezhdin had used "nihilists" in 1829 as a sneer at Pushkin's circle, and the conservative press used it now and then for materialists. But Turgenev's novel gave it a face, and within months everybody was using it.

## Bazarov: a nihilist in a novel

### The definition at breakfast

*Fathers and Sons* is set in 1859. Arkady Kirsanov comes home from university to his father's run-down estate, bringing his friend Yevgeny Bazarov, a medical student. Bazarov dissects frogs, is rude to everyone, and fascinates them all. At breakfast on the first morning, Arkady's father Nikolai and his uncle Pavel, an aging dandy, ask what Bazarov is. Arkady tells them he is a nihilist. You saw part of this scene in [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md); here is the whole exchange, the most famous definition of the word in literature, delivered as a family argument:

> 'A nihilist,' said Nikolai Petrovitch. 'That's from the Latin, _nihil_, _nothing_, as far as I can judge; the word must mean a man who ... who accepts nothing?'
>
> 'Say, "who respects nothing,"' put in Pavel Petrovitch, and he set to work on the butter again.
>
> 'Who regards everything from the critical point of view,' observed Arkady.
>
> 'Isn't that just the same thing?' inquired Pavel Petrovitch.
>
> 'No, it's not the same thing. A nihilist is a man who does not bow down before any authority, who does not take any principle on faith, whatever reverence that principle may be enshrined in.'
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. V (tr. Constance Garnett)

Chapter 1 pulled three definitions out of this exchange. Read in full, it has four, and they are not equivalent:

1. **Accepts nothing** (Nikolai): an epistemic position, a refusal to believe.
2. **Respects nothing** (Pavel): an accusation of moral emptiness. The word is still an accusation, as it was for Jacobi in [Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md), but the charge has moved from a philosophy's logic to a young man's manners.
3. **Regards everything critically** (Arkady): a method, which every scientist and philosopher since Descartes would claim.
4. **Bows to no authority, takes no principle on faith** (Arkady again): a refusal of *authority*, not of truth or value. This is closest to what the real radicals meant.

Pavel replies that without principles taken on faith "there's no taking a step, no breathing," and jibes: "We shall see how you will exist in void, in vacuum," before ringing for his cocoa. The joke is on Pavel, but his question is serious. Can a person live in the vacuum that Bazarov's criticism creates?

### What Bazarov affirms

The answer the novel first gives is that Bazarov does not live in a vacuum at all. He has a creed, and it is a strong one: natural science, utility and work. "A good chemist is twenty times as useful as any poet," he tells Pavel (ch. VI). When Arkady speaks of nature's beauty, Bazarov answers:

> 'Nature, too, is foolery in the sense you understand it. Nature's not a temple, but a workshop, and man's the workman in it.'
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. IX (tr. Constance Garnett)

This is not the absence of value. It is a ranking of values: the useful above the beautiful, the tested above the inherited, the material above the spiritual. The confrontation with Pavel in chapter X makes the logic explicit:

> 'We act by virtue of what we recognise as beneficial,' observed Bazarov. 'At the present time, negation is the most beneficial of all--and we deny----'
>
> 'Everything?'
>
> 'Everything!'
>
> 'What? not only art and poetry ... but even ... horrible to say ...'
>
> 'Everything,' repeated Bazarov, with indescribable composure.
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. X (tr. Constance Garnett)

When Nikolai objects that one must also build, Bazarov answers: "That's not our business now.... The ground wants clearing first." Read carefully, the passage contains its own refutation of the charge that Bazarov believes in nothing. He denies everything *because* denial is "beneficial." Benefit is his standard, and the standard is not denied. The nihilism of the 1860s was, in its own self-understanding, an act of demolition carried out for the sake of a future building. It was negation as a *means*.

### The haystack

The novel is greater than its hero's program, because Turgenev lets the program crack. In chapter XXI, lying under a haystack with Arkady at his parents' tiny estate, Bazarov says something that has nothing to do with utility:

> 'I think; here I lie under a haystack.... The tiny space I occupy is so infinitely small in comparison with the rest of space, in which I am not, and which has nothing to do with me; and the period of time in which it is my lot to live is so petty beside the eternity in which I have not been, and shall not be.... And in this atom, this mathematical point, the blood is circulating, the brain is working and wanting something.... Isn't it loathsome? Isn't it petty?'
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. XXI (tr. Constance Garnett)

If you read [Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md), you have heard this before. It is Pascal's two infinities almost word for word, but without Pascal's God at the end. Bazarov the materialist has stumbled from *political* nihilism (no authority deserves obedience) into *cosmic* nihilism (nothing I do matters against the scale of the universe). The first was a weapon. The second turns the weapon on the person holding it. His parents, he adds, "don't trouble themselves about their own nothingness," while he feels "nothing but weariness and anger."

### The death

Bazarov's end is almost absurdly accidental. Helping his father, a country doctor, perform an autopsy on a peasant who died of typhus, he cuts his finger and gets blood poisoning. He diagnoses himself correctly and dies within days. On his deathbed he sends for Madame Odintsov, the cool, intelligent widow he had loved and been refused, and says to her:

> You see what a hideous spectacle; the worm half-crushed, but writhing still. And, you see, I thought too: I'd break down so many things, I wouldn't die, why should I! there were problems to solve, and I was a giant! And now all the problem for the giant is how to die decently, though that makes no difference to any one either.... Never mind; I'm not going to turn tail.
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. XXVII (tr. Constance Garnett)

A moment later, half-delirious: "I was needed by Russia.... No, it's clear, I wasn't needed." His last words to her are a request so tender that it undoes his whole manner: "Breathe on the dying lamp, and let it go out ..."

The final chapter visits Bazarov's grave, where his old parents come to weep and pray. Turgenev, an agnostic, ends with a sentence that both affirms and refuses comfort:

> Can it be that their prayers, their tears are fruitless? Can it be that love, sacred, devoted love, is not all-powerful? Oh, no! However passionate, sinning, and rebellious the heart hidden in the tomb, the flowers growing over it peep serenely at us with their innocent eyes; they tell us not of eternal peace alone, of that great peace of 'indifferent' nature; tell us too of eternal reconciliation and of life without end.
>
> — Ivan Turgenev, *Fathers and Children*, ch. XXVIII (tr. Constance Garnett)

The quotation marks around "indifferent" matter. They cite Pushkin's line about "indifferent nature" shining over the grave. Turgenev holds two readings open: the flowers speak of reconciliation, or they speak only of nature's indifference and we hear reconciliation because we need to. The novel does not decide.

### The reception: everyone was offended

Conservatives thought Turgenev had glamorized a monster. Many radicals thought he had libeled them. Peter Kropotkin, then a young officer and later the leading anarchist theorist, recalled that "even his Nihilist, Bazároff, did not satisfy us": the young found him too harsh with his parents and too negligent of "his duties as a citizen." Turgenev defended himself in a letter that Kropotkin later translated:

> Nevertheless, when he calls himself nihilist you must read _revolutionist_.
>
> — Peter Kropotkin, *Russian Literature*, ch. IV, quoting Turgenev's letter

In its Russian career, "nihilist" was a *political* term long before it was a *metaphysical* one. In 1862 it meant: I refuse the authority of Church, Tsar, family and custom. It said nothing about whether life has meaning. The novel's question is whether Bazarov's meaning, usefulness, can survive the critical method he applies to everything else.

## The critics who took the name

### Pisarev: smash what can be smashed

One radical critic welcomed Bazarov with open arms. Dmitry Pisarev (1840–1868) was the son of minor gentry who wrote for the journal *Russkoe slovo* (*The Russian Word*). His 1862 essay "Bazarov" argued that Turgenev, despite himself, had drawn an accurate and admirable portrait of the new man. Kropotkin summarizes Pisarev's ideal:

> his ideal was "the thoughtful realist"--the type which Turguéneff had just represented in Bazároff, and which Písareff further developed in his critical essays. He shared Bazároff's low opinion of art
>
> — Peter Kropotkin, *Russian Literature*, ch. VIII

Pisarev's most quoted slogan, from an 1861 article, is the purest expression of 1860s negation. Paraphrased, it runs: what can be smashed should be smashed; whatever survives the blow is worth keeping, and whatever flies to pieces was rubbish; so strike right and left, for no harm can come of it. The argument is a kind of stress test. It treats criticism as a sieve. Pisarev does not doubt that some things will survive: he expects natural science, honest work and the welfare of the masses to come through. His nihilism is a method of *selection*, not a conclusion that nothing has value. Pisarev wrote his most influential essays during four years in the Peter and Paul Fortress, and drowned while swimming on the Baltic coast in 1868, aged twenty-seven.

### Chernyshevsky: rational egoism and the new people

Nikolai Chernyshevsky (1828–1889) was the most influential radical of the decade and, oddly, the author of its most influential bad novel. Arrested in 1862, he wrote *What Is to Be Done?* in the Peter and Paul Fortress. The censors, assuming a novel by a prisoner could do no harm, let it be published in 1863. Kropotkin, an admirer, did not pretend it was good art:

> From the artistic point of view this novel leaves much to be desired; but for the Russian youth of the times it was a revelation, and it became a programme.
>
> — Peter Kropotkin, *Russian Literature*, ch. VIII

The novel (paraphrased here) follows Vera Pavlovna, who escapes a forced marriage, founds a cooperative sewing workshop, and falls in love with her husband's best friend. The husband, being a "new person," fakes his suicide so that she can be happy, and nobody is jealous. The philosophy behind this is what Chernyshevsky called the "anthropological principle" and others called *rational egoism*: human beings always act from self-interest, but a properly enlightened self-interest coincides with the good of all. Morality needs no God and no sacrifice, only correct calculation. (For the philosophical problems with egoism as a theory of motivation and of value, see [Ethics, Chapter 3](../learn-ethics/03-relativism-and-egoism.md).)

Two images from the novel matter for what follows:

- **Rakhmetov**, the "special person," a minor character who trains himself for revolution. He eats raw beef for strength, reads only books that are essential, gives away his fortune, and once sleeps on a bed of nails to test his endurance. Generations of revolutionaries modeled themselves on him. Lenin titled his 1902 pamphlet on party organization *What Is to Be Done?* in Chernyshevsky's honor.
- **Vera Pavlovna's Fourth Dream**, a vision of the socialist future: people living and working in a vast building of glass and iron, modeled on the Crystal Palace that housed London's Great Exhibition of 1851. Everyone is happy, rational and free.

Chernyshevsky's "nihilist" rejected God, Tsar, Church and marriage as property, and put in their place a serene faith in science, reason and the coming harmony of interests. Kropotkin writes that the novel showed what true nihilists were, "and in what they differed from Turguéneff's Bazároff." The difference was that the true nihilists were not lonely. They had a cause.

### Kropotkin's portrait: nihilism as sincerity

The best firsthand account of the movement in English is Kropotkin's. Writing in 1899, he insists that Western readers have the word wrong:

> First of all, the Nihilist declared war upon what may be described as the 'conventional lies of civilized mankind.' Absolute sincerity was his distinctive feature, and in the name of that sincerity he gave up, and asked others to give up, those superstitions, prejudices, habits, and customs which their own reason could not justify.
>
> — Peter Kropotkin, *Memoirs of a Revolutionist*, Part IV, ch. XII

On art, Kropotkin reports the movement's most notorious slogan, and explains it as a protest against hypocrisy rather than a denial of beauty:

> Continual talk about beauty, the ideal, art for art's sake, æsthetics, and the like, so willingly indulged in--while every object of art was bought with money exacted from starving peasants or from underpaid workers, and the so-called 'worship of the beautiful' was but a mask to cover the most commonplace dissoluteness--inspired him with disgust; and the criticisms of art which one of the greatest artists of the century, Tolstóy, has now powerfully formulated, the Nihilist expressed in the sweeping assertion, 'A pair of boots is more important than all your Madonnas and all your refined talk about Shakespeare.'
>
> — Peter Kropotkin, *Memoirs of a Revolutionist*, Part IV, ch. XII

He also describes the young woman who cut her hair and went to study medicine, and the young man who would not stand when a lady entered but would walk across the city every night to tutor her for free: "it is simply a matter of equality." On Kropotkin's account, nihilism was a moral revolution in manners, its "negation" aimed at hypocrisy. Hold on to this portrait, because the next section presents the opposite picture.

## Dostoevsky's counterattack

Fyodor Dostoevsky (1821–1881) had been a radical himself. Arrested in 1849 with the Petrashevsky circle, which read Fourier, he was led out before a firing squad and reprieved at the last moment. Four years of hard labor in Siberia followed. He came back a Christian and a fierce opponent of the radicals, but he understood them from the inside, and his novels give their ideas stronger arguments than their advocates ever did.

### Notes from the Underground (1864): against the crystal palace

*Notes from the Underground* is a direct reply to *What Is to Be Done?* Its narrator, a retired minor official living in a basement flat in St. Petersburg, introduces himself in one of the great first lines:

> I am a sick man.... I am a spiteful man. I am an unattractive man. I believe my liver is diseased.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Notes from the Underground*, Part I, §I (tr. Constance Garnett)

His sickness is consciousness itself: "to be too conscious is an illness—a real thorough-going illness" (§II). Every motive dissolves under analysis.

His target is Chernyshevsky's rational egoism. If people always act from enlightened self-interest, then once science has calculated our true interests, human behavior can be predicted like the motion of planets. The Underground Man summarizes the program with savage accuracy:

> That is not all; then, you say, science itself will teach man (though to my mind it's a superfluous luxury) that he never has really had any caprice or will of his own, and that he himself is something of the nature of a piano-key or the stop of an organ, and that there are, besides, things called the laws of nature; so that everything he does is not done by his willing it, but is done of itself, by the laws of nature.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Notes from the Underground*, Part I, §VII (tr. Constance Garnett)

Then the "Palace of Crystal" will be built. The Underground Man's objection is that people will refuse it:

> One's own free unfettered choice, one's own caprice, however wild it may be, one's own fancy worked up at times to frenzy—is that very "most advantageous advantage" which we have overlooked, which comes under no classification and against which all systems and theories are continually being shattered to atoms.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Notes from the Underground*, Part I, §VII (tr. Constance Garnett)

And if science proved that human beings really were piano keys, a man "would purposely do something perverse out of simple ingratitude, simply to gain his point," and even "purposely go mad in order to be rid of reason" (§VIII). Then comes the most famous provocation:

> Twice two makes four seems to me simply a piece of insolence. Twice two makes four is a pert coxcomb who stands with arms akimbo barring your path and spitting. I admit that twice two makes four is an excellent thing, but if we are to give everything its due, twice two makes five is sometimes a very charming thing too.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Notes from the Underground*, Part I, §IX (tr. Constance Garnett)

What has this to do with nihilism? The radicals thought they had replaced the old idols with reason. The Underground Man shows that reason, applied consistently, also dissolves the *self* that was supposed to enjoy the rational utopia: if I am a piano key, my choices are not mine, and the only proof of freedom left is caprice and spite. He is what nihilism looks like after the confident phase, when negation has nothing left to negate except the negator. He even prefers suffering to the crystal palace, because "suffering means doubt, negation" (§IX).

The Underground Man is a warning, not Dostoevsky's spokesman. But his insistence that a human being is not reducible to calculable interests made the book a founding text of existentialism; Walter Kaufmann put it first in his anthology *Existentialism from Dostoevsky to Sartre*.

### The Possessed (1871–72): nihilism as possession

In November 1869 a student named Ivan Ivanov was murdered in a Moscow park by a tiny revolutionary cell led by Sergei Nechaev, a fanatic who claimed to represent a vast network. Ivanov had questioned Nechaev's authority, and Nechaev bound the group together by making them accomplices in murder. Nechaev is best known for the *Catechism of a Revolutionary* (1869), whose authorship is still debated: some historians credit Nechaev alone, others think the anarchist Mikhail Bakunin had a hand in it. It begins, in paraphrase: the revolutionary is a doomed man; he has no personal interests, no feelings, no attachments, no property, not even a name; everything in him is absorbed by one passion, the revolution. Whatever helps the revolution is moral, and whatever hinders it is immoral. The *Catechism* is a document of total instrumentalization. Every person and value becomes a means, including the revolutionary himself.

Dostoevsky read the trial reports and built a novel around them. His title, *Besy*, means "demons." Garnett translated it as *The Possessed*. The epigraph comes from the Gospel of Luke:

> "Then went the devils out of the man and entered into the swine; and the herd ran violently down a steep place into the lake and were choked.
>
> — Luke 8:33, epigraph to Dostoevsky, *The Possessed* (tr. Constance Garnett)

Russia is the possessed man, and the ideas imported from the West are the demons that drive the radicals over the cliff. The novel's town is visited by Pyotr Verkhovensky (Garnett: "Verhovensky"), a Nechaev figure, and Nikolai Stavrogin, a gifted aristocrat whom everyone wants to follow and who believes in nothing at all, and so does terrible things out of boredom.

Three portraits in the novel matter for the philosophy of nihilism.

**Shigalov, the theorist.** At a meeting of the conspirators, a gloomy theorist named Shigalov presents his system for the future organization of society:

> I am perplexed by my own data and my conclusion is a direct contradiction of the original idea with which I start. Starting from unlimited freedom, I arrive at unlimited despotism. I will add, however, that there can be no solution of the social problem but mine.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Possessed*, Part II, ch. VII (tr. Constance Garnett)

The meeting laughs. Twentieth-century readers did not: Shigalov's paradox, that a politics which denies every authority ends in the most total authority, reads as prophecy.

**Pyotr Verkhovensky, the operator.** Pyotr has no theory. He wants destruction and power. In the chapter "Ivan the Tsarevitch," he tries to recruit Stavrogin as a figurehead:

> We will proclaim destruction.... Why is it, why is it that idea has such a fascination. But we must have a little exercise; we must. We'll set fires going.... We'll set legends going. Every scurvy 'group' will be of use.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Possessed*, Part II, ch. VIII (tr. Constance Garnett)

"Russia will be overwhelmed with darkness," he adds, "the earth will weep for its old gods." His own question, "*Why* is it that idea has such a fascination," is the question the novel asks. Pyotr is no longer a Bazarov clearing ground for a building. Destruction has become attractive for its own sake.

**Kirillov, the metaphysician.** The engineer Kirillov, the novel's purest thinker, has agreed to kill himself and sign a note taking the blame for the conspiracy's murder, but his reasons are his own: "He who dares kill himself is God," he tells the narrator early on (Part I, ch. III). On his last night, he makes the argument rigorous:

> "If God exists, all is His will and from His will I cannot escape. If not, it's all my will and I am bound to show self-will."
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Possessed*, Part III, ch. VI (tr. Constance Garnett)

"If there is no God, then I am God," Kirillov says, and the highest expression of self-will is to dispose of one's own life without fear. This is Stirner's egoism from [Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md) without Stirner's playfulness, and an early version of Camus's claim that suicide is the one serious philosophical problem; Camus devotes a section of *The Myth of Sisyphus* to Kirillov (see [Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)).

Kirillov's logic deserves to be laid out:

1. If God exists, my will is subordinate to his.
2. If God does not exist, nothing stands above my will.
3. If nothing stands above my will, I must prove it by exercising it on the point where fear of death has always ruled: my own life.
4. God does not exist.
5. Therefore I must kill myself.

Premise 3 is where it breaks down. Why *must* I prove anything, and to whom? Kirillov's "bound to show self-will" smuggles in a duty to freedom after he has denied all duties. He has made a new god of his own will and sacrificed himself to it. In Stirner's language he is possessed by a spook; in Dostoevsky's, he is one of the possessed.

### The Brothers Karamazov (1879–80): "everything is lawful"

Dostoevsky's last novel turns from conspiracy to theology. Ivan Karamazov is an atheist intellectual, a Bazarov with a conscience and a better education. Early on, a relative reports Ivan's thesis to a gathering at the monastery:

> Ivan Fyodorovitch added in parenthesis that the whole natural law lies in that faith, and that if you were to destroy in mankind the belief in immortality, not only love but every living force maintaining the life of the world would at once be dried up. Moreover, nothing then would be immoral, everything would be lawful, even cannibalism.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book II, ch. VI (tr. Constance Garnett)

This is the source of the most famous sentence attributed to Dostoevsky, "If God does not exist, everything is permitted." That exact sentence does not appear in the novel. What appears are variations: "everything is lawful," "all things are lawful," spoken by different characters with different intentions. The difference matters. Ivan floats it as a paradox in conversation. His half-brother, the servant Smerdyakov, takes it as a license, murders their father, and later tells Ivan:

> I did dream of it, chiefly because 'all things are lawful.' That was quite right what you taught me, for you talked a lot to me about that. For if there's no everlasting God, there's no such thing as virtue, and there's no need of it.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book XI, ch. VIII (tr. Constance Garnett)

Ivan is horrified, and breaks down. The novel stages nihilism as a problem of *transmission*: a thought experiment in an intellectual's mind becomes a rule of action in someone less scrupulous. Dostoevsky's charge against the intelligentsia was that they took no responsibility for what their ideas would do in other hands. Whether the thesis is *true* (Plato's *Euthyphro* already doubted that morality depends on the gods; error theorists think it is groundless either way) is taken up in [Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md).

### Ivan's rebellion: nihilism from too much morality

The most surprising thing about Ivan is that his atheism comes from moral outrage, not from a lack of moral sense. In "Rebellion," he tells his devout brother Alyosha true stories from the newspapers about cruelty to children. He refuses any theodicy that would justify their suffering as part of a final harmony, which is "not worth the tears of that one tortured child." He concludes:

> Besides, too high a price is asked for harmony; it's beyond our means to pay so much to enter on it. And so I hasten to give back my entrance ticket, and if I am an honest man I am bound to give it back as soon as possible. And that I am doing. It's not God that I don't accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book V, ch. IV (tr. Constance Garnett)

This is not nihilism in the sense of "nothing matters." It is the opposite: the suffering of a single child matters so much that no cosmic order could outweigh it. Ivan's "rebellion" is a moral judgment passed *on* the universe. He does not deny value. He denies that the world as it is can be reconciled with value. Camus called this "metaphysical rebellion" and placed Ivan at its center in *The Rebel* (1951).

Then Ivan tells Alyosha his prose poem "The Grand Inquisitor." Christ returns to sixteenth-century Seville, and the aged Cardinal Inquisitor has him arrested. In a long monologue, the Inquisitor explains that the Church has "corrected" Christ's work. Christ offered human beings freedom, but freedom is unbearable:

> So long as man remains free he strives for nothing so incessantly and so painfully as to find some one to worship. But man seeks to worship what is established beyond dispute, so that all men would agree at once to worship it.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book V, ch. V (tr. Constance Garnett)

The Inquisitor gives people bread, miracle, mystery and authority, and takes their freedom in return. He does not believe in God himself: he is a nihilist ruling in God's name, Shigalov's paradox in a higher form. Christ answers by kissing him without a word, and Alyosha then kisses Ivan. Dostoevsky's answer to nihilism is not an argument but the elder Zosima's "active love" and the claim that each of us is responsible to all for all. Whether that answers Ivan is one of literature's great open questions.

## From words to bombs: the populists and the People's Will

### "Going to the people"

Meanwhile the movement Dostoevsky feared was changing shape. By the 1870s the radicals had concluded that negation was not enough. Thousands of young people resolved to repay their debt to the peasants whose labor had paid for their education. "It is bitter, the bread that has been made by slaves," Kropotkin quotes the poet Nekrasov, and "the young generation actually refused to eat that bread." Their watchword, he says, was "*V naród!*" ("To the people; be the people"). In the "mad summer" of 1874 some two to three thousand students went to the villages as teachers, nurses and laborers to rouse the peasantry to socialism. The peasants were baffled and often handed these *narodniki* (populists) to the police; well over a thousand were arrested.

### Land and Liberty, and the People's Will

Repression radicalized the survivors. In 1876 they formed a secret society, Land and Liberty (*Zemlya i Volya*). In 1878 Vera Zasulich shot the governor of St. Petersburg, who had ordered a political prisoner flogged, and a jury acquitted her. In 1879 the society split: Black Repartition kept to propaganda among peasants, while the People's Will (*Narodnaya Volya*) formed an Executive Committee that sentenced Alexander II to death.

After several failed attempts, they succeeded. On March 1, 1881 (March 13 by the Western calendar), on the Catherine Canal in St. Petersburg, a first bomb damaged the Tsar's carriage; when Alexander stepped out, a second bomber killed both the Tsar and himself. Sofia Perovskaya, who directed the operation, was hanged with four others. Dostoevsky had died five weeks earlier. The assassination was a catastrophe for its authors: Alexander III answered with a generation of reaction. One of the People's Will's last plots, in 1887, led to the hanging of Alexander Ulyanov, whose younger brother later took the name Lenin.

### Nihilism is not terrorism

To Western newspapers, all this was "nihilism": a bearded bomb-thrower in a fur hat. Oscar Wilde's first play was *Vera; or, The Nihilists* (1880). Kropotkin protested:

> In the press, for example, Nihilism is confused with terrorism. The revolutionary disturbance which broke out in Russia toward the close of the reign of Alexander II., and ended in the tragical death of the Tsar, is constantly described as Nihilism. This is, however, a mistake. To confuse Nihilism with terrorism is as wrong as to confuse a philosophical movement like Stoicism or Positivism with a political movement, such as, for example, republicanism.
>
> — Peter Kropotkin, *Memoirs of a Revolutionist*, Part IV, ch. XII

The distinction is worth keeping. The People's Will believed in the people, in justice and in self-sacrifice to the point of death; ten days after the assassination their Executive Committee offered Alexander III an end to violence in exchange for an amnesty and an elected assembly. Whatever you think of their methods, they are the opposite of Stavrogin's emptiness. And yet Dostoevsky's question remains. When one value is made absolute and all others are cleared away as "prejudice," what stops the absolute from consuming everything, including the people it was meant to serve? That is Shigalov's question, and the twentieth century answered it terribly.

## Was Russian nihilism nihilism?

Here are the figures of this chapter set against the taxonomy from [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md):

| Figure | What is negated | What is affirmed | Nihilist in which sense? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bazarov (early) | Authority, tradition, art, "principles" | Science, utility, work | Political, epistemic (anti-authority) |
| Bazarov (haystack, death) | The significance of any individual life | Courage ("I'm not going to turn tail") | Cosmic, existential |
| Pisarev | Whatever cannot survive criticism | Realism, natural science, the masses | Methodological |
| Chernyshevsky | God, Tsar, property marriage | Rational egoism, socialist harmony | Hardly at all: a utopian |
| Underground Man | Reason's claim to define the self | Caprice, consciousness, suffering | Existential, by reaction |
| Kirillov | God | His own will, to the point of death | Metaphysical, with a new idol |
| Ivan Karamazov | Any harmony bought with children's suffering | The value of each child's suffering | Rebellion, not nihilism |
| The Grand Inquisitor | God (privately) | Human happiness, managed by authority | Nihilism disguised as faith |
| People's Will | The legitimacy of the Tsarist state | The people, justice, self-sacrifice | Political, in name only |

Two patterns stand out.

**First, the Russian nihilists were moralists.** A quip usually attributed to the philosopher Vladimir Solovyov captures it: the radicals reasoned, "Man is descended from monkeys, therefore let us love one another." The "therefore" is a non sequitur. Nothing in Darwin supports the moral conclusion. But the conclusion was held with passion. Isaiah Berlin argued that this was the true character of the Russian intelligentsia: they adopted Western materialism and positivism as *moral* doctrines, as instruments of liberation, and seldom noticed that these doctrines, taken strictly, gave no support to the ideals of justice and equality that drove them.

**Second, Dostoevsky's charge is that the gap cannot stay open forever.** If you teach that morality is a prejudice, sooner or later someone (Smerdyakov, Pyotr Verkhovensky, Nechaev) will believe you. The confident nihilist of the 1860s, still running on the moral capital of Christian Russia, was in Dostoevsky's view like a man sawing off the branch he sits on. Nietzsche would make exactly this argument a decade later about the "English" moralists who abandon God but keep Christian morality. He read Dostoevsky in French translation in 1887 and wrote of him:

> Dostoiewsky's testimony is of importance—Dostoiewsky who, incidentally, was the only psychologist from whom I had anything to learn: he belongs to the happiest windfalls of my life, happier even than the discovery of Stendhal.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Twilight of the Idols*, "Skirmishes in a War with the Age" §45 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Nietzsche also took his sense of the word "nihilism" partly from French discussions of Turgenev and the Russian movement, notably Paul Bourget's *Essais de psychologie contemporaine* (1883). When he defines nihilism with the formula "the highest values are losing their value" (*The Will to Power* §2), he is generalizing the Russian story: a culture's own commitment to truthfulness, the nihilist's "absolute sincerity," turns on the beliefs that gave that commitment its meaning. That is [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md).

Before Nietzsche came a philosopher who had argued, with no political agenda at all, that existence would better not have been. Turgenev read him. So did Tolstoy, who in the late 1870s hid ropes and guns from himself during the crisis of meaning he described in *A Confession* (1882). That philosopher was Arthur Schopenhauer, the subject of [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md).

## Living it

1. **The Bazarov audit.** Take one institution you were raised to respect (a religion, a profession, a nation, a family custom). Apply Pisarev's test honestly: strike it and see what survives. Write two lists, what flew to pieces and what held. Then ask Bazarov's question about your own standard: by what authority did you decide what counts as surviving? You will find you used some value you did not strike. Name it.
2. **The transmission test.** Pick a provocative idea you hold or have enjoyed defending, such as "morality is just evolved preference," "all art is propaganda," or "nobody really has free will." Imagine explaining it, with no nuance, to a resentful acquaintance who wants permission for something. What would he hear? Smerdyakov's problem is not whether the idea is true but whether it is responsibly *held*. Write a paragraph on what, if anything, you owe to the people who will hear your ideas secondhand.
3. **Return the ticket?** Read "Rebellion" and then write your own answer to Ivan in under three hundred words. Do not reach for a theodicy you do not believe. If you agree with him, say what follows for how you live. If you disagree, say what you would say to the child's mother.

## Summary

- **Russian nihilism** was a movement of the 1860s among the new, non-noble educated class. It rejected the authority of Church, Tsar, family and convention in the name of science, utility and sincerity.
- **Turgenev's Bazarov** gave the word its face. His nihilism is anti-authoritarian and utilitarian ("Nature's not a temple, but a workshop"), but the haystack speech and his death show cosmic nihilism breaking through. Turgenev: "when he calls himself nihilist you must read *revolutionist*."
- **Pisarev** preached criticism as a sieve ("smash what can be smashed"). **Chernyshevsky**'s *What Is to Be Done?* offered rational egoism, the ascetic Rakhmetov and the crystal-palace utopia. **Kropotkin** described nihilism as a moral revolution in manners and refused to equate it with terrorism.
- **Dostoevsky** answered in three novels: the Underground Man's caprice against the crystal palace; *The Possessed*'s Shigalov ("Starting from unlimited freedom, I arrive at unlimited despotism"), Verkhovensky and Kirillov ("If there is no God, then I am God"); and *The Brothers Karamazov*'s "everything is lawful," Ivan's moral rebellion and the Grand Inquisitor.
- **The populists** went "to the people" in 1874; repression produced the **People's Will**, which assassinated Alexander II in 1881, and the West made "nihilist" mean bomb-thrower.
- Russian nihilism was negation powered by moral passion. Dostoevsky, and later Nietzsche, argued that the passion was borrowed from the tradition being negated and could not last.

### Dialogue 1 — Bazarov on whether negation is enough

**Bazarov:** You read history and literature at university. Tell me honestly: what use is it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Bazarov in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Expect him to ask "useful to whom?"*

**You:** It helps me understand people. Novels show how other people think. History shows how we got here, so we don't repeat mistakes.

**Bazarov:** Good, you said "use." So you agree that the standard is usefulness. Then let us compare. A course in chemistry produces fertilizer, medicine and clean water. A course in novels produces people who talk about novels. Which is more useful to a peasant with typhus?

**You:** The chemistry, for the typhus. But I'm saying the useful is useful *for* something. Medicine keeps people alive. Alive for what? If nobody reads, sings or loves, then you've kept them alive to do nothing.

**Bazarov:** Let them be alive first. Then we will see what they want to do. The ground wants clearing.

**You:** But you didn't clear the ground at the haystack, did you? You said a human life is a mathematical point and wondered whether anything was worth it. That's not a question chemistry answers. It's a question for the literature you just dismissed.

**Bazarov:** That was weariness, not philosophy.

**You:** Then why, at the end, did you ask a woman to breathe on the dying lamp? That's poetry. You lived by usefulness and died by something else.

**Bazarov:** A dying man is allowed to be sentimental.

**You:** Here's what I'll take from you. You're right that a lot of what passes for culture is display bought with other people's labor, and that the question "useful to whom?" is a fair one to put to my education. But usefulness can't be the *last* standard, because usefulness is always for the sake of something that isn't itself useful. If negation is a tool, I need to know what it's for, and you never told me.

**Bazarov:** For Russia.

**You:** You said yourself, at the end, that Russia didn't need you. I don't think that was true. But I think you needed more than Russia.

### Dialogue 2 — Ivan Karamazov on returning the ticket

**Ivan:** I've told you the stories: the child torn by dogs in front of her mother, the little girl locked in the outhouse. Now answer me. If you could build a world that ended in perfect harmony, where everyone was finally happy and forgiven, but it required the torture of just one small child, would you agree to build it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Ivan in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Notice whether your answer depends on whether God exists.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** No. I wouldn't. No future happiness could make that right.

**Ivan:** Then you are a rebel, like me. You refuse the harmony. And if the world we actually live in was built on exactly that bargain, you refuse the world too.

**You:** I'm not sure the world *was* built on a bargain. That only makes sense if someone designed it. If there's no designer, there's no bargain to refuse. There's just suffering, and the question is what we do about it.

**Ivan:** Clever. But then you've given up the harmony anyway. No one will ever atone for that child. Her tears are simply lost. And if no one atones, and no one judges, then what is left of good and evil? You know my phrase: everything is lawful.

**You:** That doesn't follow. Your whole rebellion depends on that child's suffering really mattering. I think you're more moral than your theory. Smerdyakov took the theory without the morality, and you saw where that led.

**Ivan:** Don't speak of Smerdyakov. You haven't answered the real question. If the suffering matters, and nothing will ever make it right, how can you bear to live in such a world?

**You:** Maybe "bearing" isn't the goal. Your brother Alyosha doesn't answer your argument. He kisses you, and goes off to help a group of boys bury their friend. Perhaps the answer to suffering that can't be justified isn't a justification but a response, doing what can be done for the next child.

**Ivan:** That is love without a reason.

**You:** Or love *as* the reason. Here's where I'll leave it. Your rebellion shows that refusing to justify evil isn't the same as believing nothing matters. In fact it's the opposite. If I end up without God, I'd rather be where you started, outraged on the child's behalf, than where Smerdyakov ended.

**Ivan:** And where do *I* end?

**You:** That's up to you. But I'd keep the ticket in your hand a while longer before you tear it up.

</details>

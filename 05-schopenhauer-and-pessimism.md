# Chapter 5 — Schopenhauer and Pessimism

The Russian nihilists of [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md) denied authority in the name of a better future. This chapter turns to a philosopher who denied the better future itself. Arthur Schopenhauer argued that the inner nature of everything is a blind, insatiable striving he called *will*; that life therefore swings "like a pendulum" between pain and boredom; that this is, in a precise sense, "the worst of all possible worlds"; and that the only real deliverance is for the will to turn against itself and fall silent. Schopenhauer was the first major Western philosopher to make *pessimism* (the claim that existence is, on balance, bad) the center of a system. In the decades after his death, pessimism became a fashion, a scandal and a scholarly controversy, with followers who went further than he did: Philipp Mainländer, who taught that God had committed suicide in creating the world, and Eduard von Hartmann, who predicted that humanity would one day will the universe out of existence. Alongside them stands the Italian poet Giacomo Leopardi, whom Schopenhauer saluted as his kindred spirit. The question that runs through the chapter is whether pessimism is a form of nihilism. Schopenhauer judged existence severely, but to judge it at all he needed a standard of value, and he believed that compassion, art and saintliness were real. Nietzsche, who began as his disciple, would conclude that pessimism was the last disguise of the very values it claimed to see through. Previous: [Chapter 4 — Russian Nihilism](04-russian-nihilism.md). Next: [Chapter 6 — Nietzsche: The Death of God](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md).

## The man who lectured against Hegel

Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) was born in Danzig to a rich merchant father, who wanted him in the business, and a novelist mother, Johanna, who ran a literary salon in Weimar that Goethe attended. His father died, probably by suicide, when Arthur was seventeen. He then left commerce for philosophy and quarreled with his mother so thoroughly that they did not meet for the last twenty-four years of her life.

At thirty he published his masterpiece, *The World as Will and Representation* (1818/19). The old translation used in this chapter, by R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp, renders the German *Vorstellung* as "Idea," so the title appears as *The World as Will and Idea*. Almost nobody read it. In 1820 he became a lecturer at Berlin and, in a gesture of magnificent arrogance, scheduled his lectures at the same hour as those of Hegel, the most famous philosopher in Germany. Hegel drew crowds, and Schopenhauer drew a handful of students. He gave up teaching, lived on his inheritance in Frankfurt with a succession of poodles, all named Atma ("world-soul"), and nursed a lifelong contempt for "university philosophy." Fame came only in the 1850s, after a volume of popular essays, *Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851). By the time he died, readers across Europe were discovering him. Richard Wagner sent him the libretto of *The Ring*. Tolstoy, reading him in 1869, called him a genius. Nietzsche found the book in a Leipzig secondhand shop in 1865, took it home, and was converted.

## The world as idea

Schopenhauer began from Kant. Kant had argued that space, time and causality are the forms in which a knowing mind receives the world, not features of things as they are in themselves. Schopenhauer's first sentence compresses this into a slogan:

> "The world is my idea:"—this is a truth which holds good for everything that lives and knows, though man alone can bring it into reflective and abstract consciousness. If he really does this, he has attained to philosophical wisdom. It then becomes clear and certain to him that what he knows is not a sun and an earth, but only an eye that sees a sun, a hand that feels an earth
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §1 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

This is not yet pessimism. It is idealism of the Kantian kind: the world as we experience it is a *representation*, structured by our minds. You may notice its kinship with the Cartesian and Fichtean starting points that worried Jacobi in [Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md). If the world is only "my idea," what is it *in itself*? Kant said we could never know. Schopenhauer thought he had found a back door.

## The world as will

The back door is your own body. You know your body in two ways. From outside, it is an object among objects, an arrangement of flesh obeying physical laws. From inside, you experience its movements as *willing*: you want to raise your arm, and the arm rises. These are not two events, a volition causing a motion, but one event known in two ways (§18). Schopenhauer then generalizes. What you find inside yourself, the striving, wanting, pushing, is the inner nature of everything: the force that makes the magnet point north, the plant turn toward the light, the animal hunt, and the lovers fall into each other's arms. He calls this inner nature *will*, while warning that it is not conscious, rational or purposeful:

> The will, which, considered purely in itself, is without knowledge, and is merely a blind incessant impulse, as we see it appear in unorganised and vegetable nature and their laws, and also in the vegetative part of our own life, receives through the addition of the world as idea, which is developed in subjection to it, the knowledge of its own willing and of what it is that it wills.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §54 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

Notice how different this is from every previous metaphysics in the West. For Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics and the Christian philosophers, the ultimate reality was rational and good: the Form of the Good, the unmoved mover, *logos*, God. (On the Stoic version, see [Stoicism, Chapter 6](../learn-stoicism/06-virtue-and-the-good.md).) Even Hegel's world-spirit was reason working itself out through history. Schopenhauer turned the picture upside down. At the bottom of things there is no reason and no purpose, only an endless craving that wants nothing in particular, can never be satisfied, and tears itself apart in the struggle of each of its manifestations against the others. The intellect, which philosophers had treated as our highest faculty, is the will's servant, a lamp it lights to find its food.

In this sense Schopenhauer is a *metaphysical nihilist* about purpose, in the terms of [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md): the world has no goal, no meaning, no design. But he is not a nihilist about *value*. On the contrary, the next step in his argument is a fierce evaluative judgment.

## Why life is suffering

### The argument from desire

Schopenhauer's case for pessimism has a simple structure:

1. Living beings are expressions of will, and to will is to want.
2. To want is to lack something, and lack is a kind of pain.
3. When a desire is satisfied, the pain stops, but nothing positive takes its place; either a new want arises at once, or, if none arises, we are bored.
4. Boredom is itself a form of suffering, the felt emptiness of existence.
5. So life oscillates between the pain of want and the pain of boredom, with brief, merely negative intervals of relief between them.

He puts the core of it in one of his most famous passages:

> Willing and striving is its whole being, which may be very well compared to an unquenchable thirst. But the basis of all willing is need, deficiency, and thus pain. Consequently, the nature of brutes and man is subject to pain originally and through its very being. If, on the other hand, it lacks objects of desire, because it is at once deprived of them by a too easy satisfaction, a terrible void and ennui comes over it, _i.e._, its being and existence itself becomes an unbearable burden to it. Thus its life swings like a pendulum backwards and forwards between pain and ennui.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §57 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

He adds a joke: after humanity "had transferred all pain and torments to hell, there then remained nothing over for heaven but ennui." Premise 3, the *negativity thesis*, is stated in the next section:

> All satisfaction, or what is commonly called happiness, is always really and essentially only _negative_, and never positive. It is not an original gratification coming to us of itself, but must always be the satisfaction of a wish.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §58 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

Even the body, on this view, is a holding action. Walking is "a constantly prevented falling," and so:

> Every breath we draw wards off the death that is constantly intruding upon us. In this way we fight with it every moment, and again, at longer intervals, through every meal we eat, every sleep we take, every time we warm ourselves, &c. In the end, death must conquer, for we became subject to him through birth, and he only plays for a little while with his prey before he swallows it up.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §57 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

### The argument from the facts

Schopenhauer did not rest on the a priori argument alone. He also appealed to what you can see if you look. His later essays, written for a general audience, are full of grimly comic one-liners:

> The pleasure in this world, it has been said, outweighs the pain; or, at any rate, there is an even balance between the two. If the reader wishes to see shortly whether this statement is true, let him compare the respective feelings of two animals, one of which is engaged in eating the other.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *Studies in Pessimism*, "On the Sufferings of the World" (tr. T. Bailey Saunders)

In *The World as Will and Idea* the same appeal becomes an indictment of optimism:

> if we were to conduct the confirmed optimist through the hospitals, infirmaries, and surgical operating-rooms, through the prisons, torture-chambers, and slave-kennels, over battle-fields and places of execution; if we were to open to him all the dark abodes of misery, where it hides itself from the glance of cold curiosity, and, finally, allow him to glance into the starving dungeon of Ugolino, he, too, would understand at last the nature of this "best of possible worlds."
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §59 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

The phrase "best of possible worlds" is aimed at Leibniz, whose *Theodicy* (1710) argued that God, being perfectly good and wise, must have created the best world possible. Voltaire had already mocked this in *Candide* after the Lisbon earthquake of 1755. Schopenhauer goes further: optimism is not just false but "a really _wicked_ way of thinking, as a bitter mockery of the unspeakable suffering of humanity" (§59). In the second edition he added a mock-proof that this is in fact the *worst* possible world:

> For possible means, not what one may construct in imagination, but what can actually exist and continue. Now this world is so arranged as to be able to maintain itself with great difficulty; but if it were a little worse, it could no longer maintain itself. Consequently a worse world, since it could not continue to exist, is absolutely impossible: thus this world itself is the worst of all possible worlds.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. III, ch. XLVI (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

The argument is a parody, and it has an obvious flaw: a world could be much worse without being less stable, just as a building can be uglier without being closer to collapse. But the parody makes a serious point. Leibniz's optimism was also an a priori argument that ignored the evidence. If you are going to reason from first principles about the goodness of the world, the principles can be bent either way.

### Life as a mistake

From here it is a short step to the conclusion that existence itself would better not have been:

> Human life must be some kind of mistake. The truth of this will be sufficiently obvious if we only remember that man is a compound of needs and necessities hard to satisfy; and that even when they are satisfied, all he obtains is a state of painlessness, where nothing remains to him but abandonment to boredom. This is direct proof that existence has no real value in itself; for what is boredom but the feeling of the emptiness of life?
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *Studies in Pessimism*, "On the Vanity of Existence" (tr. T. Bailey Saunders)

And, in a passage that is the seed of modern *antinatalism*, the view that it is wrong to have children (see [Chapter 13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md)):

> If children were brought into the world by an act of pure reason alone, would the human race continue to exist? Would not a man rather have so much sympathy with the coming generation as to spare it the burden of existence? or at any rate not take it upon himself to impose that burden upon it in cold blood.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *Studies in Pessimism*, "On the Sufferings of the World" (tr. T. Bailey Saunders)

### Testing the argument

Schopenhauer's pessimism is a philosophical thesis, so you should test it like one. Three objections are standard.

**Against the negativity thesis.** Are all pleasures merely the relief of a prior want? Consider the smell of coffee you didn't know was brewing, a joke you didn't see coming, or the absorption of a musician playing well (what psychologists call "flow"). None of these seems to be the removal of a felt lack. If some pleasures are positive, premise 3 fails, and the pendulum has a third position.

**Against the boredom premise.** Boredom is real, but is it the inevitable state of a satisfied being? Many people describe their best moments as times of engaged activity, not of satisfied rest. Aristotle had already argued that happiness is an *activity*, not a state of having what one wants. Schopenhauer's picture fits the consumer, who wants an object, gets it and wants another, better than it fits the craftsman or the friend.

**Against the inference to "should not be."** Suppose life does contain more suffering than pleasure. Does it follow that life is not worth living? Only if the value of a life is the sum of its pleasures minus its pains. That is a hedonist assumption, and most ethical traditions reject it. A life might contain more pain than pleasure and still contain love, understanding and achievement that make it worth living. Schopenhauer would answer that these goods are themselves forms of will and so bring suffering in their train, but that answer assumes what it needed to prove.

None of these objections is decisive, and the debate is alive in contemporary philosophy, especially in the "asymmetry" arguments of David Benatar ([Chapter 13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md)). But they show that Schopenhauer's pessimism depends on specific, contestable premises about desire and value. It is not simply what anyone sees who looks honestly at the world.

## Is pessimism nihilism?

It is tempting to file Schopenhauer under "nihilism" and move on. That would be a mistake, and seeing why sharpens the concept.

A *value nihilist* holds that nothing is good or bad. Schopenhauer holds the opposite: suffering is bad, very bad, and there is a great deal of it. His whole system is a verdict *on* the world, and a verdict needs a standard. He is closer to Ivan Karamazov in [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md), who returns his ticket because a child's suffering matters too much, than to Bazarov or Stirner.

Moreover, Schopenhauer had a positive ethics, one of the most attractive in the nineteenth century. The source of morality, he argued, is *compassion* (*Mitleid*), the direct participation in another's suffering. Compassion is possible because individuality is ultimately an illusion. At the level of the will, the torturer and the victim are one being, and the person of good character dimly sees this. Schopenhauer borrowed a formula from the Upanishads:

> by the formula of the Vedas already quoted: "Tat twam asi!" (This thou art!) Whoever is able to say this to himself, with regard to every being with whom he comes in contact, with clear knowledge and firm inward conviction, is certain of all virtue and blessedness, and is on the direct road to salvation.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §66 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

Schopenhauer extended compassion to animals, which he insisted have moral standing, and attacked the European tradition (Christian and Kantian alike) for denying it (see [Ethics, Chapter 20](../learn-ethics/20-animals-and-the-environment.md)).

So the right classification is this: Schopenhauer is a **metaphysical nihilist about purpose** (the world has no meaning or design), an **evaluative pessimist** (existence is, on balance, bad), and an **ethical realist of compassion** (suffering is really bad and compassion really good). That combination is unstable, and Nietzsche would exploit the instability. If there is no God and no purpose, where do Schopenhauer's standards come from? Nietzsche's answer, as you will see, was that they come from Christianity, and that Schopenhauer had kept the Christian verdict on the world ("this world is a vale of tears") after throwing out the Christian God.

## Three escapes

If the world is will and will is suffering, is there any way out? Schopenhauer offers three, in ascending order of finality.

### Art: the Sabbath of willing

In aesthetic contemplation, he argues, we stop wanting. When you lose yourself in a landscape or a painting, you no longer see things as means to your ends. You become, for a moment, a "pure will-less subject of knowledge," and what you perceive are the timeless Ideas (in roughly Plato's sense) of which individual things are copies. The effect is relief:

> Then all at once the peace which we were always seeking, but which always fled from us on the former path of the desires, comes to us of its own accord, and it is well with us. It is the painless state which Epicurus prized as the highest good and as the state of the gods; for we are for the moment set free from the miserable striving of the will; we keep the Sabbath of the penal servitude of willing; the wheel of Ixion stands still.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §38 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

(Ixion, in Greek myth, was bound to an eternally turning wheel of fire in the underworld.) Music holds a special place. The other arts copy the Ideas, but music, Schopenhauer says, is "the _copy of the will itself_" (§52), which is why it moves us so deeply: it expresses the striving, tension and resolution of the will without any particular object. Wagner read this in 1854 and wrote *Tristan und Isolde* under its spell. Schopenhauer gave the nineteenth century a metaphysics of music, and through Wagner and the young Nietzsche's *Birth of Tragedy* it shaped modern art.

But art's relief is temporary. The concert ends, and the wheel turns again.

### Compassion

The second escape is moral. The compassionate person sees through the "veil of Mâyâ" (Schopenhauer's term, borrowed from Indian philosophy, for the illusion of separate individuality) and treats others' suffering as his own. This is better than art, because it is not just a moment's distraction but a change in how one lives. Yet compassion still operates within the world of will. It relieves suffering here and there, and cannot touch its source.

### Denial of the will

The final and only complete escape is what Schopenhauer calls the *denial of the will to live*. In rare people (saints, ascetics, mystics of every religion) the knowledge that all life is suffering becomes so complete that the will turns against itself. Such a person stops desiring: practices chastity, poverty and fasting, accepts injury without resentment, and waits for death with serenity. Schopenhauer cites Christian saints, Hindu *sannyasins* and Buddhist monks as examples of the same phenomenon.

He is careful to distinguish this from suicide:

> Far from being denial of the will, suicide is a phenomenon of strong assertion of will; for the essence of negation lies in this, that the joys of life are shunned, not its sorrows. The suicide wills life, and is only dissatisfied with the conditions under which it has presented itself to him.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §69 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

This is a subtle and important point. The person who kills himself in despair *wants* a different life and destroys this one because it fails him. His act is an expression of will, not its quieting. The saint, by contrast, no longer wants anything, including death.

And what is left when the will is wholly denied? Schopenhauer's answer is the last sentence of the book, one of the most extraordinary endings in philosophy:

> Rather do we freely acknowledge that what remains after the entire abolition of will is for all those who are still full of will certainly nothing; but, conversely, to those in whom the will has turned and has denied itself, this our world, which is so real, with all its suns and milky-ways—is nothing.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. I, §71 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

The final word is "nothing," and it is double-edged. From the standpoint of the will, salvation looks like annihilation, the "empty nothingness" that we "fear as children fear the dark." From the standpoint of salvation, it is *our* world that is nothing. Schopenhauer will not say which nothing is the real one.

### A note on Buddhism

Schopenhauer kept a gilded statue of the Buddha in his apartment and believed that his philosophy and Buddhism agreed on the essentials: life is suffering (*dukkha*), suffering arises from craving (*tanhā*), and liberation (*nirvana*) comes from the extinction of craving. His footnote to the word "nothing" reads: "This is also just the Prajna—Paramita of the Buddhists, the 'beyond all knowledge'." He wrote at a time when European knowledge of Buddhism was thin, filtered through a few translations and missionary reports, and his reading, though sympathetic, was partly a projection of his own system.

That reading had a long afterlife. Through Schopenhauer, many nineteenth-century Europeans came to think of Buddhism as a "religion of nothingness" and of nirvana as annihilation. Nietzsche called Buddhism a "passive nihilism." As [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md) explained, Buddhist traditions themselves reject this: the Buddha explicitly rejected "annihilationism" (*ucchedavāda*) alongside "eternalism," and Nagarjuna's emptiness (*śūnyatā*) is a claim about how things exist (dependently, without fixed essence), not a claim that nothing exists or matters. Nirvana is described in early texts as peace, freedom and the highest happiness, not as a void. Schopenhauer's own ending is more careful than his followers: "nothing" is how liberation *looks from the outside*, to a mind still full of will.

## Leopardi: the poet of the mockery of existence

### "No one has so thoroughly handled this subject"

When Schopenhauer surveyed the writers who had seen the misery of life most clearly, he gave the crown to an Italian:

> Yet no one has so thoroughly and exhaustively handled this subject as, in our own day, Leopardi. He is entirely filled and penetrated by it: his theme is everywhere the mockery and wretchedness of this existence; he presents it upon every page of his works, yet in such a multiplicity of forms and applications, with such wealth of imagery that he never wearies us, but, on the contrary, is throughout entertaining and exciting.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, vol. III, ch. XLVI (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

Giacomo Leopardi (1798–1837) grew up in the small papal town of Recanati, in a noble family's vast library, and taught himself Greek, Hebrew and half a dozen modern languages before he was eighteen. Years of study bent his spine and ruined his eyes. He spent most of his short life in poverty and ill health, and died in Naples during a cholera epidemic, aged thirty-eight. He is Italy's greatest poet after Dante and the author of an extraordinary philosophical notebook, the *Zibaldone*, and of a book of satirical prose dialogues, the *Operette morali* (1827). He reached pessimism independently of Schopenhauer and before him.

### Nature and the Icelander

Leopardi's most famous dialogue sends an Icelander, who has fled cold and storms all his life, to the equator, where he finds Nature herself, a colossal woman sitting against a mountain. He lists his complaints: everywhere he went, she persecuted him with heat, cold, disease and decay. Nature replies:

> _Nature_. Thinkest thou then that the world was made for thee? It is time thou knewest that in my designs, operations, and decrees, I never gave a thought to the happiness or unhappiness of man. If I cause you to suffer, I am unaware of the fact; nor do I perceive that I can in any way give you pleasure. What I do is in no sense done for your enjoyment or benefit, as you seem to think. Finally, if I by chance exterminated your species, I should not know it.
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, "Dialogue between Nature and an Icelander," *Essays and Dialogues* (tr. Charles Edwardes)

This is cosmic indifference, the theme Pascal feared and Bazarov felt under his haystack, stated as plainly as it ever has been. The Icelander answers with the question that the whole of this course circles around:

> For whose pleasure and service is this wretched life of the world maintained, by the suffering and death of all the beings which compose it?
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, "Dialogue between Nature and an Icelander," *Essays and Dialogues* (tr. Charles Edwardes)

He gets no answer. Two starving lions appear and eat him, and they are so weak that the meal only gives them strength "to live to the end of the day." Leopardi's dark comedy is the point. The universe does not even feed its predators well.

### The almanac seller

A second, gentler dialogue anticipates one of Nietzsche's central ideas. On New Year's Eve, a passer-by asks a street vendor whether he would like to live his past life over again, exactly as it was, with all its pleasures and pains. The vendor says no. Nor would anyone, the passer-by suggests, from prince to peasant. What everyone wants is a life whose contents they do not know in advance:

> It is what I should wish, had I to live my life over again, and so would every one. But this proves that Fate has treated us all badly. And it is clear that each person is of opinion that the evil he has experienced exceeds the good, if no one would wish to be re-born on condition of living his own life over again from the beginning, with just its same proportion of good and evil.
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, "Dialogue between an Almanac Seller and a Passer-by," *Essays and Dialogues* (tr. Charles Edwardes)

Then he buys the best almanac, and the seller goes on calling, "Almanacs! New Almanacs!" Remember this test. Nietzsche will turn it into "the heaviest burden" (Kaufmann translates "the greatest weight") in [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md): would you will to live your life again, exactly as it was, innumerable times? Leopardi thinks honest people answer no. Nietzsche thinks the capacity to answer yes is the mark of a life that has overcome nihilism.

### The poems: from the infinite to the broom flower

Leopardi's poems trace the same arc. In "The Infinite" (1819), sitting behind a hedge that blocks the horizon, he imagines the "boundless spaces" beyond it, and the poem ends: "And sweet to me is shipwreck on this sea." The infinite is not yet terrifying; it is a kind of drowning bliss. In the "Night Song of a Wandering Shepherd in Asia," a herdsman asks the moon what everything means:

> "Why all these torches bright?
> What mean these depths of air,
> This vast, this silent sky,
> This nightly solitude? And what am I?"
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, "Night Song of a Wandering Shepherd in Asia," *Poems* (tr. Frederick Townsend)

By the short poem "To Himself" (1833), written after a failed love, the last illusion has gone:

> Nor wilt thou rest forever, weary heart.
> The last illusion is destroyed,
> That I eternal thought. Destroyed!
> I feel all hope and all desire depart,
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, "To Himself," *Poems* (tr. Frederick Townsend)

But Leopardi did not end there. His last great poem, "The Ginestra" (1836), is addressed to the broom flower that grows on the lava fields of Vesuvius, above the ruins of Pompeii. The broom does not pretend the volcano is kind, and it does not bow its head before it. The poem's ethical conclusion is solidarity. The noble person, Leopardi says, tells the truth about our condition, names nature (not other people) as the enemy, and:

> And thinking all the human race
> Against her armed, as is the case,
> E'en from the first, united and arrayed,
> All men esteems confederates,
> And with true love embraces all,
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, "The Ginestra," *Poems* (tr. Frederick Townsend)

This is an important alternative within pessimism. Schopenhauer's response to a hostile world is to withdraw from willing. Leopardi's, at the end, is a fraternity of the doomed: since nature is our common enemy, the absurdity is to fight one another. A century later, Albert Camus would make almost the same move in *The Plague*, whose doctor fights an epidemic he cannot defeat, alongside others, because it is the decent thing to do (see [Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)).

## After Schopenhauer: the pessimism controversy

From the 1860s to the 1890s, pessimism was the most discussed philosophical topic in Germany. Frederick Beiser calls this the "pessimism controversy" (*Pessimismusstreit*). The question "Is life worth living?" was debated in books, journals and lecture halls, and pessimism became a fashionable pose among the educated young. Two of Schopenhauer's successors pushed his ideas to remarkable conclusions. Neither has a public-domain English translation on Project Gutenberg, so they are paraphrased here.

### Mainländer: the death of God as the birth of the world

Philipp Batz (1841–1876), who wrote under the name Philipp Mainländer ("the man from the Main"), was a merchant's son from Offenbach who discovered Schopenhauer at nineteen. His single major work, *The Philosophy of Redemption* (*Die Philosophie der Erlösung*, 1876), offers a cosmic myth wrapped in a metaphysics.

Before the world existed, Mainländer argued, there was a single, simple, divine unity. This unity found its own existence unbearable and wanted not to be. But being simple, it could not simply cease, so it shattered itself into the multiplicity of the world. The universe is the process of God's dissolution, the fragments of a deity moving, through all their striving, toward the nothingness that God originally willed. "God has died, and his death was the life of the world" is the usual summary of his thought. Every living thing, on this view, harbors under its will to live a deeper will to *death*. Mainländer's ethics drew the consequences: chastity, so as not to prolong the process; and, for those ready for it, a calm acceptance of death, including voluntary death.

He saw the first volume through the press in early 1876 and, on 1 April, shortly after the first printed copies reached him, hanged himself in Offenbach, aged thirty-four. (The story that he stood on a pile of his own books is a later embellishment.) Nietzsche, in *The Joyful Wisdom*, dismissed him contemptuously as a "mawkish apostle of virginity." Mainländer is worth knowing for two reasons. First, he is the most consistent case of a philosopher for whom nihilism was not a danger to be overcome but a *goal*: non-being as redemption. Second, he gives a striking early version of the phrase "God is dead," six years before Nietzsche's madman. For Mainländer the death of God is a literal, cosmological event at the beginning of time. For Nietzsche it would be a cultural event in the present.

### Hartmann: the unconscious and the end of the world

Eduard von Hartmann (1842–1906), a former Prussian officer forced out of the army by a knee injury, published *The Philosophy of the Unconscious* in 1869. It was a sensation, running through eleven editions by 1904. Hartmann synthesized Schopenhauer's will with Hegel's reason: the ultimate reality is an "Unconscious" that is both will and idea, blind striving and hidden purpose.

The most famous part of the book is its treatment of happiness as a series of illusions that humanity must outgrow:

1. **The first stage** is the belief that happiness can be attained in this life, through pleasure, wealth, love or honor. The ancient world and every young person live in it. Experience refutes it.
2. **The second stage** is the belief that happiness will come in a life after death. Christianity lives in it. Science and criticism refute it.
3. **The third stage** is the belief that happiness will come in the future of the race, through progress in science, politics and economics. The modern world lives in it. Hartmann argues that progress multiplies needs as fast as it satisfies them, and increases sensitivity to suffering faster than it removes the causes.

When humanity has passed through all three illusions, Hartmann speculated, it will be ready for the final act: a collective, coordinated denial of the will, by which the conscious part of the world would bring the whole world process to an end. Where Schopenhauer's saint denies the will alone, Hartmann imagined a planetary act of redemption, and he trusted the purposive Unconscious to lead history there.

Hartmann's book was mocked as much as it was admired. Nietzsche, in *The Joyful Wisdom* §357, wondered whether Hartmann was not simply "too clever for us," a rogue who had made fun of German pessimism from the start. But the three stages of illusion were influential, and they offer a useful model for the history of nihilism itself: each stage is a source of meaning that fails, and the question is what remains when the last one has gone.

## Nietzsche's verdict: the question and the premature answer

Friedrich Nietzsche began as Schopenhauer's disciple. His *Birth of Tragedy* (1872) is soaked in Schopenhauer's metaphysics of music, and his essay *Schopenhauer as Educator* (1874) honors him as a model of intellectual honesty. By the 1880s Nietzsche had turned against him, but he never stopped crediting Schopenhauer with having asked the decisive question:

> When we thus reject the Christian interpretation, and condemn its "significance" as a forgery, we are immediately confronted in a striking manner with the _Schopenhauerian_ question: _Has existence then a significance at all?_—the question which will require a couple of centuries even to be completely heard in all its profundity.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §357 (tr. Thomas Common)

But Schopenhauer's *answer*, Nietzsche goes on, was "a premature, juvenile reply, a mere compromise, a stoppage and sticking in the very same Christian-ascetic, moral perspectives, _the belief in which had got notice to quit_ along with the belief in God." In other words, Schopenhauer's verdict that life is bad and ought to be denied is not the conclusion of a godless world-view. It is the Christian verdict on "this world" with God subtracted. The saint who renounces the will is the Christian ascetic in Indian dress.

In the notebooks published after his death as *The Will to Power*, Nietzsche presses the point. He asks:

> _To what extent does Schopenhauerian Nihilism continue to be the result of the same ideal as that which gave rise to Christian Theism?_
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, §17 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

This is the hinge of the course. For Nietzsche, pessimism is not the *truth* that remains when illusions have been stripped away. It is a *symptom*: the reaction of a culture that still measures the world by a standard (an ideal of eternal peace, painlessness, a "true world" beyond this one) which the world necessarily fails. Change the standard, and the verdict changes. Schopenhauer's "nothing" at the end of the book, on this reading, is the same nothing that the Christian beyond had always been, now named honestly. Nietzsche's diagnosis of how Europe's highest values came to devalue themselves is the subject of [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md).

## Living it

1. **The pendulum log.** For three days, note five times a day whether you are (a) wanting something you lack, (b) bored, or (c) in some third state that is neither. Describe each case of (c) in a sentence. At the end, judge Schopenhauer's negativity thesis by your own evidence. Is your third state just "relief," or is it something positive? What kinds of activity produce it?
2. **The almanac question.** Answer the passer-by honestly in writing: would you live your past life again, exactly as it was, with no changes? If not, what precisely would you need to change, and why? If yes, what makes it worth repeating, including the bad parts? Keep your answer. You will need it in [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md).
3. **The broom-flower exercise.** Leopardi's response to an indifferent nature was solidarity. Pick one source of suffering that no one can abolish (illness, aging, grief, a chronic condition in yourself or someone close). Write down one concrete act, this week, that treats others who suffer from it as "confederates" rather than strangers. Do it, and write afterwards whether it changed how the suffering itself appeared to you.

## Summary

- **Schopenhauer** inherited Kant's distinction between the world as it appears ("The world is my idea") and the thing-in-itself, and identified the thing-in-itself as **will**: a blind, purposeless, insatiable striving at the root of everything.
- His **pessimism** argues that all willing springs from lack, that satisfaction is merely **negative** (the cessation of pain), and that life swings "like a pendulum" between pain and boredom. He adds empirical arguments (the two animals, the optimist's tour of hospitals and prisons) and a parody-proof that this is "the worst of all possible worlds."
- The **objections**: positive pleasures exist, activity is not boredom, and pain outweighing pleasure does not by itself show that life is not worth living.
- Schopenhauer is a nihilist about **purpose** but not about **value**: his verdict on the world presupposes that suffering is bad, and his ethics rests on **compassion** ("Tat twam asi").
- The three escapes are **art** ("the wheel of Ixion stands still"; music as the copy of the will), **compassion**, and the **denial of the will** by the ascetic saint, which is not suicide (suicide is an *assertion* of will). The book ends with "nothing," a word seen from two sides.
- He read Buddhism as kindred, but nirvana is not annihilation, and **Buddhism is not nihilism**.
- **Leopardi** reached pessimism independently: Nature's indifference to the Icelander, the almanac seller who would not relive his life, and, in "The Ginestra," solidarity among human beings against a hostile nature.
- **Mainländer** taught that the world is the dissolution of a God who willed not to be, and took his own life. **Hartmann** described three stages of illusion (happiness now, hereafter, in the future) and imagined humanity's collective negation of the will.
- **Nietzsche** credited Schopenhauer with the question "Has existence then a significance at all?" but called his answer a Christian-ascetic compromise: pessimism is a symptom of the old values, not their refutation.

### Dialogue 1 — Schopenhauer on the pendulum

**Schopenhauer:** Tell me about the best thing that happened to you this month.

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Schopenhauer in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Expect him to ask what you wanted before it happened.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I got the internship I applied for. I was thrilled.

**Schopenhauer:** And before you got it?

**You:** I was anxious for weeks. I checked my email constantly.

**Schopenhauer:** So the "best thing" was the end of a month of suffering. And how long did the thrill last?

**You:** A couple of days, honestly. Then I started worrying about whether I'd be good at it.

**Schopenhauer:** You see? The want was long and painful; the satisfaction brief and merely negative, the removal of anxiety; and then a new want arrived at once to take its place. You have just described my pendulum.

**You:** That fits this example. But last weekend I went hiking with friends, and at one point we came over a ridge and saw a valley I'd never seen. I didn't want that view beforehand. I didn't even know it existed. That wasn't relief. It was just joy.

**Schopenhauer:** Ah, but that is my second door. You were, for a moment, a pure will-less subject of knowledge, contemplating the Idea. That is aesthetic experience, and I grant it is real. It is also brief.

**You:** Brief, sure. But you can't have it both ways. If there are moments of positive joy, then happiness isn't *always* negative. Your thesis needs an exception, and the exception is the best part of life.

**Schopenhauer:** An exception that lasts ten minutes, against a life of seventy years.

**You:** Maybe. Or maybe a lot more of life could be like that ridge if I stopped living it as a string of wants. That's almost your own advice, isn't it? Want less, see more.

**Schopenhauer:** That is the beginning of the denial of the will.

**You:** Or the beginning of a different way of willing. Here's where I come out. You've convinced me that a lot of my life runs on the pendulum, and that chasing the next thing won't get me off it. But you haven't convinced me that life *is* the pendulum. The view from the ridge wasn't nothing. I'd rather learn to have more of those than learn to want nothing.

**Schopenhauer:** Come back when you are sixty and tell me how many ridges you found.

### Dialogue 2 — Leopardi's passer-by on living your life again

**Passer-by:** Happy New Year, friend. A question, since you're buying an almanac: would you like this coming year to be just like the past twenty? Or rather, would you like to live your whole life over again, exactly as it was, every pleasure and every pain?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer the passer-by in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Notice whether your answer depends on the worst moments or on the best.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Exactly as it was? No. There are a couple of years I'd never want to go through again.

**Passer-by:** Just as I thought. And so would everyone. Then it's clear that each of us thinks the bad in his life has outweighed the good. Otherwise you'd take the bargain.

**You:** That doesn't follow. I wouldn't want to *repeat* those years, but that's not the same as wishing my life had never happened. Some of the best things in my life came out of them.

**Passer-by:** Then take the whole package! The bad years with the good fruits.

**You:** That's harder. Knowing what's coming would change it. Part of what made the good things good was that they were surprises.

**Passer-by:** Exactly my point. What we love is not the life we know, but the life we don't know yet. The future, always the future. That's what I'm buying with this almanac: hope.

**You:** So the only life worth wanting is one you haven't lived. That's bleak.

**Passer-by:** It's honest. Have you a better answer?

**You:** Maybe this. If I refuse to relive my life, I'm judging it by a standard that only an imaginary life could meet, one with all the good and none of the bad. But no real life is like that. Maybe the question isn't whether my life passes that test, but whether I can stand behind the life I actually had, bad years included.

**Passer-by:** Stand behind it? You'd say yes to the bad years?

**You:** I'm not there. But I can see that it's a different question from yours, and a better one. Yours asks whether life adds up to a profit. Mine asks whether I can own it.

**Passer-by:** A German philosopher will make a great deal of that question one day. In the meantime, buy the almanac.

</details>

# Chapter 6 — Nietzsche: The Death of God

Friedrich Nietzsche did not invent the word "nihilism" ([Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md)), and he was not the first to imagine that God might be dead (Jean Paul and Hegel had both used the image; Mainländer had made it a cosmology, [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md)). What he did was turn nihilism from an accusation into a diagnosis. For Jacobi, nihilism was what happened to philosophers who reasoned too much; for the Russian conservatives, what happened to young people who read too much; for Dostoevsky, what happened to anyone who stopped believing in God. Nietzsche agreed that the loss of God was the decisive event of the age, but he insisted that it was not a mistake that could be corrected by believing harder. It was the *consequence* of Europe's own highest values, above all its demand for truthfulness, and it would unfold over "the next two centuries" whether anyone wanted it to or not. This chapter reads the parable of the madman in *The Joyful Wisdom* §125 slowly, follows Nietzsche's analysis of how "the highest values devalue themselves" in the notebooks published as *The Will to Power*, distinguishes the passive from the active form of nihilism, and ends with the figure Nietzsche feared more than the despairing nihilist: the comfortable "last man" who has "discovered happiness" and blinks. The constructive response, the revaluation of values, eternal recurrence and the *Übermensch*, is the subject of [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md). Previous: [Chapter 5 — Schopenhauer and Pessimism](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md). Next: [Chapter 7 — Nietzsche Beyond Nihilism](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md).

## A pastor's son

Friedrich Wilhelm Nietzsche was born in 1844 in Röcken, a village in Prussian Saxony, the son and grandson of Lutheran pastors. His father died when he was four. He was a brilliant, pious schoolboy at Schulpforta, Germany's most rigorous classical boarding school, studied theology briefly at Bonn, dropped it, and moved to classical philology at Leipzig. There, in 1865, he found Schopenhauer's *World as Will and Idea* in a secondhand bookshop. In 1869, at twenty-four and before he had finished his doctorate, he was appointed professor of classical philology at Basel, in Switzerland. He became a close friend of Richard Wagner, and his first book, *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872), was in part a manifesto for Wagner's music drama.

Then everything came apart. The friendship with Wagner ended in mutual disgust, and Nietzsche's health collapsed into migraines and near-blindness; in 1879 he resigned his chair on a small pension. For ten years he lived as a wanderer between Sils-Maria, Genoa, Nice and Turin, writing in rented rooms the books for which he is known: *The Joyful Wisdom* (1882; fifth book 1887), *Thus Spake Zarathustra* (1883–85), *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), *The Genealogy of Morals* (1887), and, in 1888, *The Twilight of the Idols*, *The Antichrist* and *Ecce Homo*. Almost nobody read them. In January 1889 he collapsed in Turin (the story that he embraced a beaten horse is famous but poorly attested) and never recovered his mind. He died in Weimar in 1900, just as he was becoming famous.

### A warning about *The Will to Power*

Much of this chapter draws on *The Will to Power*, and you should know what that book is. It is *not* a book Nietzsche wrote. In the 1880s he planned a major work under that title and filled notebooks with drafts. He abandoned the plan in 1888. After his collapse, his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, who controlled his papers, selected and arranged notes from those notebooks with the help of Heinrich Köselitz ("Peter Gast"), and published them under the abandoned title (1901, expanded 1906). The Ludovici translation follows the 1906 edition. Elisabeth was a nationalist and antisemite, the widow of the antisemitic agitator Bernhard Förster; Nietzsche had despised Förster and his politics, and wrote contemptuously of antisemites. She later welcomed Mussolini and Hitler to the Nietzsche Archive in Weimar, and her editorial work helped to make her brother a prophet of German nationalism.

The notes are genuinely Nietzsche's and contain his most sustained reflections on nihilism. But treat the book as a notebook, not a treatise: the arrangement and numbering are the editors', some notes are drafts he rejected, and where a note conflicts with the published books, the books win.

## The madman

### The scene

The most famous passage in Nietzsche's writings is a page in *The Joyful Wisdom*. Read it whole:

> _The Madman._—Have you ever heard of the madman who on a bright morning lighted a lantern and ran to the market-place calling out unceasingly: "I seek God! I seek God!"—As there were many people standing about who did not believe in God, he caused a great deal of amusement. Why! is he lost? said one. Has he strayed away like a child? said another. Or does he keep himself hidden? Is he afraid of us? Has he taken a sea-voyage? Has he emigrated?—the people cried out laughingly, all in a hubbub. The insane man jumped into their midst and transfixed them with his glances. "Where is God gone?" he called out. "I mean to tell you! _We have killed him_,—you and I! We are all his murderers! But how have we done it? How were we able to drink up the sea? Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the whole horizon? What did we do when we loosened this earth from its sun? Whither does it now move? Whither do we move? Away from all suns? Do we not dash on unceasingly? Backwards, sideways, forwards, in all directions? Is there still an above and below? Do we not stray, as through infinite nothingness? Does not empty space breathe upon us? Has it not become colder? Does not night come on continually, darker and darker? Shall we not have to light lanterns in the morning? Do we not hear the noise of the grave-diggers who are burying God? Do we not smell the divine putrefaction?—for even Gods putrefy! God is dead! God remains dead! And we have killed him!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §125 (tr. Thomas Common)

The madman goes on: the deed is too great for us; "Shall we not ourselves have to become Gods, merely to seem worthy of it?" His listeners stare at him in silence. He throws his lantern to the ground, where it breaks and goes out, and says:

> "I come too early," he then said, "I am not yet at the right time. This prodigious event is still on its way, and is travelling,—it has not yet reached men's ears. Lightning and thunder need time, the light of the stars needs time, deeds need time, even after they are done, to be seen and heard. This deed is as yet further from them than the furthest star,—_and yet they have done it!_"
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §125 (tr. Thomas Common)

Finally, he goes into churches and sings a requiem for God, asking: "What are these churches now, if they are not the tombs and monuments of God?"

### Five things to notice

**1. The audience are atheists.** The madman does not preach to believers. The people in the market-place already "did not believe in God," and they laugh at him. This is the key to the parable. Nietzsche is not arguing that God does not exist; he takes that for granted, and so do his listeners. His target is the complacent unbeliever who thinks that the loss of God is a small matter, a change of opinion like giving up belief in ghosts, after which life goes on as before. The madman's message is that they have not understood what they have done.

**2. The lantern in daylight.** Educated readers would have recognized the allusion to Diogenes the Cynic, who, according to Diogenes Laertius, "Having lighted a candle in the day time, he said, 'I am looking for a man.'" Diogenes' lamp exposed the absence of real human beings among the citizens. The madman's lantern exposes the absence of God, and also the fact that, without the sun, we may soon need lanterns at noon.

**3. The images are cosmic, not moral.** Drinking up the sea, wiping away the horizon, unchaining the earth from its sun: these are images of losing *orientation*. "Is there still an above and below?" The sun, in the Platonic tradition, is the image of the Good, the source of light and life ([Philosophy, Chapter 5](../learn-philosophy/05-plato.md)). God, for the culture the madman addresses, was not just one item of belief. He was the fixed point by which everything else was measured: up and down, true and false, good and evil, meaningful and absurd. Remove the fixed point, and every measurement is in question. This is nihilism as *disorientation*, in Bernard Reginster's term ([Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md)).

**4. "We have killed him."** God did not simply fade away; he was killed, and by us. How? The parable does not say, but Nietzsche's answer, given elsewhere, is that Christianity killed its own God through the virtues it cultivated, above all truthfulness (see below). The phrase also implies responsibility. A murder is a deed, and a deed demands that the doer become equal to it: "Shall we not ourselves have to become Gods, merely to seem worthy of it?" That question points directly to [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md).

**5. "I come too early."** The death of God is an event whose consequences arrive later than the event itself, like the light of a dead star. People have stopped believing, but they have not yet felt what that means; they still live off the moral, political and emotional capital that belief accumulated. The madman is a prophet of a *delayed* catastrophe.

### God's shadow

Earlier in the same book, Nietzsche had already stated the problem in four sentences:

> _New Struggles._—After Buddha was dead people showed his shadow for centuries afterwards in a cave,—an immense frightful shadow. God is dead: but as the human race is constituted, there will perhaps be caves for millenniums yet, in which people will show his shadow,—And we—we have still to overcome his shadow!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §108 (tr. Thomas Common)

The cave recalls Plato's cave, where prisoners mistake shadows for reality. What are God's shadows? In the next section (§109) Nietzsche lists some: the belief that the world is a living organism, that it is a machine, that it has a purpose, that there are laws of nature in the sense of commands, that there are things that persist. More generally, God's shadows are all the beliefs that survive the death of God but only make sense if God is alive: that history has a direction, that the universe is rational, that there is one true morality binding on all rational beings, that truth is always good for us. Overcoming the shadow is harder than killing God, because the shadow does not look like religion.

### "The most important of more recent events"

Five years later, in the fifth book of *The Joyful Wisdom* (1887), Nietzsche returned to the theme in his own voice and spelled out what the madman meant:

> The most important of more recent events—that "God is dead," that the belief in the Christian God has become unworthy of belief—already begins to cast its first shadows over Europe.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §343 (tr. Thomas Common)

Note the definition: "God is dead" means that "the belief in the Christian God has become unworthy of belief." It is a claim about the *credibility* of a belief in a culture, not a metaphysical proof. The consequences, he says, have hardly begun to be understood: "what must all collapse now that this belief had been undermined,—because so much was built upon it, so much rested on it, and had become one with it: for example, our entire European morality." But the section ends not with gloom but with exhilaration:

> In fact, we philosophers and "free spirits" feel ourselves irradiated as by a new dawn by the report that the "old God is dead"; our hearts overflow with gratitude, astonishment, presentiment and expectation. At last the horizon seems open once more, granting even that it is not bright; our ships can at last put out to sea in face of every danger; every hazard is again permitted to the discerner; the sea, _our_ sea, again lies open before us; perhaps never before did such an "open sea" exist.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §343 (tr. Thomas Common)

This is the two-sidedness of Nietzsche's thought about nihilism in a single page. The same event that, for the madman, wipes away the horizon, opens it again for the free spirit. The sea that "we" drank up in §125 is "_our_ sea" in §343. Whether the death of God is catastrophe or liberation depends on who receives the news, and on what they are able to do with it.

## How we killed him: the will to truth

### Truthfulness turns against itself

Why did belief in God become "unworthy of belief"? Not, for Nietzsche, primarily because of Darwin or biblical criticism, though both played a part, but because of something inside Christianity itself. Christianity taught Europeans, for nearly two thousand years, to examine their consciences with ruthless honesty, to confess, to hate lies, to value truth above comfort. That training eventually turned on Christianity's own claims. Nietzsche puts the argument most clearly in the *Genealogy of Morals*:

> All great things go to ruin by reason of themselves, by reason of an act of self-dissolution: so wills the law of life, the law of necessary "self-mastery" even in the essence of life
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, Third Essay, §27 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

In this way, he continues, Christianity did "_go to ruin as a dogma_, through its own morality," and now it must go to ruin as a morality too:

> After Christian truthfulness has drawn one conclusion after the other, it finally draws its _strongest conclusion_, its conclusion against itself; this, however, happens, when it puts the question, "_what is the meaning of every will for truth?_"
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, Third Essay, §27 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

The structure of the argument is a kind of internal critique. A value system contains a value (truthfulness) which, applied consistently, undermines the system's other claims (that God exists, that the world has a moral order, that suffering is punishment or test). The scientist who refuses to believe without evidence is the heir of the monk who refused to tell a lie. Nietzsche quotes his own earlier formulation: the Christian conscience was "translated and sublimated into the scientific conscience."

### Science rests on faith

The argument has a second, more unsettling turn. If truthfulness has destroyed God, what supports truthfulness? In *The Joyful Wisdom* §344, titled "To what Extent even We are still Pious," Nietzsche asks why science values truth "at all costs." Not because truth is always useful: illusions are often useful too. The unconditional will to truth, he argues, is a *moral* commitment ("I will not deceive, not even myself"), and that commitment rests on a metaphysical faith:

> it is always a _metaphysical belief_ on which our belief in science rests,—and that even we knowing ones of to-day, the godless and anti-metaphysical, still take _our_ fire from the conflagration kindled by a belief a millennium old, the Christian belief, which was also the belief of Plato, that God is truth, that the truth is divine.... But what if this itself always becomes more untrustworthy, what if nothing any longer proves itself divine, except it be error, blindness, and falsehood;—what if God himself turns out to be our most persistent lie?
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §344 (tr. Thomas Common)

Here the knife cuts the hand that holds it. The atheist scientist thinks he has left religion behind, but his devotion to truth is God's shadow. Nietzsche elsewhere calls Christianity "Platonism for the 'people'" (*Beyond Good and Evil*, Preface): the belief in a true world of perfect, unchanging being, opposed to the deceptive world of change. Modern science, in its faith that truth is always worth having, still lives on that belief. This is the deepest layer of Nietzsche's diagnosis: nihilism is the *self-devaluation* of the highest values, and the will to truth is both the instrument and the last victim.

### Testing the argument

Is this a good argument? Two worries are worth pressing.

First, it can look like a **genetic fallacy**: inferring that a belief is false or unjustified from facts about its origin. Even if our commitment to truth descends historically from Christian asceticism, it does not follow that it is unjustified now. Many valuable practices have embarrassing ancestors. Nietzsche's defenders reply that he is not refuting truthfulness by its origin, but asking what *justifies* it once its original justification (that God is truth) is gone. That is a legitimate question.

Second, the argument seems to **undermine itself**. If the will to truth is a questionable value, why should we accept Nietzsche's *truthful* exposure of it? Nietzsche saw this; it is one reason he speaks of perspectives, experiments and masks rather than doctrines. A charitable reading is that he does not ask us to abandon truthfulness, but to stop treating it as unconditional and divine, and to ask what it is *for*, what kind of life it serves. Whether that position is stable is one of the live questions of Nietzsche scholarship.

## The anatomy of nihilism

### "The most gruesome of all guests"

In the notebooks of 1885–88, Nietzsche tried to analyze nihilism systematically. The first note in *The Will to Power* opens like a novel:

> Nihilism is at our door: whence comes this most gruesome of all guests to us?--To begin with, it is a mistake to point to "social evils," "physiological degeneration," or even to corruption as a cause of Nihilism.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, §1 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

(Kaufmann's translation has "this uncanniest of all guests"; the German is *unheimlichste*, literally "least homelike.") Nihilism, the note continues, is "the absolute repudiation of worth, purpose, desirability," and its cause is not suffering, poverty or decadence, which can be interpreted in many ways, but something more specific: "Nihilism harbours in the heart of Christian morals." Then the definition you met in [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md) and [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md):

> What does Nihilism mean?--_That the highest values are losing their value._ There is no bourne. There is no answer to the question: "to what purpose?"
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, §2 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Nietzsche's preface for the planned book presents the whole project as prophecy:

> What I am now going to relate is the history of the next two centuries. I shall describe what will happen, what must necessarily happen: _the triumph of Nihilism._
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, Preface, §2 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

### Morality as antidote, morality as poison

Why does nihilism grow from the heart of Christian morality? Nietzsche's answer has two steps. First, Christian morality was a protection against nihilism. It gave every individual an infinite value, explained suffering as meaningful, assured people that they could know absolute values, and so prevented them from despairing:

> In short: Morality was the great _antidote_ against practical and theoretical Nihilism.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, §4 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Second, the antidote contained its own poison. Among the virtues morality cultivated was truthfulness, and truthfulness eventually exposed the moral interpretation itself as a construction serving human needs:

> But among the forces reared by morality, there was _truthfulness_: this in the end turns against morality, exposes the _teleology_ of the latter, its interestedness, and now the _recognition_ of this lie so long incorporated, from which we despaired of ever freeing ourselves, acts just like a stimulus.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, §5 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

The result is an impossible position: "We have _ceased_ from attaching any worth to what we know, and we _dare_ not attach any more worth to that with which we would fain deceive ourselves--from this antagonism there results a process of dissolution." We cannot believe what we need to believe, and we cannot stop needing it.

### Three categories that failed

The most careful analysis is a long note (§12) on how nihilism arises "as a psychological condition." Nietzsche distinguishes three routes.

1. **Purpose.** We sought in everything that happens a goal that is not there, whether a moral world-order, the growth of love, universal happiness, or even universal nothingness. When we realize that "Becoming has been aiming at _nothing,_ and has achieved nothing," we lose courage.
2. **Unity.** We imagined a totality behind all events, a great whole (God, Nature, History, the State) in which the individual could lose himself and so feel valuable. "At bottom, man loses the belief in his own worth when no infinitely precious entity manifests itself through him."
3. **Truth or Being.** Having lost purpose and unity, we retreat to a "true world" beyond this world of change, a world of real being. When we see that this world was "devised only for the purpose of meeting certain psychological needs," we are left with only becoming, and a strange condition: "_this world is no longer endured, although no one wishes to disown it._"

The summary is precise:

> In short, the categories, "Purpose," "Unity," "Being," by means of which we had lent some worth to life, we have once more divorced from it--and the world now appears _worthless_ to us....
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, §12 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Then comes the crucial twist, in part B of the same note. If the world seems worthless because it fails to satisfy these categories, perhaps the fault lies in the categories, not the world:

> Result: _The belief_ in the categories of reason is the cause of Nihilism--we have measured the worth of the world according to categories _which can only be applied to a purely fictitious world._
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, §12 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

This is the key to Nietzsche's whole treatment of nihilism, and it is worth stating plainly. The nihilist says: "The world has no purpose, no unity, no ultimate truth, therefore it is worthless." Nietzsche replies: "The world has no purpose, no unity, no ultimate truth, therefore the standard by which you judged it worthless was the wrong standard." Nihilism is not the discovery that the world lacks value. It is the disappointment of a particular, impossible *demand* for value. Change the demand, and the conclusion no longer follows. (Compare Schopenhauer's verdict that life is bad because it never reaches painless peace, [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md).)

### How the true world became a fable

In *The Twilight of the Idols* (1888), Nietzsche told the same story as a six-stage comedy, "The History of an Error." It begins with Plato ("The true world, attainable to the sage, the pious man and the man of virtue"), passes through Christianity (the true world "promised" to the virtuous), Kant (unattainable, "but even as a thought, alone, it is a comfort, an obligation, a command") and positivism (unattained and therefore unknown), until the "true world" becomes "a useless idea that has become quite superfluous." Then the last stage:

> 6. We have suppressed the true world: what world survives? the apparent world perhaps?... Certainly not! _In abolishing the true world we have also abolished the world of appearance!_
>
> (Noon; the moment of the shortest shadows; the end of the longest error; mankind's zenith; _Incipit Zarathustra._)
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Twilight of the Idols*, "How the 'True World' Ultimately Became a Fable" (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

The point of the last stage is subtle. If there is no "true world," then this world is not "merely apparent," a shadow or copy of something better. It is simply *the* world, and it does not need to be measured against anything else. The disappointment of nihilism depended on the contrast between the two worlds; abolish the contrast, and the disappointment loses its ground. Noon, "the moment of the shortest shadows," is the moment when God's shadow is smallest. And it is where Zarathustra begins.

### Morality without God: the English way

A practical consequence follows. Many Victorians, such as the novelist George Eliot, lost their Christian faith and yet held on, often more fiercely than before, to Christian morality: duty, self-sacrifice, compassion. Nietzsche thought this was incoherent:

> They are rid of the Christian God and therefore think it all the more incumbent upon them to hold tight to Christian morality: this is an English way of reasoning.... When we renounce the Christian faith, we abandon all right to Christian morality.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Twilight of the Idols*, "Skirmishes in a War with the Age," §5 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Christian morality, he argues, "is a command, its origin is transcendental"; "it stands or falls with the belief in God." To keep it without God is to live in God's shadow.

This is a strong and contestable claim, and it is close to Ivan Karamazov's "everything is permitted" ([Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md)), though drawn with the opposite emotion. Ask yourself whether it is true. Philosophers since Plato's *Euthyphro* have argued that morality cannot *depend* on God's commands, because then it would be arbitrary; if God commands kindness because kindness is good, kindness is good independently of God ([Ethics, Chapter 4](../learn-ethics/04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md)). Secular ethical theories, from Hume's sentimentalism to Kant to utilitarianism to contractualism, all claim to ground morality without a God. Nietzsche's reply is that these theories are themselves Christian valuations in secular costume: they still assume the equal worth of every person, the badness of suffering, the virtue of self-denial. Whether that makes them unjustified, rather than merely historically indebted, is the genetic-fallacy question again. You will meet the contemporary form of the debate in [Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md).

## Passive and active nihilism

### Two responses to the same event

[Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md) introduced the distinction Nietzsche draws in *Will to Power* §22:

> A. Nihilism as a sign of _enhanced spiritual strength_: active Nihilism.
>
> B. Nihilism as a sign of the _collapse_ and _decline_ of spiritual _strength_: passive Nihilism.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, §22 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

The next note explains. Nihilism is "a _normal_ condition." It may be a sign that a person or culture has outgrown its old goals: the "convictions" that once expressed its needs no longer fit, because it has become stronger. Or it may be a sign of "_insufficient_ strength, to fix a goal, a 'wherefore,' and a faith for itself." Active nihilism reaches its maximum "as a powerful _destructive_ force": it tears down what no longer deserves belief, as the Russian nihilists of [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md) set out to do. Passive nihilism, by contrast, is "_weary_ Nihilism, which no longer attacks": when values disintegrate, "everything which is relieving, which heals, becalms, or stupefies, steps into the foreground under the cover of various _disguises,_ either religious, moral, political or æsthetic."

The categories help to sort the figures of earlier chapters. Bazarov, the bomb-throwers of Narodnaya Volya, and Stirner are active nihilists: they destroy. Schopenhauer's saint, who quiets the will, and the Underground Man, who lies on his sofa spiteful and inert, are passive. But note that *neither* form is, for Nietzsche, the goal. Active nihilism clears the ground; it does not build. Nietzsche sometimes calls the full journey "completed" nihilism: going all the way through the loss of the old values, refusing every half-measure and shadow, to the point where new values can be created. In the preface to the planned book he describes himself, with characteristic self-dramatization, as "the first perfect European Nihilist, who, however, has already outlived Nihilism in his own soul--who has out-grown, overcome, and dismissed it."

### Buddhism, pity, and the passive form

Nietzsche names Buddhism as "the most renowned form" of passive nihilism (§23), and calls Christianity and Buddhism alike "nihilistic religions." This needs the same care as Schopenhauer's reading in [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md). Nietzsche's knowledge of Buddhism came largely through Schopenhauer and the German Indology of his time, and his use of "nihilistic" here is a term of *diagnosis*: a religion that seeks peace, the end of craving, and release from rebirth expresses, he thinks, a weary or over-sensitive life that wants rest more than growth.

Yet even Nietzsche admired Buddhism. In *The Antichrist* he wrote:

> Buddhism is a hundred times more realistic than Christianity,—it is part of its constitutional heritage to be able to face problems objectively and coolly, it is the outcome of centuries of lasting philosophical activity. The concept "God" was already exploded when it appeared.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Antichrist*, §20 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

It speaks, he says, not of the "struggle with _sin_" but of the "struggle with _pain_," and stands "_Beyond Good and Evil_." So Nietzsche's "passive nihilism" is not the claim that Buddhists believe nothing matters; Buddhists plainly do not, and Nietzsche knew it. It is the claim that a whole way of life aimed at the cessation of suffering expresses a certain *exhaustion*. A Buddhist would reply that compassion (*karuṇā*) and the bodhisattva's vow to work for all beings are hardly signs of weariness, and that Nietzsche, like Schopenhauer, confused the extinction of craving with the extinction of life ([Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md), [Chapter 15](15-beyond-nihilism.md)).

The same diagnosis drives Nietzsche's notorious attack on pity (*Mitleid*), the virtue Schopenhauer had made the foundation of ethics. In *The Antichrist* §7 he writes that Schopenhauer "was right in this respect: by means of pity, life is denied and made _more worthy of denial,_—pity is the _praxis_ of Nihilism." This is one of Nietzsche's most troubling claims, and it has been misused. His point, at its most defensible, is psychological: a culture that takes *suffering* as the fundamental fact about life, and its relief as the highest good, has already judged life as something to be escaped. At its least defensible, the passage slides into a contempt for the weak that is hard to separate from cruelty. Read it critically, and set it beside the compassion ethics of [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md) and [Ethics, Chapter 15](../learn-ethics/15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md).

## The last man

### Despair is not the danger

If you expected Nietzsche's worst-case scenario to be mass despair, suicide waves or bomb-throwing, you would be wrong. The figure he feared most appears in the Prologue to *Thus Spake Zarathustra* (1883). Zarathustra, a prophet who has spent ten years alone in the mountains, comes down to share his wisdom. On the way, he meets an old hermit who still praises God in the forest, and as they part, Zarathustra says to himself: "Could it be possible! This old saint in the forest hath not yet heard of it, that GOD IS DEAD!" In the market-place he teaches the crowd about the Superman (*Übermensch*), and they laugh. So he tries another tack. He will tell them about "the most contemptible thing":

> Alas! there cometh the time when man will no longer launch the arrow of his longing beyond man—and the string of his bow will have unlearned to whizz!
>
> I tell you: one must still have chaos in one, to give birth to a dancing star. I tell you: ye have still chaos in you.
>
> Alas! There cometh the time when man will no longer give birth to any star. Alas! There cometh the time of the most despicable man, who can no longer despise himself.
>
> Lo! I show you THE LAST MAN.
>
> "What is love? What is creation? What is longing? What is a star?"—so asketh the last man and blinketh.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Prologue, §5 (tr. Thomas Common)

The portrait continues:

> The earth hath then become small, and on it there hoppeth the last man who maketh everything small. His species is ineradicable like that of the ground-flea; the last man liveth longest.
>
> "We have discovered happiness"—say the last men, and blink thereby.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Prologue, §5 (tr. Thomas Common)

The last men have left "the regions where it is hard to live; for they need warmth." They work, "for work is a pastime." They avoid both wealth and poverty, ruling and obeying, because "Both are too burdensome." They take "A little poison now and then: that maketh pleasant dreams. And much poison at last for a pleasant death." "No shepherd, and one herd! Every one wanteth the same; every one is equal: he who hath other sentiments goeth voluntarily into the madhouse." They have "their little pleasures for the day, and their little pleasures for the night, but they have a regard for health." And they are not unhappy. They have "discovered happiness."

The crowd's response is the bitterest joke in the book: "Give us this last man, O Zarathustra," they shout, "make us into these last men! Then will we make thee a present of the Superman!"

### Why the last man is a nihilist

The last man does not *say* that nothing matters. He would never be so dramatic. But he is the purest case of passive nihilism, because the question of value has simply stopped arising for him. "What is love? What is creation? What is longing? What is a star?" These are not skeptical questions; they are the questions of someone for whom the words have lost their sense. He has no goal beyond comfort, nothing he would sacrifice comfort for, and no capacity to "despise himself," that is, to feel the gap between what he is and what he might become. In the terms of [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md), he is not a theoretical nihilist but a practical one: his life enacts the absence of the highest values, without noticing.

Nietzsche's critics have accused him here of aristocratic snobbery: what is so terrible about a peaceful, healthy, comfortable society without great passions? Isn't that what most people, sensibly, want? Liberal democracy, the welfare state and modern medicine look a lot like the last man's world, and they have relieved enormous suffering. The objection deserves weight. But consider what the image is *for*. It asks whether a life can be fully human if nothing in it reaches beyond its own comfort: no love that risks anything, no work that matters more than the paycheck, no "longing." Many readers who reject Nietzsche's politics recognize the last man in themselves: the evening spent scrolling, the "little poison" of distraction, the shrug at any question too large for a meme. Contemporary writers on the attention economy, consumerism and "optimistic nihilism" return to the image again and again ([Chapter 14](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md)).

The alternative Zarathustra offers is not a return to God:

> The Superman is the meaning of the earth. Let your will say: The Superman SHALL BE the meaning of the earth!
>
> I conjure you, my brethren, REMAIN TRUE TO THE EARTH, and believe not those who speak unto you of superearthly hopes! Poisoners are they, whether they know it or not.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Prologue, §3 (tr. Thomas Common)

The last man and the Superman are the two possible outcomes of the death of God: humanity shrinking into comfortable smallness, or humanity creating new values out of its own resources, faithful to this earth rather than a "true world" beyond it. What the Superman is, and whether the idea survives scrutiny, is the business of [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md).

## The extremest form

One more note from the notebooks prepares the transition. Nietzsche asks us to imagine the thought of a world without purpose in its most terrible form:

> Let us imagine this thought in its worst form: existence, as it is, without either a purpose or a goal, but inevitably recurring, without an end in nonentity: "_Eternal Recurrence._"
>
> This is the extremest form of Nihilism: nothing (purposelessness) eternal!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, §55 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Mainländer and Hartmann ([Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md)) offered at least the consolation of an *ending*, the world process running down into rest. Eternal recurrence takes even that away: if time is infinite and the world finite, everything that has happened will happen again, endlessly, with no goal and no exit. This is nihilism's hardest test. And yet Nietzsche made this same thought the center of his *affirmation* of life. How the "extremest form of Nihilism" could become its overcoming is the central puzzle of the next chapter.

## Is Nietzsche a nihilist?

You can now answer a question that the popular image of Nietzsche gets badly wrong. Is Nietzsche a nihilist?

- **As an accusation**, the label has stuck since the 1890s: the philosopher with the hammer, who said God is dead and morality a lie. His Nazi appropriation (largely the work of his sister and of selective quotation) made the accusation political.
- **As a description of his diagnosis**, it is half right. Nietzsche thought nihilism was the inevitable fate of European culture, and that the old values were finished. He did not want to slow the process down by propping them up.
- **As a description of his position**, it is wrong. Nietzsche's lifelong project was the *overcoming* of nihilism. He regarded Christianity, Platonism and Schopenhauer's pessimism as themselves nihilistic, because they measured this world against a "true world" and found it wanting. His own aim was to affirm this world, without that measure.

Nietzsche turned the accusation around. Jacobi had called Fichte a nihilist for dissolving God into the self ([Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md)); Dostoevsky called the atheists nihilists ([Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md)). Nietzsche replied that the real nihilists were the believers, who had always said "No" to life in the name of another world. Atheism did not create nihilism; it revealed a nihilism that had been there all along. You do not have to accept that reversal. But after Nietzsche you cannot use "nihilist" as a simple insult for unbelievers without asking who, exactly, is denying what.

## Living it

1. **Find a shadow.** Nietzsche's "shadows of God" are beliefs that survive the loss of God but only make sense if God exists. Write down three of your own beliefs about value or meaning (for example, "everything happens for a reason," "history is moving toward justice," "every person has infinite worth," "the truth always helps"). For each, write two sentences: what would justify it if there were a God, and what justifies it if there is not. Mark any where you have no answer to the second question. You don't have to give the belief up. The aim is to know where it stands.
2. **A last-man audit.** For one week, keep a log of the "little poison" in your days: the distractions you reach for automatically to avoid boredom or discomfort. At the end of the week, pick one evening and replace the poison with an activity that has a "longing" in it, something difficult that you care about (practice, a long conversation, a hard book, making something). Note how the evening felt, during and afterwards.
3. **Read the madman aloud.** Read *Joyful Wisdom* §125 aloud, to yourself or a friend, and then answer in writing: if you are a believer, what would it take for this event to happen to you? If you are not, have you noticed its consequences in your own life, or are you, like the people in the market-place, still waiting for the thunder?

## Summary

- Nietzsche (1844–1900) turned "nihilism" from an accusation into a **diagnosis** of European culture. *The Will to Power* is a **posthumous compilation** of his notebooks, edited by his antisemitic sister; use it with care.
- In the parable of the **madman** (*Joyful Wisdom* §125), God is dead and "we have killed him." The listeners are unbelievers; the point is that they have not understood the event's consequences. "I come too early": the effects arrive after the cause.
- "God is dead" means that "the belief in the Christian God has become unworthy of belief" (§343), with the result that "our entire European morality" must collapse; but for free spirits the news is also an "open sea."
- God was killed by Christianity's own **will to truth**: "Christian truthfulness ... finally draws ... its conclusion against itself" (*Genealogy* III §27). Even science "take[s] _our_ fire" from the faith "that God is truth" (§344).
- Nihilism means "**the highest values are losing their value.**" Morality was the "antidote" to nihilism but bred the truthfulness that destroyed it. Nihilism arises when the categories of **purpose, unity and being** fail; but "_the belief_ in the categories of reason is the cause of Nihilism," since they fit only a fictitious world.
- *Twilight*'s "**How the 'True World' Ultimately Became a Fable**": abolishing the true world also abolishes the "merely apparent" one. Christian morality without God (the "**English way**") is, for Nietzsche, living in God's shadow.
- **Active nihilism** (a sign of strength, destructive) vs **passive nihilism** (weary, seeks what "becalms, or stupefies"). Nietzsche calls Buddhism passive nihilism but also "a hundred times more realistic than Christianity"; **Buddhism is not nihilism** in the sense of "nothing matters."
- The **last man** ("We have discovered happiness") is the nightmare outcome: comfortable, small, unable to despise himself. The alternative: "REMAIN TRUE TO THE EARTH."
- Eternal recurrence is "**the extremest form of Nihilism**," and the hinge to [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md).
- Nietzsche is a **diagnostician** and would-be **overcomer** of nihilism, not its advocate; he reverses the accusation onto the believers.

**The madman:** Obvious! It seemed obvious to almost no one in Athens or Rome. It became obvious because for two thousand years people were taught that every soul is equal before God. You have kept the furniture and burned the house.

**You:** So your point isn't that I'm wrong to be decent. It's that I haven't done the work of finding out why.

**The madman:** My point is that the thunder hasn't reached you yet. When it does, you will either build something, or you will shrug and turn on the television. I came too early.

### Dialogue 2 — Zarathustra and the last man

**Zarathustra:** You look content. Tell me what you want from your life.

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Zarathustra in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Notice whether anything in your answer would be worth discomfort.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Honestly? A stable job, a nice apartment, good friends, some travel, and not too much stress. Is that so bad?

**Zarathustra:** Not bad. Warm. And what would you give up the apartment for?

**You:** I don't know. Nothing, if I could help it.

**Zarathustra:** Is there anything you love so much that you would risk being uncomfortable for it?

**You:** My friends, I guess. If one of them needed me, I'd show up, even if it was a hassle.

**Zarathustra:** There! That is not the last man speaking. The last man says: "One still loveth one's neighbour and rubbeth against him; for one needeth warmth." He loves for the warmth. You would love even when it costs you warmth.

**You:** That seems like a low bar.

**Zarathustra:** It is the first step on a rope over an abyss. Now: is there anything you are *making*, something that does not exist yet and will not exist unless you bring it about?

**You:** Not really. I used to write music. I stopped when it got hard.

**Zarathustra:** "One must still have chaos in one, to give birth to a dancing star." You still have the chaos. You have only covered it with a nice apartment.

**You:** You make comfort sound like a crime. Most people in history would have killed for my apartment.

**Zarathustra:** I don't despise comfort. I despise the man who has nothing he would trade it for. You have your friends, and you had your music. Keep the apartment. Pick up the music.

**You:** And if I'm not good at it?

**Zarathustra:** Then you will despise yourself a little. Good. The last man can no longer despise himself. That is precisely what makes him the last.

# Chapter 7 — Nietzsche Beyond Nihilism

[Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) left Nietzsche at the edge of the abyss. God is dead; the highest values are devaluing themselves; the measure by which Europe judged the world (purpose, unity, a "true world") turns out to fit only a fiction; and the most likely outcome is the comfortable "last man" who has "discovered happiness." This chapter follows Nietzsche's attempt to go *through* nihilism and out the other side. He gave his answer several names: the *revaluation of all values*; *eternal recurrence*, the thought he called "the highest formula of a Yea-saying to life that can ever be attained"; *amor fati*, love of fate; and the *Übermensch* or "Superman," the human being who could create values and bear the thought of recurrence. Behind all of these stands his *genealogy of morals*, the historical inquiry that was meant to show that our values have origins and therefore could be otherwise. The chapter sets out each idea, reads the key texts closely, and then asks the hard questions. Can values be *created* without becoming arbitrary? Does affirming everything mean affirming atrocity? Is the Superman an aristocratic fantasy? And did Nietzsche overcome nihilism, or, as Heidegger argued, complete it? Previous: [Chapter 6 — Nietzsche: The Death of God](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md). Next: [Chapter 8 — Kierkegaard and Despair](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md).

## The problem restated: suffering without a meaning

The *Genealogy of Morals* ends with a diagnosis that gathers up everything in [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) and states the problem Nietzsche's positive philosophy has to solve:

> If you except the ascetic ideal, man, the animal man had no meaning. His existence on earth contained no end; "What is the purpose of man at all?" was a question without an answer; the _will_ for man and the world was lacking; behind every great human destiny rang as a refrain a still greater "Vanity!"
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, Third Essay, §28 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

Note the echo of Ecclesiastes ([Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md)): "Vanity!" The human animal, Nietzsche goes on, can bear almost any suffering, but not *meaningless* suffering:

> _Not_ suffering, but the senselessness of suffering was the curse which till then lay spread over humanity--_and the ascetic ideal gave it a meaning!_ It was up till then the only meaning; but any meaning is better than no meaning
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, Third Essay, §28 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

The "ascetic ideal" is Nietzsche's name for the family of values that interpret suffering as guilt and promise redemption through self-denial: the priest's morality, the saint's, Schopenhauer's. With it, "the door to all suicidal Nihilism was closed." But at a price. The ascetic ideal is itself "a will for Nothingness, a will opposed to life," a hatred of the senses, of change, of the body. The book ends:

> and to say at the end that which I said at the beginning--man will wish _Nothingness_ rather than not wish _at all_.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, Third Essay, §28 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

So there are two nihilisms in play. The first is the *hidden* nihilism of the old values, which saved the will by turning it against life. The second is the *open* nihilism that follows when those values collapse, leaving suffering meaningless again. Nietzsche's question is whether there can be a meaning for human life and suffering that is *not* a will to nothingness: a way of saying Yes to this world, without a "true world" behind it. Everything in this chapter is an attempt to answer that question.

## Genealogy: values have a history

### The value of values

The first step is to loosen the grip of the old values by showing that they have a *history*. If values were eternal truths, discovered like mathematical theorems, nothing could be done about them. If they were made, by particular people for particular reasons, they can be questioned and remade. The preface to the *Genealogy* states the program:

> Let us speak out this _new demand_: we need a _critique_ of moral values, _the value of these values_ is for the first time to be called into question
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, Preface, §6 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

Note the precise question. Not "Which actions are good?" but "What is the value of our *values*? What kind of life do they express and promote?" A moral code is treated like a symptom, to be read for what it reveals about the health of those who hold it.

### Good and bad, good and evil

The First Essay offers a speculative history of two kinds of morality (developed also in *Beyond Good and Evil* §260; see [Ethics, Chapter 15](../learn-ethics/15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md) for a fuller treatment).

*Master morality* begins with self-affirmation. The strong, healthy, noble types of the ancient world call *themselves* "good," meaning noble, powerful, happy, beautiful; "bad" is an afterthought, meaning low, common, unfortunate. *Slave morality* begins with a No. The weak and oppressed, unable to act against their masters, take revenge in imagination: they call the masters "evil," and then define themselves as "good" by contrast, meaning meek, humble, patient. The engine of this inversion is *ressentiment* (Samuel's translation renders it "resentment"):

> The revolt of the slaves in morals begins in the very principle of _resentment_ becoming creative and giving birth to values--a resentment experienced by creatures who, deprived as they are of the proper outlet of action, are forced to find their compensation in an imaginary revenge. While every aristocratic morality springs from a triumphant affirmation of its own demands, the slave morality says "no" from the very outset to what is "outside itself," "different from itself," and "not itself": and this "no" is its creative deed.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, First Essay, §10 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

Nietzsche's most famous image for the process is a fable:

> It is not surprising that the lambs should bear a grudge against the great birds of prey, but that is no reason for blaming the great birds of prey for taking the little lambs. And when the lambs say among themselves, "These birds of prey are evil, and he who is as far removed from being a bird of prey, who is rather its opposite, a lamb,--is he not good?" then there is nothing to cavil at in the setting up of this ideal, though it may also be that the birds of prey will regard it a little sneeringly, and perchance say to themselves, "_We_ bear no grudge against them, these good lambs, we even like them: nothing is tastier than a tender lamb."
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, First Essay, §13 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

The trick of slave morality, Nietzsche argues, is to invent a "subject" behind the deed, a free agent who *could* have refrained, so that the bird of prey can be blamed for being a bird of prey, and the lamb's weakness praised as a free choice of meekness.

The Second Essay adds a history of *bad conscience*: when human beings were first confined within society, the aggressive instincts that could no longer discharge outward turned inward, against the self, producing guilt, self-torment and the "inner life." Religion then gave this self-torment a cosmic meaning (we are guilty before God). The Third Essay, as you have seen, shows the ascetic ideal harnessing it.

### How to read the genealogy

Three cautions are in order.

**It is not history in the ordinary sense.** Nietzsche cites some etymologies and a few historical facts, but the *Genealogy* is a philosophical reconstruction, a "just-so story" in places. Its value lies in the questions it teaches you to ask (whom does this value serve? what feeling does it express?) more than in its claims about what happened in antiquity.

**It is not a simple call to become a bird of prey.** Nietzsche is often read as celebrating the masters and despising the slaves. But he also says that slave morality made human beings "interesting": the inward turn created depth, self-reflection, spirit. Without it, there would be no philosophy, including his own. His point is not to go back to the masters but to go beyond both.

**Its treatment of Judaism needs a critical eye.** In §7 of the First Essay, Nietzsche identifies the priestly people of ancient Israel as the originators of the slave revolt in morals. He despised the antisemites of his own day, broke with his publisher and quarreled with his sister over them, and wrote admiringly of Jewish contributions to European culture. Yet the passage repeats stereotyped generalizations about a whole people, and it was later quoted by people who shared none of his contempt for antisemitism. Read it as a historical claim to be tested, and note how easily a "genealogy" of values can become a genealogy of peoples.

**It raises the genetic fallacy again.** Even if Christian morality began in *ressentiment*, it does not follow that its values (compassion, equality, the protection of the weak) are bad. Nietzsche's defenders answer that he is not arguing from origin to falsehood, but using origin to break the spell of self-evidence; once you see that a value was *made*, you can ask whether it still serves life. That question still has to be answered on its merits.

## The revaluation of all values

### Not inversion, but a new standard

"Revaluation" (*Umwertung*; older translations, including Ludovici's, say "transvaluation") is the name Nietzsche gave to his project in his last years. In *Ecce Homo* he wrote, with the grandiosity of his final months:

> I know my destiny. There will come a day when my name will recall the memory of something formidable--a crisis the like of which has never been known on earth, the memory of the most profound clash of consciences, and the passing of a sentence upon all that which theretofore had been believed, exacted, and hallowed. I am not a man, I am dynamite.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Ecce Homo*, "Why I Am a Fatality," §1 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Revaluation does not mean simply turning the old values upside down: calling cruelty good and kindness bad. It means *re-evaluating* the old values by a new standard. The old standard was the "true world," God, the ideal of a life without suffering or change; by that standard, *this* life was found wanting. The new standard is life itself: does a value express and enhance life, strength, growth, creativity, or does it express exhaustion, revenge against life, a wish for nothingness? Nietzsche thought that by this standard many Christian virtues (pity, humility, self-denial) would be found to express weariness, while some things Christianity called sins (pride, sensuality, the desire for distinction) would be found to express health. But the new standard could also endorse some old values, reinterpreted: honesty, for example, survives revaluation, as long as it stops being worshipped as divine.

### Valuing is creating

Where do new values come from? Zarathustra's answer, in "The Thousand and One Goals," is that they have always come from human beings. Every people has hung a "table of excellencies" over itself, the expression of its needs and its will; none found its values "as a voice from heaven":

> Verily, men have given unto themselves all their good and bad. Verily, they took it not, they found it not, it came not unto them as a voice from heaven.
>
> Values did man only assign to things in order to maintain himself—he created only the significance of things, a human significance! Therefore, calleth he himself "man," that is, the valuator.
>
> Valuing is creating: hear it, ye creating ones! Valuation itself is the treasure and jewel of the valued things.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "The Thousand and One Goals" (tr. Thomas Common)

The wordplay rests on an etymology Nietzsche spells out in the *Genealogy*: "Perhaps our word 'Mensch' (*manas*) still expresses just something of *this* self-pride: man denoted himself as the being who measures values" (Second Essay, §8, tr. Horace B. Samuel). He is linking *Mensch* to the Sanskrit *manas*, "mind," and through it to measuring; the etymology is speculative and no historical linguist would sign it, but it shows what he wanted the word "man" to mean. This is the decisive turn. The nihilist reasoned: values are not found in the world, so there are no values. Nietzsche reasons: values were never found in the world, they were always made, so the collapse of the belief that they were found does not destroy them; it reveals *us* as their makers. The death of God is not the end of value, but the end of the illusion that value came from outside.

### The camel, the lion and the child

Zarathustra's first speech, "The Three Metamorphoses," gives the stages of this transformation as a parable:

> Three metamorphoses of the spirit do I designate to you: how the spirit becometh a camel, the camel a lion, and the lion at last a child.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "The Three Metamorphoses" (tr. Thomas Common)

The *camel* is the reverent, load-bearing spirit that kneels and asks to be laden with the heaviest duties, and carries them into the desert. There, in the loneliest wilderness, it becomes a *lion*, which fights the great dragon whose name is "Thou shalt":

> The values of a thousand years glitter on those scales, and thus speaketh the mightiest of all dragons: "All the values of things—glitter on me.
>
> All values have already been created, and all created values—do I represent. Verily, there shall be no 'I will' any more." Thus speaketh the dragon.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "The Three Metamorphoses" (tr. Thomas Common)

The lion says "I will" and defeats the dragon. But the lion cannot create:

> To create new values—that, even the lion cannot yet accomplish: but to create itself freedom for new creating—that can the might of the lion do.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "The Three Metamorphoses" (tr. Thomas Common)

So the lion must become a *child*:

> Innocence is the child, and forgetfulness, a new beginning, a game, a self-rolling wheel, a first movement, a holy Yea.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "The Three Metamorphoses" (tr. Thomas Common)

Map this onto the vocabulary of [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md). The camel is the believer, who takes on the old values with reverence. The lion is the *active nihilist*: Bazarov, Stirner, the critic who destroys the "Thou shalt." The lion is necessary, but it is not the goal; a life of pure negation is still defined by what it negates. The child is the creator, who plays, begins again, and says a "holy Yea" without needing an enemy. Many people get stuck as lions. The parable says that nihilism, even heroic nihilism, is a stage to pass through.

### The hard question: arbitrary values?

Here is the most serious objection to Nietzsche's positive philosophy. If values are created rather than discovered, what makes one set of created values better than another? If nothing, then the revaluation is arbitrary, and we are back in nihilism with extra steps. If something, then there is a standard of value that is not created, and Nietzsche has quietly reintroduced the objective values he denied.

Nietzsche's answer, as far as he gives one, is that the standard is *life*, specifically life as "will to power": the drive of every living thing not merely to survive but to grow, to expand, to overcome resistance, to express its strength. Values are better or worse according to whether they serve "ascending" or "declining" life. Critics reply that this is either a disguised objective value (life-enhancement is *really* good), in which case Nietzsche is no anti-realist; or it is just Nietzsche's own preference, in which case why should anyone share it? Scholars divide here. Some (Brian Leiter) read Nietzsche as a kind of naturalist who thinks his preferred values are the expression of a certain type of person and makes no claim that others are bound by them. Others (Bernard Reginster) argue that the will to power is a structural fact about valuing as such (to will is to seek resistance to overcome), and that it yields a non-arbitrary standard for evaluating values. Still others think the tension is unresolved. Nietzsche himself seems to accept a degree of the arbitrariness. In *The Joyful Wisdom* §290 he praises the art of "giving style" to one's character, and remarks that "whether the taste was good or bad is of less importance than one thinks,—it is sufficient that it was _a taste_!"

You do not have to resolve this to see what is at stake. It is the same problem that [Chapter 11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md) will meet in Sartre, for whom we invent our values and [Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) in contemporary metaethics: once you deny that values are part of the furniture of the universe, you owe an account of why some are better than others.

## Eternal recurrence

### The heaviest burden

In August 1881, walking beside the lake of Silvaplana in the Swiss Alps, Nietzsche stopped beside a large pyramid-shaped rock near Surlei, and the thought struck him that he would later call the center of his philosophy. He described the moment in *Ecce Homo*:

> The fundamental idea of the work, the _Eternal Recurrence,_ the highest formula of a Yea-saying to life that can ever be attained, was first conceived in the month of August 1881. I made a note of the idea on a sheet of paper, with the postscript: "Six thousand feet beyond man and time."
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Ecce Homo*, "Thus Spake Zarathustra," §1 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Its first published statement, at the end of the fourth book of *The Joyful Wisdom* (1882), is one of the most famous thought experiments in philosophy:

> _The Heaviest Burden._—What if a demon crept after thee into thy loneliest loneliness some day or night, and said to thee: "This life, as thou livest it at present, and hast lived it, thou must live it once more, and also innumerable times; and there will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and every sigh, and all the unspeakably small and great in thy life must come to thee again, and all in the same series and sequence—and similarly this spider and this moonlight among the trees, and similarly this moment, and I myself. The eternal sand-glass of existence will ever be turned once more, and thou with it, thou speck of dust!"—Wouldst thou not throw thyself down and gnash thy teeth, and curse the demon that so spake? Or hast thou once experienced a tremendous moment in which thou wouldst answer him: "Thou art a God, and never did I hear aught more divine!"
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §341 (tr. Thomas Common)

And then the practical point:

> If that thought acquired power over thee, as thou art, it would transform thee, and perhaps crush thee; the question with regard to all and everything: "Dost thou want this once more, and also for innumerable times?" would lie as the heaviest burden upon thy activity! Or, how wouldst thou have to become favourably inclined to thyself and to life, so as _to long for nothing more ardently_ than for this last eternal sanctioning and sealing?
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §341 (tr. Thomas Common)

### From the extremest nihilism to the highest affirmation

Recall the note from [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md): eternal recurrence is "the extremest form of Nihilism: nothing (purposelessness) eternal!" (*Will to Power* §55). Now it is "the highest formula of a Yea-saying to life." How can the same thought be both?

Because it is a *test*, and everything depends on how you respond. For someone who measures life by a goal outside it (heaven, progress, the final victory of justice, or Hartmann's peaceful end of the world process), recurrence is the worst news possible: there is no goal, no end, no exit, and every pain will return forever. That is nihilism at its most extreme. But for someone who can say Yes to it, recurrence becomes a way of giving every moment *infinite* weight without any appeal to another world. If this moment will return eternally, then it is not a means to some later end; it is itself eternal. The "true world" that the old metaphysics placed beyond time is, so to speak, folded back into time. Recurrence does to eternity what the death of God did to value: it brings it down to earth.

Compare the Leopardi dialogue from [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md). The almanac seller would not live his life again, and the passer-by concluded that "each person is of opinion that the evil he has experienced exceeds the good." Leopardi treats the question as a sum: would the pleasures outweigh the pains? Nietzsche transforms it. The question is not whether your life adds up to a profit, but whether you can *will* it, as a whole, with its pains, because they belong to it. In *Beyond Good and Evil* he contrasts the "most world-renouncing of all possible modes of thought," Schopenhauer's and the Buddha's, with:

> the ideal of the most world-approving, exuberant, and vivacious man, who has not only learnt to compromise and arrange with that which was and is, but wishes to have it again AS IT WAS AND IS, for all eternity, insatiably calling out da capo, not only to himself, but to the whole piece and play
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil*, §56 (tr. Helen Zimmern)

*Da capo* is the musical instruction "from the top." The pessimist at the end of the performance asks for his money back; the affirmer shouts for the whole piece again.

### The gateway called "This Moment"

In *Zarathustra*, recurrence appears as a vision. In "The Vision and the Enigma," Zarathustra climbs a mountain path carrying a dwarf, the "spirit of gravity," on his shoulders. They stop at a gateway:

> "Look at this gateway! Dwarf!" I continued, "it hath two faces. Two roads come together here: these hath no one yet gone to the end of.
>
> This long lane backwards: it continueth for an eternity. And that long lane forward—that is another eternity.
>
> They are antithetical to one another, these roads; they directly abut on one another:—and it is here, at this gateway, that they come together. The name of the gateway is inscribed above: 'This Moment.'
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "The Vision and the Enigma" (tr. Thomas Common)

The dwarf answers glibly: "All truth is crooked; time itself is a circle." Zarathustra is angry, because the dwarf has made the thought *easy*. Said lightly, eternal recurrence is a piece of cosmology; felt seriously, it is a weight that falls on "This Moment," right now, and on every choice made in it.

Later in the book, the "ugliest man," who had murdered God because he could not bear to be seen, says after a single day's festival with Zarathustra: "'Was THAT—life?' will I say unto death. 'Well! Once more!'" And Zarathustra's "Drunken Song" gives recurrence its lyrical form:

> Said ye ever Yea to one joy? O my friends, then said ye Yea also unto ALL woe. All things are enlinked, enlaced and enamoured,—
>
> —Wanted ye ever once to come twice; said ye ever: "Thou pleasest me, happiness! Instant! Moment!" then wanted ye ALL to come back again!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "The Drunken Song," §10 (tr. Thomas Common)

The argument hidden in the poem is worth spelling out. If all things are "enlinked," you cannot have the joy without the woe that led to it. To say Yes to one moment wholeheartedly is to say Yes to the whole chain of causes that produced it, which is to say Yes to everything.

### Is it true? Does it matter?

Did Nietzsche believe that the universe *literally* repeats itself? In his notebooks (*Will to Power* §1066) he sketched a proof: if the universe is a finite quantity of energy in infinite time, "the universe must go through a calculable number of combinations," so every combination must recur infinitely often. The argument has well-known problems. A finite system need not pass through all its possible states, or return to any of them: the philosopher and sociologist Georg Simmel, in *Schopenhauer and Nietzsche* (1907), imagined three wheels of equal size on one axis, each with a mark, the marks first lined up; turn them at speeds *n*, *2n* and *n/π*, and the marks will never line up again, however long the wheels turn (Walter Kaufmann made the objection well known in English). And modern cosmology, with its expanding universe and its thermodynamic arrow of time, gives the argument no support. Nietzsche never published the proof.

Most interpreters today read eternal recurrence primarily as a *practical* or *ethical* thought: a test of one's attitude toward one's life, not a hypothesis in physics. Nehamas reads it as a demand that your life form a coherent whole, like a well-made novel in which no episode could be removed without changing the character. On this reading it does not matter whether recurrence is true. What matters is the question: *could you will it?* The answer reveals whether you are living in a way you can fully stand behind, or in a way that you endure, postpone or apologize for.

There are objections even to the practical reading. Must you will the *exact* repetition of your worst mistakes, or of the suffering of others? A torture victim, or a parent who has lost a child, may find the demand obscene. Nietzsche's answer would be that refusing recurrence because of such events still measures life by an ideal of a world without them, the old "true world." That reply will satisfy some readers and not others. Notice also that the demon's question is addressed to *you*, about *your* life. It does not ask you to approve of every event as morally good, but to affirm your life as a whole, and the world without which it could not have been yours.

## Amor fati

### "Let that henceforth be my love"

The attitude that passes the test of recurrence has a Latin name. On New Year's Day 1882 (as the fourth book of *The Joyful Wisdom* imagines it) Nietzsche wrote:

> I want more and more to perceive the necessary characters in things as the beautiful:—I shall thus be one of those who beautify things. _Amor fati_: let that henceforth be my love! I do not want to wage war with the ugly. I do not want to accuse, I do not want even to accuse the accusers. _Looking aside_, let that be my sole negation! And all in all, to sum up: I wish to be at any time hereafter only a yea-sayer!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §276 (tr. Thomas Common)

And in *Ecce Homo*, six years later:

> My formula for greatness in man is! _amor fati_: the fact that a man wishes nothing to be different, either in front of him or behind him, or for all eternity. Not only must the necessary be borne, and on no account concealed,--all idealism is falsehood in the face of necessity,--but it must also be _loved...._
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Ecce Homo*, "Why I Am So Clever," §10 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

(The exclamation mark after "is" is in Ludovici's text.) *Amor fati* goes beyond Stoic acceptance. The Stoics taught that one should accept what one cannot control, and Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus have passages that come close to love of fate ([Stoicism, Chapter 10](../learn-stoicism/10-epictetus.md)). But Stoic acceptance rests on the belief that the cosmos is rational and providential: what happens is for the best because Zeus, or *logos*, arranges it. Nietzsche's love of fate has no such guarantee. It loves a necessity that is blind, purposeless and indifferent, which is to say, it loves the world as Schopenhauer described it, and draws the opposite conclusion.

### Redemption from "It was"

In Zarathustra's speech "Redemption," the psychology behind *amor fati* comes into focus. The will, Zarathustra says, can do many things, but it cannot will backwards. It cannot change the past, and this is its "lonesomest tribulation":

> "It was": thus is the Will's teeth-gnashing and lonesomest tribulation called. Impotent towards what hath been done—it is a malicious spectator of all that is past.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "Redemption" (tr. Thomas Common)

From this impotence, Nietzsche argues, comes the *spirit of revenge*: the will takes out its frustration on everything that suffers, invents "penalty," and finally condemns existence itself as guilt. "This, yea, this alone is REVENGE itself: the Will's antipathy to time, and its 'It was.'" Zarathustra even names the Schopenhauerian solution, only to reject it as a "fabulous song of madness": "Unless the Will should at last deliver itself, and Willing become non-Willing." Denial of the will ([Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md)) is the last revenge against time. Zarathustra's alternative:

> To redeem what is past, and to transform every "It was" into "Thus would I have it!"—that only do I call redemption!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "Redemption" (tr. Thomas Common)

This is *amor fati* as a task: to take up the past, including its accidents and wounds, as material for a self one can affirm. Nietzsche's model is artistic. "All 'It was' is a fragment, a riddle, a fearful chance—until the creating Will saith thereto: 'But thus would I have it.'" A novelist does not regret the obstacles in the plot; they are what make the story. Similarly, *The Joyful Wisdom* §290 describes how to "give style" to one's character: the artist of the self surveys everything in their nature, strong and weak, "and then fashions it into an ingenious plan, until everything appears artistic and rational, and even the weaknesses enchant the eye." Nietzsche's own life (illness, loneliness, failure) was the material he worked on. The subtitle of *Ecce Homo* is "How One Becomes What One Is."

### Does amor fati excuse everything?

A natural worry: if you love fate, and wish "nothing to be different," you cannot protest against injustice. *Amor fati* would make Ivan Karamazov's rebellion ([Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md)) impossible, and it might seem to excuse the powerful who cause suffering. Three replies are possible. First, *amor fati* concerns the *past* and the *necessary*; it does not forbid acting to change the future, and Nietzsche's own life was a campaign against what he found contemptible. Second, the passage in §276 does not renounce judgment; it renounces *accusation*, the resentful stance that blames the world for being as it is. One can work against suffering without hating existence for containing it. Third, and more honestly, there is a real tension, and many readers think Nietzsche does not resolve it. Camus, who learned much from him, rejected *amor fati* in favor of *revolt*: lucid acceptance that the world is absurd, combined with a refusal to consent to it ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)).

## The Übermensch

### A rope over an abyss

The figure who could create values and love fate is the *Übermensch*, translated by Common as "Superman" and by others as "overman" or "overhuman." He is introduced in Zarathustra's first public speech, in the Prologue:

> I TEACH YOU THE SUPERMAN. Man is something that is to be surpassed. What have ye done to surpass man?
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Prologue, §3 (tr. Thomas Common)

And in the next section:

> Man is a rope stretched between the animal and the Superman—a rope over an abyss.
>
> A dangerous crossing, a dangerous wayfaring, a dangerous looking-back, a dangerous trembling and halting.
>
> What is great in man is that he is a bridge and not a goal: what is lovable in man is that he is an OVER-GOING and a DOWN-GOING.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Prologue, §4 (tr. Thomas Common)

The Superman "is the meaning of the earth" (§3). He is what humanity might aim at once it gives up aiming at heaven: not a God beyond the world, but a higher human being within it. The "last man" ([Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)) is his opposite, the human being who has stopped trying to surpass himself.

### What the Superman is not

The idea has been badly abused, and Nietzsche himself complained that it was misunderstood. In *Ecce Homo* he rejected the readings already current in his lifetime:

> The word "Superman," which designates a type of man that would be one of nature's rarest and luckiest strokes, as opposed to "modern" men, to "good" men, to Christians and other Nihilists
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Ecce Homo*, "Why I Write Such Excellent Books," §1 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

He goes on to mock those who read the word as an "'ideal' type," half saint and half genius, or "suspected me of Darwinism," or found in it Carlyle's "hero cult." Notice that he counts Christians among the "Nihilists": the Superman is opposed to nihilism in Nietzsche's sense, the will to nothingness behind the old values.

So the Superman is *not*:

- **A biological species** produced by evolution or breeding. Nietzsche rejected "Darwinism," and the Superman is a cultural and spiritual ideal, not a eugenic program, despite later Nazi propaganda.
- **A racial or national type.** Nietzsche despised German nationalism and antisemitism; his models of greatness were individuals from many peoples.
- **A tyrant who does what he likes.** The Superman's distinguishing mark is self-mastery: the ability to give his life a "style," to create values and hold himself to them.

What is he, then? Nietzsche never describes the Superman in detail, and the vagueness is partly deliberate: an ideal of *self-overcoming* cannot have a fixed content. His closest historical model was Goethe, whom he described in *The Twilight of the Idols*:

> Such a spirit, _become free_, appears in the middle of the universe with a feeling of cheerful and confident fatalism; he believes that only individual things are bad, and that as a whole the universe justifies and, affirms itself—_He no longer denies_.... But such a faith is the highest Of all faiths: I christened it with the name of Dionysus.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Twilight of the Idols*, "Skirmishes in a War with the Age," §49 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

(The stray comma after "and" and the capital "Of" are in Ludovici's text.) Goethe, Nietzsche says, "_created_ himself": disciplined his passions without killing them, integrated reason and sensuality, and affirmed the world as a whole. That is the ideal: not a conqueror, but a person whole enough to say Yes.

### The elitism problem

Even purged of Nazi distortions, Nietzsche's ideal is unashamedly aristocratic. He thought only rare individuals could achieve it, and he sometimes suggested that the purpose of a culture was to produce them, at the expense of the many. He opposed democracy, socialism, feminism and the equality of rights as expressions of slave morality. A reader today can respond in several ways. Some (Walter Kaufmann) emphasize the individualist, existential Nietzsche and set aside the politics as peripheral. Others (Bertrand Russell, for one) judge the politics central and the philosophy tainted. Still others try to "democratize" the ideal: every person can practice self-overcoming and the affirmation of their own life, whatever their station. That last move goes beyond what Nietzsche wrote, but it is how most contemporary readers who find him liberating actually use him. Consider whether the core ideas (values are made, life should be affirmed, become what you are) survive without the aristocratic frame.

## Tragic affirmation

The ideas of this chapter come together in Nietzsche's concept of the *tragic* and the *Dionysian*, which reaches back to his first book. Schopenhauer had argued that Greek tragedy teaches resignation: the hero's fall shows that life is not worth living. Nietzsche, in *The Twilight of the Idols*, reverses this:

> Tragedy is so far from proving anything in regard to the pessimism of the Greeks, as Schopenhauer maintains, that it ought rather to be considered as the categorical repudiation and _condemnation_ thereof. The saying of Yea to life, including even its most strange and most terrible problems, the will to life rejoicing over its own inexhaustibleness in the _sacrifice_ of its highest types—this is what I called Dionysian
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Twilight of the Idols*, "Things I Owe to the Ancients," §5 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Here is the final contrast with pessimism. Schopenhauer and the pessimists of [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md) looked at suffering and concluded that life ought not to be. Nietzsche looked at the same suffering and concluded that a strong enough life could say Yes to it, not because suffering is secretly good, or will be compensated in another world, but because it belongs to a life that is worth affirming as a whole. "The tragic artist is no pessimist," he wrote in the same book, "he says _Yea_ to everything questionable and terrible, he is Dionysian."

## Did Nietzsche overcome nihilism?

The question has no settled answer, and you should leave this chapter with a view of your own. Here are the main positions.

**Yes, in principle.** On this reading (Kaufmann, Reginster), Nietzsche shows how value is possible after the death of God: values are created; the creation is not arbitrary because it can be judged by whether it affirms life; eternal recurrence and *amor fati* give a test and an attitude that make meaning possible without a "true world." Nihilism was the hangover of the old values, and Nietzsche shows how to live without them.

**No, he completed it.** Martin Heidegger, in lectures from 1936 to 1940, argued that Nietzsche's "will to power" was the last stage of Western metaphysics, not its overcoming. By making value something posited by the will, Nietzsche turned the whole world into material for valuation and mastery, which is the essence of modern technology and the deepest form of nihilism ([Chapter 9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md)).

**No, it can't be done his way.** Critics from many directions argue that value-creation by individual will is incoherent: values that I create and can uncreate at will have no authority over me, so they are not really values at all. This objection drives existentialist debates ([Chapter 11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md)) and contemporary metaethics ([Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md)).

**Partly, as a way of life.** A more modest reading (Nehamas) treats Nietzsche less as a theorist of value than as an exemplar: his books show a person shaping his life into something he could affirm, and invite readers to do the same in their own way. On this reading, the overcoming of nihilism is not a doctrine but an achievement, and it has to be achieved by each person anew.

What is clear is that after Nietzsche the question changed. Before him, the debate was whether God exists, and nihilism was what you were accused of if you said no. After him, the question is what to do *given* that the old foundations are gone. Kierkegaard ([Chapter 8](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md)), writing a generation earlier, had already asked that question from inside Christianity, and his answer, the leap of faith, is the great alternative to Nietzsche's.

## Living it

1. **The demon test.** Return to your answer to the almanac question from [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md). Now answer Nietzsche's demon: could you will your life to recur, exactly, forever? List three things in your life now that you could *not* will to recur. For each, write whether the problem is the thing itself, or how you are living with it. Choose one you can change this month, and change it.
2. **Turn an "It was."** Pick one episode from your past you still resent or regret: a failure, a loss, an injury. Write a page that does not deny or excuse it, but traces what it made possible in you (a skill, a friendship, a refusal, a sensitivity). Then decide whether you can say, honestly, "Thus would I have it," or only "Thus I can live with it." Both answers are acceptable; note which, and why.
3. **Write your own table.** Zarathustra says every people hangs a "table of excellencies" over itself. Write yours: five values you actually live by (judging from how you spend your time and money, not what you profess), and five you would *choose* to live by. Where the lists differ, write one concrete practice that would close the gap. Revisit the table in a month.

## Summary

- The problem Nietzsche must solve: humans can bear suffering but not **meaningless** suffering. The **ascetic ideal** gave it a meaning at the price of a "will for Nothingness": "man will wish _Nothingness_ rather than not wish _at all_."
- **Genealogy** shows that values have a history, so "_the value of these values_" can be questioned. **Master morality** says "good" of itself; **slave morality**, driven by **ressentiment**, says "evil" of the masters first (the lambs and the birds of prey). Read it as philosophical reconstruction, watch for the **genetic fallacy**, and note the problematic treatment of Judaism.
- The **revaluation of all values** judges values by a new standard, life and its enhancement, rather than a "true world." "**Valuing is creating**": values were always made by humans. The **camel, lion and child**: reverence, destructive freedom (active nihilism), and creative innocence, a "holy Yea."
- The key objection: if values are created, are they **arbitrary**? Nietzsche appeals to life and will to power; interpreters (Leiter, Reginster) disagree about whether this works.
- **Eternal recurrence** (*Joyful Wisdom* §341): would you will your life again, innumerable times? The same thought is "the extremest form of Nihilism" and "the highest formula of a Yea-saying to life." Its cosmological "proof" fails; its force is practical. Contrast Leopardi's sum with Nietzsche's *da capo*.
- **Amor fati**: "wishes nothing to be different, either in front of him or behind him, or for all eternity." Redemption is turning every "It was" into "**Thus would I have it!**"; the spirit of revenge is "the Will's antipathy to time." It differs from Stoic acceptance in having no providence behind it; it sits uneasily with protest against injustice.
- The **Übermensch**: "Man is a rope stretched between the animal and the Superman." Not a species, race or tyrant, but an ideal of self-overcoming; Goethe is the model. Nietzsche's ideal is **aristocratic**, and readers must decide what survives without that frame.
- **Tragic, Dionysian affirmation**: "The saying of Yea to life, including even its most strange and most terrible problems," against Schopenhauer's resignation.
- Did he overcome nihilism? Yes (Kaufmann, Reginster); no, he completed it (**Heidegger**); no, value-creation is incoherent; partly, as an exemplary way of life (**Nehamas**).

**You:** …

**You:** My first reaction is dread, honestly. There's a year of my life I'd do almost anything not to repeat.

**The demon:** Then you would gnash your teeth and curse me. Good, at least you're honest. Most people say "Sure!" because they don't believe me.

**You:** But that year is also when I met the people who matter most to me now. If I cut it out, I don't get them.

**The demon:** All things are enlinked. You cannot keep the friends and cut out the year that brought them.

**You:** So I either take the whole thing or nothing.

**The demon:** Exactly. Now: is your answer still a curse?

**You:** It's... less clear. I don't want the pain. But I don't want a life without what came out of it. If those are my only options, maybe I'd take it.

**The demon:** "Maybe I'd take it" is not "Thou art a God." You're bargaining, like the man with the almanac.

**You:** Fine. Then here's the honest answer: right now, I couldn't say yes with my whole heart. Not because the bad parts were bad, but because there are things I'm doing *now* that I'm only tolerating: a job I'm drifting through, a promise I keep postponing. Those are the parts I wouldn't want forever.

**The demon:** Ah. So the thought has done its work. It doesn't ask you to love the past. It asks what you are doing with *this moment*, which will also come again.

**You:** Then the real question isn't about the past at all.

**The demon:** The past is the gateway. The road runs both ways. Come back in a year and I'll ask again.

### Dialogue 2 — Nietzsche on creating values

**Nietzsche:** You tell me you've stopped believing in God, and now you feel nothing has any value. Why do you think values ever came from God?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Nietzsche in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Expect him to ask who wrote your values before you lost your faith.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Because that's what I was taught. God said what was right and wrong, and that's why it was right and wrong. Without that, it's all just opinion.

**Nietzsche:** And who taught you what God said?

**You:** My parents, church, the Bible.

**Nietzsche:** People, then. People who heard it from other people, who wrote it down in a particular time and place, for their own reasons. Values have always come from human beings. You have only discovered who the author was.

**You:** But that makes it worse! If people made values up, why should I follow them?

**Nietzsche:** You shouldn't, merely because they were made. You should ask what they are for. Does a value make you stronger, more alive, more capable of creating? Or does it make you smaller, more resentful, more afraid?

**You:** And who decides what counts as "more alive"? That's just your value.

**Nietzsche:** Of course it is mine! Did you expect a value that belongs to no one? That was the old error.

**You:** Then I could pick any values I like, and they'd be just as good as yours.

**Nietzsche:** Try it. Choose to value laziness, cowardice, and lying to yourself, and see whether you can affirm that life, whether you could will it to recur forever. You'll find that not every "taste" survives the test.

**You:** So the test isn't whether a value is written in the sky. It's whether I can stand behind the life it produces.

**Nietzsche:** Now you are no longer a camel. Be careful you don't become a lion and stay one, saying No to everything your parents gave you. Some of what you inherited may be worth keeping, once you have made it your own.

**You:** That sounds almost conservative.

**Nietzsche:** It is the child's game: keep what you can affirm, make what you lack. The point is that now you are the one doing it.

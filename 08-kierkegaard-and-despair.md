# Chapter 8 — Kierkegaard and Despair

Nietzsche announced the death of God. Kierkegaard, a generation earlier and a Christian to the bone, noticed something stranger: God could go on living in every church and every sermon while having died in every heart, and almost nobody would notice. His Copenhagen was prosperous, educated, religious and, he thought, spiritually empty: an age of "reflection" in which everyone had an opinion and nobody had a passion. This chapter reads Kierkegaard as the first great *diagnostician* of modern nihilism. You will meet his account of the "present age" and its leveling, the three "stages" or spheres of existence, the anatomy of despair in *The Sickness unto Death*, the scandal of Abraham in *Fear and Trembling*, and the famous (and famously misnamed) "leap of faith." You do not have to share his faith to use his diagnosis; much of twentieth-century existentialism did exactly that, as the next three chapters show. Previous: [Chapter 7 — Nietzsche Beyond Nihilism](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md). Next: [Chapter 9 — Heidegger and the Nothing](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md).

## Copenhagen, 1846: a philosopher is laughed to pieces

Picture a thin man with a stoop, a walking stick and a habit of stopping strangers in the street for long conversations. In the winter of 1845–46 that man became the most ridiculed person in Denmark. *The Corsair* (*Korsaren*), a satirical weekly, ran cartoon after cartoon of Søren Kierkegaard: his uneven trouser legs, his spindly shanks, his umbrella. Street boys shouted "Either-Or!" at him, the title of his first big book. He had more or less asked for it. After a hostile review of his work by one of the paper's contributors, Kierkegaard published a reply demanding to be abused in *The Corsair*, since it was so embarrassing to be the only Danish author it had spared. The paper obliged. His translator Lee Hollander quotes from the journal what it felt like:

> What is it to be roasted alive at a slow fire, or to be broken on the wheel or, as they do in warm climates, to be smeared with honey and put at the mercy of the insects—what is that in comparison with this torture: to be grinned to death!
>
> — Kierkegaard, *Journals*, quoted in Hollander's Introduction to *Selections from the Writings of Kierkegaard* (1923)

The episode matters philosophically, not just biographically. Out of it came the short book that makes Kierkegaard a founding analyst of nihilism: *A Literary Review* (*En literair Anmeldelse*, 1846), nominally a review of a novel called *Two Ages*, whose final part is a savage portrait of "the present age." Hollander's summary is fair:

> He now appended a series of bitter reflections on the Present Times, paying his respects to the Press, which he calls incomparably the worst offender in furnishing people with cheap irony, in forcibly levelling out and reducing to mediocrity all those who strive to rise above it intellectually
>
> — Lee M. Hollander, Introduction to *Selections from the Writings of Kierkegaard* (1923)

Some facts to hang the rest on. Kierkegaard was born in 1813, the youngest child of a wealthy, guilt-ridden Copenhagen wool merchant. He studied theology, wrote a dissertation on Socratic irony (1841), and broke off his engagement to Regine Olsen in 1841 for reasons he spent the rest of his life half-explaining. The years 1843–46 produced a torrent of pseudonymous books (*Either/Or*, *Fear and Trembling*, *The Concept of Anxiety*, *Philosophical Fragments*, *Stages on Life's Way*, *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*), followed by overtly Christian works such as *The Sickness unto Death* (1849) and, in 1854–55, a public war on the Danish state church. He collapsed in the street in October 1855 and died on 11 November, aged 42.

## How to read a writer who hides

Before any argument, a warning. Kierkegaard practised what he called *indirect communication* (*indirekte Meddelelse*). Most of his philosophical books are signed by invented authors, each with a point of view Kierkegaard did not simply share. "A," the young aesthete of *Either/Or*, is witty and desperate; Judge William, who answers him, is earnest and a bit dull; Johannes de Silentio ("John of Silence") writes *Fear and Trembling* while confessing that he cannot understand his hero; Johannes Climacus, a humorist who is "not a Christian," writes the *Postscript*; and Anti-Climacus, a Christian of a higher order than Kierkegaard claimed to be, writes *The Sickness unto Death*. In *The Point of View for My Work as an Author* (written 1848, published posthumously) he asks readers to quote the pseudonyms under their own names.

Why go to the trouble? Because of a Socratic conviction: the truths that matter most for a life cannot be handed over like information. If a book tells you directly "you must choose yourself," you file it away as a proposition and go on as before. If instead it shows you a seductive, clever, hollow life from the inside and lets you feel its hollowness yourself, you might actually change. The method is a cousin of the Socratic dialogue, and Kierkegaard knew it: his dissertation was about Socrates' irony.

So when this chapter says "Kierkegaard argues," read "the relevant pseudonym argues, and Kierkegaard wants you to wrestle with it." Scholars disagree about how much distance to keep. Readers in the "literary" camp (Roger Poole, and in a different way Louis Mackey) stress the masks and resist extracting doctrines; most philosophical commentators (Alastair Hannay, C. Stephen Evans, John Lippitt, Anthony Rudd) think stable positions can be reconstructed, provided you note who is speaking.

## The present age: leveling without drama

Here is the core diagnosis of *A Literary Review*, in paraphrase. Earlier ages, including the revolutionary age of 1789, were ages of *passion*: people acted, rebelled and sometimes did terrible things, but they were wholly in what they did. The present age is an age of *reflection* (*Reflexion*). It discusses everything and does nothing. It is "essentially a sensible, reflecting age, devoid of passion," which flares up briefly in enthusiasm and then subsides into indolence (Dru's translation, paraphrased). Its typical activity is not revolt but *chatter* (*Snak*). Its typical feeling is envy, which no longer takes the form of hatred of the great but of a quiet insistence that nobody should stand out.

This process he calls *leveling* (*Nivellering*). Leveling is not anyone's project; it is an anonymous drift in which every distinction of quality (in courage, devotion, talent, seriousness) is pressed flat into the average. Its agent is a strange entity Kierkegaard calls "the public" (*Publikum*). The public is not a people, a crowd in a square or a community. It is an abstraction made of individuals at the moment when they are *not* acting as individuals: readers of the same newspaper who form opinions they never have to stand behind. Kierkegaard calls it a phantom and a monstrous nothing. The press is its organ, and anonymity is its condition, since nobody is answerable for what "people are saying."

Why is this a form of nihilism? Not because anyone denies that values exist. Everyone in Copenhagen affirmed duty, love, God, art. The point is that values have been turned into topics. They are *discussed* rather than *lived*, and a value that is only discussed has lost its power to bind. You can put this as an argument:

1. A value is operative in a life only if the person is willing to stake something on it (to act, risk, suffer for it).
2. In the present age, values are held as opinions: endorsed, debated, compared, but not staked.
3. A value that is held only as an opinion is not operative.
4. So in the present age, values are universally affirmed and nowhere operative: a nihilism of practice, not of theory.

Notice how different this is from the dramatic nihilists of [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md), who denied everything and threw bombs. Kierkegaard would have found Bazarov almost refreshing: at least he meant it. The same complaint appears in *Either/Or*, put into the mouth of the aesthete:

> Let others complain that the times are wicked. I complain that they are paltry; for they are without passion. The thoughts of men are thin and frail like lace, and they themselves are feeble like girl lace-makers. The thoughts of their hearts are too puny to be sinful.
>
> — Kierkegaard (as "A"), *Either/Or*, Diapsalmata (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

And *Fear and Trembling* opens with a joke about the economy of ideas:

> Not only in the world of commerce but also in the world of ideas our age has arranged a regular clearance-sale. Everything may be had at such absurdly low prices that very soon the question will arise whether any one cares to bid.
>
> — Kierkegaard (as Johannes de Silentio), *Fear and Trembling*, Introduction (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

The target there is the Hegelian fashion of "going further": everyone in Copenhagen claimed to have *doubted everything* (as Descartes proposed) and to have moved beyond mere *faith* (to the higher standpoint of speculative philosophy). Doubt and faith, which once took a lifetime, are now bought cheap and resold.

### Leveling and the last man

Compare Nietzsche's "last man" in [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md): comfortable, cautious, blinking, everyone wanting the same thing. The two portraits converge on a single fear: not that people will believe something monstrous, but that they will believe nothing hard enough to be changed by it. They diverge on the cure. For Nietzsche the antidote to the herd is the creator of new values; for Kierkegaard it is *the single individual* (*den Enkelte*) before God. "The crowd is untruth" is one of his slogans (from the 1847–48 note "That Individual," appended to *The Point of View*). In a late polemic he puts the same idea with characteristic violence: when something serious must be done, the well-meaning crowd is the obstacle, since

> the effect of the crowd is to hamstring the whole cause by robbing it of its seriousness while heartfelt sympathy is pretended.
>
> — Kierkegaard, *The Present Moment* (*Øieblikket*), "What Says the Fire-Marshal" (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

### Objection and reply

*Objection:* this is elitist grumbling. Every generation thinks the next is shallow, and Kierkegaard's hatred of the press shades into contempt for ordinary people (Hollander himself notes he "ever doubted the ability of the 'people' to think for themselves"). Democratic discussion is not chatter; it is how free people govern themselves.

*Reply:* the strongest Kierkegaardian answer is that leveling is not about class. The philistine of the educated elite, with a well-stocked opinion on every question, is the purest product of the present age. The criterion is not intelligence or status but whether a person has *anything they would stake themselves on*. That is a test anyone can pass or fail. Still, the objection lands a blow: Kierkegaard offers no account of how a society of passionate individuals could deliberate together, and his late writings drift toward a contempt for "the numerical" that is hard to square with politics of any kind. Political philosophers from Habermas onward have taken the other side. For a provocative update, see Hubert Dreyfus's *On the Internet* (2001), which argues that online commentary, anonymous, riskless and endless, fits Kierkegaard's description of the press better than 1846 did.

## The aesthetic life and its despair

*Either/Or* (1843) is two volumes in a single cover, supposedly found in the secret drawer of a secondhand writing desk by its "editor," Victor Eremita. Volume One contains the papers of "A," a brilliant young man who lives for the interesting. Volume Two contains letters to A from Judge William, an older friend who urges him to choose a different life.

A's opening aphorisms, the *Diapsalmata* (Greek for "refrains" or musical interludes), are some of the best things Kierkegaard wrote. They are a self-portrait of a man who is witty about everything because nothing has hold of him:

> What is a poet? An unhappy man who conceals profound anguish in his heart, but whose lips are so fashioned that when sighs and groans pass over them they sound like beautiful music.
>
> — Kierkegaard (as "A"), *Either/Or*, Diapsalmata (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

> In addition to my numerous other acquaintances I have still one more intimate friend—my melancholy. … My melancholy is the most faithful sweetheart I have had—no wonder that I return the love!
>
> — Kierkegaard (as "A"), *Either/Or*, Diapsalmata (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

A despises the busy bourgeois ("Of all ridiculous things the most ridiculous seems to me, to be busy") and sees through the conventional meanings of his town:

> I beheld that the meaning of life was to make a living; its goal, to become Chief Justice; that the delights of love consisted in marrying a woman with ample means; … that it showed piety to partake of the communion once a year. I saw that and laughed.
>
> — Kierkegaard (as "A"), *Either/Or*, Diapsalmata (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

This is a recognizably nihilist voice: clear-eyed, funny, and unattached. A has seen through every value on offer and has replaced commitment with *interest*. In "Rotation of Crops," his mock-treatise on avoiding boredom, he recommends treating life like a farmer rotates fields: never commit to a friendship, a marriage, or a job; vary not the soil but the way you look at things; enjoy arbitrarily (watch only one drop of sweat on a boring speaker's forehead). Boredom, he says, is the root of all evil.

### The ethical: choosing oneself

Judge William's reply is that A's life, for all its brilliance, *is* despair, whether A admits it or not. The aesthete lives "in the moment" and in possibility; he never becomes anyone, because becoming someone means closing off possibilities. The ethical person, by contrast, *chooses himself*: takes over his history, his talents and limitations, his concrete duties (a job, a marriage, citizenship) as his own. Hollander's paraphrase captures the Judge's message:

> One floundering in this inevitable slough of despond, who earnestly wishes to escape from it and to save himself from the ultimate destruction of his personality, must choose and determine to rise into the ethical sphere.
>
> — Lee M. Hollander, Introduction to *Selections* (1923), summarizing *Either/Or*, Part II

The Judge's key thought is subtle. The important thing is not *what* you choose between good and evil but *that* you choose in the mode of earnestness at all. Someone who really chooses, he thinks, will discover the difference between good and evil in choosing. This is the passage that led Alasdair MacIntyre, in *After Virtue* (1981, chapter 4), to read Kierkegaard as the inventor of "criterionless choice": since there is no rational ground for choosing the ethical rather than the aesthetic, the choice is ultimately arbitrary, and modern moral philosophy inherits this arbitrariness. Defenders (Anthony Rudd, John Davenport, and others in *Kierkegaard After MacIntyre*, 2001) reply that the Judge is not describing a leap in the dark. The aesthete is already in despair, and the choice of the ethical is the reasonable response to a condition he can come to recognize in himself, much as a patient's choice to take treatment is reasonable once he admits he is sick.

### The stages, or spheres, of existence

From *Either/Or* and *Stages on Life's Way* (1845) comes the famous triad of the *aesthetic*, the *ethical* and the *religious*, called "stages" (*Stadier*) or, more accurately, "spheres of existence" (*Existents-Sphærer*):

| Sphere | Lives for | Typical figure | Characteristic failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aesthetic | the interesting, pleasure, possibility | Don Juan, the seducer, the ironist | boredom, melancholy, despair hidden by wit |
| Ethical | duty, the universal, a chosen self | Judge William, the married citizen | guilt: the discovery that you cannot fulfill the demand |
| Religious | an absolute relation to the absolute (God) | Abraham, the knight of faith | offense: the temptation to reject the paradox |

Three cautions. First, these are not life stages like childhood and adulthood; a sixty-year-old can be an aesthete. Second, the move from one to the next is not a logical inference but a transition through crisis, a *leap*. Third, in the *Postscript*, Climacus splits the religious sphere in two: "Religiousness A," a general religiosity of resignation, suffering and guilt available to anyone; and "Religiousness B," specifically Christian faith in the paradox that the eternal God became a historical human being. Irony sits on the border between aesthetic and ethical, and humor on the border between ethical and religious, which is why Kierkegaard's pseudonyms are so often ironists and humorists.

## The Sickness unto Death: an anatomy of despair

In 1849 a new pseudonym, Anti-Climacus, published *The Sickness unto Death* (*Sygdommen til Døden*), a "Christian psychological exposition for upbuilding and awakening." The title comes from John 11:4, where Jesus says of the dead Lazarus that "this sickness is not unto death." The Christian, says Anti-Climacus, has learned that physical death is not the worst thing. The sickness truly "unto death" is *despair* (*Fortvivlelse*), and its torment is the reverse of dying: it is being unable to die, being stuck with a self you can neither be nor get rid of.

### The self as a relation

The book begins with one of the most notorious paragraphs in philosophy, a parody of Hegelian jargon that is also completely serious. The opening definition, in the Hongs' translation, reads:

> The self is a relation that relates itself to itself or is the relation's relating itself to itself in the relation; the self is not the relation but is the relation's relating itself to itself.
>
> — Kierkegaard (as Anti-Climacus), *The Sickness unto Death*, Part One, A (tr. Howard V. Hong and Edna H. Hong, Princeton, 1980)

Unpacked: a human being is a *synthesis* of opposites: infinite and finite, possibility and necessity, the eternal and the temporal. You are a body with a date of birth and a death certificate on the way (finitude, necessity, temporality); you are also imagination, aspiration and a capacity to stand back from all of that (infinitude, possibility, the eternal). A synthesis alone is not yet a self. You become a self when you *relate yourself to* this synthesis: take up an attitude toward being this tension, and hold it together. And, Anti-Climacus adds, such a self did not establish itself; it is a relation that has been established by another (God). This last clause drives everything: despair is ultimately a misrelation to the power that established you.

### The forms of despair

Despair is *not* primarily a feeling. It is a structural misrelation in the self, and you can be in it while feeling fine. That is the book's most disturbing claim. Anti-Climacus distinguishes forms along several axes:

- **By the factors of the synthesis.** The *despair of infinitude* is getting lost in imagination: the fantasist, the person whose feelings and projects are ever more abstract and never get anywhere. The *despair of finitude* is the opposite: the person so absorbed in the everyday that they have, in effect, let themselves be ground smooth like a pebble and become a copy, a number, "one of the crowd." The *despair of possibility* is the dreamer for whom everything is possible and nothing is actual; the *despair of necessity* is the fatalist or the philistine for whom nothing is possible.
- **By consciousness.** The lowest form is *despair that is ignorant of being despair*, ignorant even of having a self. This is the despair of the present age: the busy, successful, cheerful person who has never once faced the question of who they are. Anti-Climacus compares such a person to someone who owns a house with a cellar and a ground floor and an upper story, and lives in the cellar.
- **In weakness: not willing to be oneself.** The person who despairs *over* something earthly (a lost lover, a failed career) and, under that, despairs of *themselves*: they wish to be someone else. Deeper still is despair *over* one's weakness, a self-enclosed shame.
- **In defiance: willing in despair to be oneself.** The highest and most dangerous form. The self wants to be itself, but on its own terms, as its own creation, refusing any dependence on a power that established it. Its extreme is "demonic" despair, which clings to its own suffering as proof against God.

Readers of [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md) will notice that defiant despair looks remarkably like Nietzsche's active nihilism and his ideal of self-creation, and that despair in weakness resembles passive nihilism. Kierkegaard died when Nietzsche was eleven, but the parallel is not accidental: both are mapping the same territory from opposite ends.

### The cure: faith as transparency

The book ends Part One with its formula for the self free of despair. Again in the Hongs' version:

> in relating itself to itself and in willing to be itself, the self rests transparently in the power that established it.
>
> — Kierkegaard (as Anti-Climacus), *The Sickness unto Death*, Part One, A (tr. Howard V. Hong and Edna H. Hong, Princeton, 1980)

This, Anti-Climacus says, is the definition of *faith*. Note what it does *not* say. Faith is not belief in propositions about God. It is a way of being a self: willing to be exactly the finite, temporal self you are, while resting in a ground you did not make. The person of faith is neither the fantasist nor the philistine, neither the coward who wishes to be someone else nor the rebel who insists on being self-made.

### The argument, and an objection

Reconstructed:

1. A human self is a synthesis that must relate itself to itself.
2. A self that relates itself to itself either rests in the power that established it or does not.
3. If it does not, it is in a misrelation: it is either trying not to be itself or trying to be itself on its own terms.
4. Such a misrelation is despair, whether or not it is felt.
5. So every self that is not in faith is in despair.

*Objection (unfalsifiability):* premise 4 makes despair immune to evidence. Tell Anti-Climacus you are content and he replies that you are in the lowest despair of all, the despair that does not know it is despair. A diagnosis that every possible report confirms explains nothing.

*Reply:* Anti-Climacus is not doing empirical psychology but conceptual analysis of selfhood, and conceptual claims are not refuted by reports of feelings, any more than "you can be wrong about whether you are in love" is refuted by your sincere declaration. The claim stands or falls with premise 1, the picture of the self as a task rather than a given. The fairer complaint is premise 2: why should the only alternative to despair be resting in *God*? Secular readers (Heidegger in *Being and Time*, Sartre, Camus) accepted much of the anatomy while rejecting the cure; [Chapter 9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md) and [Chapter 11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md) show how the analysis looks once the "power that established it" is removed. Contemporary philosophers have also explored a "deflationary" reading in which the established-by-another clause expresses the fact that we do not choose our nature, history or embodiment, and that a self which denies this dependence is self-deceived (see the debate between Hannay and Rudd, and Michael Theunissen's *Kierkegaard's Concept of Despair*, 1993; English tr. 2005).

## Fear and Trembling: Abraham and the absurd

*Fear and Trembling* (*Frygt og Bæven*, 1843) retells the story of Genesis 22: God commands Abraham to take his son Isaac to Mount Moriah and sacrifice him; Abraham obeys; at the last moment an angel stays his hand and a ram is offered instead. Everyone in Copenhagen's churches praised Abraham, the father of faith. Johannes de Silentio asks whether they had any idea what they were praising.

### The nihilist hypothesis, stated plainly

In the middle of a eulogy to Abraham, Johannes writes a passage that could be the motto of this whole course. It is a conditional: *if* there is no eternal, then this.

> If a consciousness of the eternal were not implanted in man; if the basis of all that exists were but a confusedly fermenting element which, convulsed by obscure passions, produced all, both the great and the insignificant; if under everything there lay a bottomless void never to be filled—what else were life but despair?
>
> — Kierkegaard (as Johannes de Silentio), *Fear and Trembling*, "A Panegyric on Abraham" (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

> if eternal oblivion were ever greedily watching for its prey and there existed no power strong enough to wrest it from its clutches—how empty were life then, and how dismal!
>
> — Ibid.

He continues: "And therefore it is not thus." Notice the logic. Kierkegaard is not offering a proof that the antecedent is false; he is stating, with great clarity, that the nihilist picture (a blind fermenting nature, generations passing like leaves, oblivion at the end) would make life despair. That conditional is accepted by nearly everyone in this course. Schopenhauer and the pessimists of [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md) affirm the antecedent and accept the consequent; Nietzsche affirms the antecedent and tries to deny the consequent; Kierkegaard denies the antecedent by faith; Camus in [Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md) will call Kierkegaard's move "philosophical suicide."

### The teleological suspension of the ethical

The ethical, for Johannes, is the *universal*: what applies to everyone, at all times, and can be stated and justified publicly. This is Hegel's ethical life (*Sittlichkeit*) as much as Kant's moral law. In the ethical, the individual's task is to express himself in the universal; to set oneself as a particular above the universal is sin. Measured by the ethical, Abraham is simply a would-be murderer. Johannes refuses to soften this:

> The fact is, the ethical expression for what Abraham did is that he wanted to murder Isaac; the religious, that he wanted to sacrifice him. But precisely in this contradiction is contained the fear which may well rob one of one's sleep.
>
> — Kierkegaard (as Johannes de Silentio), *Fear and Trembling*, "Preliminary Expectoration" (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

So, he asks in *Problema I*: is there a *teleological suspension of the ethical* (*teleologisk Suspension af det Ethiske*)? Is there a higher *telos* (end) for the sake of which the ethical, without being abolished, is suspended? If there is not, Abraham is lost and so is faith; if there is, then "the single individual" stands, as particular, in an absolute relation to the absolute, higher than the universal. That is the *paradox* of faith, and it cannot be *mediated*, that is, absorbed into a higher rational synthesis in Hegel's manner.

Johannes sharpens the point by contrast with the *tragic hero*. Agamemnon sacrifices Iphigenia so that the Greek fleet can sail; Jephthah sacrifices his daughter to keep a vow that saved Israel; Brutus executes his sons for treason. Terrible, but intelligible: each sacrifices one ethical duty (to a child) for a higher *ethical* duty (to the state), and everyone can weep with them. Abraham has no such higher ethical reason. He cannot explain himself; he cannot even speak (*Problema III*). He is utterly alone.

### Infinite resignation and faith: the double movement

Johannes distinguishes two "movements." The *knight of infinite resignation* gives up the finite thing he loves (the classic example: a young man's love for a princess he can never have) and finds peace in an eternal consciousness of that love. This is noble, painful, and humanly possible; Johannes can do it himself. It is, in effect, the Stoic movement of [the Stoicism course](../learn-stoicism/12-stoic-practices.md): let go of what is not up to you.

The *knight of faith* makes this movement and then a second one: *by virtue of the absurd*, he gets the finite back. Abraham gives up Isaac completely, and yet believes, at the same time, that he will receive Isaac again in this life:

> the movement of faith must ever be made by virtue of the absurd, but, note well, in such wise that one does not lose the things of this world but wholly and entirely regains them.
>
> — Kierkegaard (as Johannes de Silentio), *Fear and Trembling*, "Preliminary Expectoration" (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

> As far as I am concerned, I am able to describe most excellently the movements of faith; but I cannot make them myself.
>
> — Ibid.

The comedy of the book is that the knight of faith is undetectable. The knights of resignation are easy to spot: "their gait is dancing and bold." The knight of faith, when Johannes finally imagines meeting one, is a shock:

> Good Lord! that person? Is it really he—why, he looks like a parish-beadle!
>
> — Kierkegaard (as Johannes de Silentio), *Fear and Trembling* (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

(Other translators have "tax collector.") He walks in the woods, enjoys the new omnibuses, looks forward to a roast lamb's head for dinner, and is unbothered when his wife has not made it: "He belongs altogether to this world, no philistine more so." The point is philosophical, not whimsical. Faith, unlike resignation, does not *look* like anything special, because what it achieves is a return to ordinary finite life, received now as a gift rather than possessed as a right. That is also the precise difference between faith and the despair of finitude: both look like the life of a contented shopkeeper, but one has been through the infinite and come back, and the other has never left.

### Objections

- **Kant's objection** (in advance). In *The Conflict of the Faculties* (1798), Kant had already said what Abraham ought to have answered: that he can be certain he must not kill his innocent son, but can never be certain that the voice commanding it is God's. Moral certainty outranks any claimed revelation. Kierkegaard's defenders reply that this is exactly the ethical standpoint whose adequacy is in question; critics reply that a standpoint that cannot distinguish Abraham from a psychotic is no standpoint at all.
- **The religious-violence objection.** After a century of murder in the name of absolute commands, the teleological suspension looks dangerous. Martin Buber ("The Suspension of the Ethical," 1952) asked how anyone can know that the voice is God's and not Moloch's. Emmanuel Levinas, in essays collected in *Proper Names*, insisted that the high point of the story is the angel's voice that *stops* Abraham, returning him to the ethical.
- **Interpretive replies.** Many scholars deny that *Fear and Trembling* licenses anything. Some read it as a reductio of Hegelian complacent Christianity: if you praise Abraham, see what you are committed to (Louis Mackey, Edward Mooney's *Knights of Faith and Resignation*, 1991). Others read it as a divine-command theory of ethics in which God's will grounds duty (C. Stephen Evans, *Kierkegaard's Ethic of Love*, 2004). Others read it biographically, as Kierkegaard's sacrifice of Regine. Jacques Derrida's *The Gift of Death* (1992) generalizes it: every responsible act sacrifices some other obligation, and so every responsible act has something of Abraham's silence. These readings are not exclusive. The book is designed to make you unable to settle into one.

## Truth as subjectivity, and the leap

Johannes Climacus, in the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846), asks a deliberately naive question, which Hollander renders: "How shall I become a Christian?" His answer turns on a distinction between *objective* and *subjective* reflection. Objective reflection asks *what* is true and tries to eliminate the knower's personal stake. That is right for mathematics and history. But for an *existing* person, someone who must live and die and decide, some truths matter only in the *how* of one's relation to them. Climacus's famous definition, in the Hongs' translation:

> An objective uncertainty, held fast through appropriation with the most passionate inwardness, is the truth, the highest truth there is for an existing person.
>
> — Kierkegaard (as Johannes Climacus), *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, Part Two, Section II, ch. 2 (tr. Howard V. Hong and Edna H. Hong, Princeton, 1992)

His illustration, which Hollander summarizes, is deliberately provocative: someone who prays with passion to an idol is closer to the truth than someone who prays to the true God with a false, merely conventional spirit. The point is not that any belief is as good as any other. It is that, for existential truths, the *mode* of holding them (*inwardness*, *Inderlighed*) is part of what makes them true *for* someone.

Faith therefore cannot be reached by argument. Historical evidence about Jesus yields at best probability, and probability is the wrong currency for an eternal happiness. Lessing had spoken of an "ugly broad ditch" between contingent truths of history and necessary truths of reason. Climacus says the ditch is crossed only by a *leap* (*Spring*), a qualitative transition that no amount of quantitative approximation can produce.

A note on misattribution: the phrase "leap of faith" does not occur in Kierkegaard. He speaks of the leap, of faith, and of "the leap" as the category of decision, but the stock English phrase is a later summary. Nor is the leap a blind jump into irrationality. Climacus thinks reason can and should *clarify* exactly what it cannot do: it can bring you to the edge and show you why you are at an edge. That is why the *Postscript* is several hundred pages of argument.

### Is this nihilism's cure, or nihilism wearing a cassock?

Critics from opposite sides have argued that subjective truth is itself nihilistic. If truth for an existing person is a matter of passionate commitment to an objective uncertainty, what distinguishes the knight of faith from the passionate fanatic? Theodor Adorno, in *Kierkegaard: Construction of the Aesthetic* (1933), accused Kierkegaard of a bourgeois retreat into an empty "inwardness." Others see in "truth is subjectivity" a forerunner of relativism. The strongest reply comes from Kierkegaard's insistence that the subject is not free to manufacture its object: Christian faith, for Climacus, is faith in a particular, offensive, historical paradox (God in time), not in whatever one happens to feel strongly about. Subjectivity is the *how*; the *what* is given. Whether that reply is stable, once you remove Kierkegaard's specific Christianity, is exactly the question existentialism inherited.

## The attack on Christendom

The last year of Kierkegaard's life was a public war. In January 1854 Bishop Jakob Mynster, primate of the Danish church and an old family friend, died. In a memorial sermon a few days later, Hans Lassen Martensen, soon to be his successor (and a Hegelian theologian Kierkegaard despised), praised Mynster as a "witness to the truth" in the line of the apostles. Kierkegaard exploded. A comfortable state official who had never suffered for anything, a witness to the truth? From December 1854 he published newspaper articles, then a pamphlet series of his own, *The Moment* (*Øieblikket*): nine issues appeared in 1855, and a tenth was found ready for the press among his papers after his death in November.

> The intention of Christianity was: to change everything.
>
> The result, the Christianity of "Christendom" is: everything, literally everything, remained as it had been, with just the difference that to everything was affixed the attribute "Christian"
>
> — Kierkegaard, *The Present Moment*, "The Christianity of the New Testament; the Christianity of 'Christendom'" (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

This is the leveling diagnosis turned on religion itself. "Christendom" (*Christenhed*), a whole society in which everyone is Christian by birth, baptism and taxation, has made Christianity impossible, because it has made it effortless. Values that cost nothing have become, in effect, no values: a state church can be a vast machine for producing nihilism while every bell rings. When he lay dying, Kierkegaard reportedly told his friend Emil Boesen that he would take communion only from a layman, not from a priest, since priests were royal civil servants. The family buried him with full church ceremony anyway; his nephew Henrik Lund protested at the graveside.

Nietzsche never read Kierkegaard. The Danish critic Georg Brandes, who lectured on Nietzsche in Copenhagen in 1888, urged him in a letter to look at Kierkegaard; Nietzsche replied that he meant to, and then his mind collapsed in January 1889. The two attacks on Christianity (*The Antichrist* and *The Moment*) were written about thirty years apart by men who never met, and they agree on a surprising amount: that Christendom is not Christianity, that the comfortable faith of the bourgeoisie is dead, and that the age is drifting into a nihilism it does not even notice. They disagree only about whether the remedy is more Christianity or none.

## Kierkegaard's legacy in the nihilism debate

Three lines run forward from Kierkegaard.

1. **Existential anxiety.** *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844) analyzed *Angest* (anxiety, or "dread" in older translations) as the "dizziness of freedom": the feeling of standing before pure possibility, anxious about nothing in particular. Heidegger turned this into the mood that reveals "the nothing" ([Chapter 9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md)); Sartre into the anguish of radical freedom ([Chapter 11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md)).
2. **The absurd.** Kierkegaard's faith "by virtue of the absurd" became, in Camus, the absurd itself, now without a God to leap toward ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)).
3. **Authenticity.** The single individual against the public became Heidegger's authentic self against *das Man* ("the they"), and, via Dreyfus and Charles Taylor, a central theme of debates about modern identity.

For the philosophy-of-religion side of Kierkegaard (the Hegel critique, the *Fragments*), see [the Philosophy course, Chapter 14](../learn-philosophy/14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md); for the existentialist ethics that developed from him, see [the Ethics course, Chapter 16](../learn-ethics/16-existentialist-ethics.md).

## Living it

Kierkegaard's own advice about reading him was to read slowly and aloud, and to ask of every page "does this concern me?" Three practices follow from the texts.

**A despair inventory.** Take Anti-Climacus's map and locate yourself honestly. Where do you live in possibility (plans, fantasies, "one day") and never in actuality? Where have you been ground smooth into the finite (a job, a role, a feed) so that you could not say who you would be without it? When something goes badly wrong, do you wish to be someone else (weakness), or insist on being yourself owing nothing to anyone (defiance)? You do not need to accept the theology to find the categories uncomfortably precise. Write one paragraph for each axis. The test of the exercise is not whether it makes you feel better; it is whether you recognize anything you had not admitted.

**The leveling audit.** For one week, notice each time you form an opinion you would never act on or answer for: the comment, the share, the verdict on a stranger. Kierkegaard's question is not "is your opinion right?" but "what do you have at stake in it?" At the end of the week, pick one value you affirm in conversation and ask what it has actually cost you in the last year. If the answer is "nothing," Kierkegaard would say you hold it as a topic, not as a value.

**Willing one thing.** In *Purity of Heart Is to Will One Thing* (1847), Kierkegaard asks whether your life is organized around one thing you will wholeheartedly, or around many things you want partly, for reward, out of fear, or "to a certain degree." Try writing down what you would say is the one thing. If you cannot, that is information. If you can, compare it with how you spent the last ten days. The gap is the subject matter of this chapter.

## Summary

- Kierkegaard diagnoses a nihilism of *practice*: in a "reflective," passionless age, values are universally affirmed as opinions and nowhere lived. *Leveling* flattens every qualitative distinction; "the public" is a phantom of uncommitted individuals.
- He writes indirectly, through pseudonyms, so that readers must appropriate rather than merely learn; attribute views to the right voice.
- The *aesthetic*, *ethical* and *religious* are spheres of existence, not age stages; transitions between them are leaps through crisis. MacIntyre reads the choice of the ethical as "criterionless"; defenders reply that despair makes it reasonable.
- *The Sickness unto Death*: the self is a relation relating itself to itself, established by another. *Despair* is a structural misrelation, possibly unfelt, in weakness (not willing to be oneself) or defiance (willing to be oneself on one's own terms). Faith is resting transparently in the power that established the self.
- *Fear and Trembling* states the nihilist conditional (if under everything lay a bottomless void, life would be despair) and asks whether the ethical can be teleologically suspended. Faith is a double movement: infinite resignation, then receiving the finite back by virtue of the absurd. Objections: Kant, Buber, Levinas; readings: reductio, divine command, biographical, Derrida.
- "Truth is subjectivity" concerns the *how* of existential truths; the leap is a qualitative transition. "Leap of faith" is not Kierkegaard's phrase.
- The attack on Christendom turns the leveling diagnosis on the church. Anxiety, the absurd and authenticity pass from Kierkegaard to Heidegger, Camus and Sartre.

**You:** …

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes, broadly. I'm not in despair. Despair is when you've lost hope, and I haven't.

**Johannes Climacus:** Then you mean despair is a feeling, the way a toothache is a feeling. If you don't feel it, you don't have it.

**You:** What else could it be?

**Johannes Climacus:** My more serious colleague Anti-Climacus says it is a condition of the self, as a disease is a condition of the body. Have you never had an illness before you had symptoms?

**You:** Of course. But a body is a physical thing. A self is something I'm aware of from the inside. How could I be wrong about whether my own self is in a bad state?

**Johannes Climacus:** You have never been wrong about whether you were in love? Or whether you were angry? People discover these things about themselves daily. But let us test it. Suppose you lost the job tomorrow, and the friends drifted away. What would remain of you?

**You:** I'd be miserable, obviously. But I'd still be me.

**Johannes Climacus:** And who is that? Describe the "me" that would remain, without mentioning the job or the friends or what anyone thinks of you.

**You:** … That's harder than it sounds. I suppose I'd say my values, my character.

**Johannes Climacus:** Which values have you ever paid for?

**You:** That's unfair. I haven't been tested.

**Johannes Climacus:** Not unfair; precise. Anti-Climacus calls the lowest form of despair the one that does not know it has a self at all, that lives in the cellar of its own house. Such a person is happy as long as the finite holds. When it gives way, they despair *over* the lost thing, and then discover that they were despairing of *themselves* all along. The loss doesn't create the despair; it reveals it.

**You:** But then you've made despair unfalsifiable. Whatever I say, you'll say I'm in despair.

**Johannes Climacus:** A good objection, and Anti-Climacus would accept part of it: he is not offering an empirical survey. He is offering a picture of what a self is: not a given, but a task, a relation that must relate itself to itself. If that picture is wrong, the diagnosis falls. So attack the picture, not the diagnosis.

**You:** Fine. Maybe a self isn't a task. Maybe I'm just a person with preferences, and when the preferences are satisfied I'm happy, full stop.

**Johannes Climacus:** That is the aesthete's answer, and a coherent one. But notice that you just *chose* it, here, in front of me. You took a stand on what you are. Can a mere bundle of preferences choose what it is?

**You:** … So the very act of denying that I'm a self-relating self is an instance of self-relating.

**Johannes Climacus:** I make no claims; I am only a humorist. But you see now why I say this cannot be settled by asking how you feel. The question is whether you will take up the task. Contentment is not evidence either way.

**You:** Then the honest conclusion is: I don't know whether I'm in despair, but I can no longer use "I feel fine" as the answer.

**Johannes Climacus:** That is already more than most of Copenhagen has managed.

### Dialogue 2 — On Abraham and the suspension of the ethical

**Socrates:** You have just read *Fear and Trembling*. Tell me plainly: did Abraham do right?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** No. A father who would kill his child because a voice told him to is dangerous, whatever he calls the voice.

**Socrates:** You agree with Kant, then: whatever the voice says, you can be more certain that killing an innocent child is wrong than that the voice is divine.

**You:** Exactly.

**Socrates:** And yet you have heard Abraham praised as the father of faith, in synagogue, church and mosque alike. Were all those people praising a would-be murderer?

**You:** They were praising the idea of trust in God. They weren't thinking about what it would mean to do it.

**Socrates:** That is precisely Johannes de Silentio's charge against his own congregation. He says the ethical expression for Abraham's act is that he wanted to murder Isaac. So you and Johannes agree about the facts. Where do you disagree?

**You:** He thinks there might be a higher end than the ethical. I don't.

**Socrates:** Then let us test your position. In my own city, Euthyphro prosecuted his father for murder because, he said, the gods require piety. I asked him whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved by the gods. Which do you say?

**You:** That the gods, if they exist, love it because it's good. Goodness comes first.

**Socrates:** Then you have already denied the teleological suspension: there can be no command higher than the good, because the good is what any legitimate command must track. Johannes would say you have made God a mere guarantor of ethics, an "invisible vanishing point," and that faith then becomes unnecessary. Does that trouble you?

**You:** Not really. If ethics is enough, why do I need faith?

**Socrates:** Perhaps you don't. But consider what the ethical cannot do. It is universal: it can say what anyone in your position ought to do. Can it tell you whom to love, which life to give yourself to, what to stake everything on?

**You:** Not exactly. It can rule things out. It can't choose for me.

**Socrates:** Then there is a region of your life where the universal falls silent, and where you must act as this particular person, without being able to justify yourself to everyone. Is it not there that Johannes locates Abraham's terror?

**You:** Maybe. But choosing a career isn't like killing a child. The silence of ethics about my career doesn't license breaking its clear commands.

**Socrates:** That is a fine distinction, and I think it is the one Levinas draws when he says the decisive voice in the story is the one that stops the knife. Where does that leave us?

**You:** With two claims. First, ethics doesn't settle everything, and in what it leaves open we choose alone and can't fully explain ourselves. Second, that solitude never licenses violating what ethics does settle. Abraham, as a model, is useful for the first and dangerous for the second.

**Socrates:** And would Johannes accept your second claim?

**You:** Probably not. That's the point of the book: he wants me to feel that faith can't be made safe.

**Socrates:** Then we have learned where the disagreement really lies. Not about Abraham's act, but about whether anything may stand higher than the universal. That is worth knowing, even if we cannot settle it today.

</details>

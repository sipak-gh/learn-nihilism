# Chapter 9 — Heidegger and the Nothing

On 24 July 1929, in the main hall of the University of Freiburg, a short man in a peasant-style jacket gave his inaugural lecture before the assembled faculties. His subject was nothing. Not "nothing in particular": *the nothing* (*das Nichts*). Martin Heidegger argued that the sciences, for all their rigor, rest on a question they cannot ask, that a mood we all know (anxiety) opens onto this nothing, and that human existence is "held out into" it. Three years later the logician Rudolf Carnap made one sentence from that lecture the textbook example of meaningless metaphysics. Heidegger went on to give the most ambitious account of nihilism after Nietzsche: nihilism is not a modern mood or a moral failure but the hidden history of Western thought itself, culminating in the age of technology. He also joined the Nazi party. This chapter covers "What Is Metaphysics?", the analysis of anxiety, *das Man* and death in *Being and Time*, Heidegger's reading of Nietzsche, his exchange with Ernst Jünger about "crossing the line" of nihilism, and the political shadow over all of it. Previous: [Chapter 8 — Kierkegaard and Despair](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md). Next: [Chapter 10 — Camus and the Absurd](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md).

## Freiburg, 1929: a lecture about nothing

Heidegger had just succeeded his teacher Edmund Husserl, the founder of phenomenology, in the chair of philosophy at Freiburg. *Being and Time* (1927) had made him famous; students called him "the little magician from Messkirch." The audience at his inaugural lecture was not only philosophers but physicians, lawyers and chemists, so he began with them.

Every science, he said, relates to *beings* (*das Seiende*): the chemist to substances, the historian to past events, the biologist to living things. Each does so with a distinctive rigor and submission to its object. And each, asked what it investigates, would answer: beings, and beyond that, nothing. The researcher studies what is, "and nothing else." But then, Heidegger asks, what about this nothing, which science keeps mentioning in order to dismiss? Science wants to know nothing of the nothing. Yet in rejecting it, science appeals to it.

The obvious objection comes immediately, and Heidegger raises it himself. To ask "what is the nothing?" is to treat the nothing as a something, a being that "is" thus and so. That is self-contradictory. Logic seems to forbid the question. Heidegger's answer is the first daring move of the lecture: the nothing is *not* derived from logical negation. It is the other way around. We can only say "not" because we have already, in some prior way, encountered the nothing. Negation in logic presupposes something more original. So if we want to understand the nothing, logic cannot be the final court.

### An old perplexity

Heidegger was not the first to find "is not" puzzling. In Plato's *Sophist*, the Eleatic Stranger, unable to say how what is not can in any sense be, turns the puzzle back on his predecessors and on being itself. Heidegger chose this passage as the epigraph of *Being and Time*:

> Since, then, we are in a difficulty, please to tell us what you mean, when you speak of being; for there can be no doubt that you always from the first understood your own meaning, whereas we once thought that we understood you, but now we are in a great strait.
>
> — Plato, *Sophist* 244a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

And in the opening of Hegel's *Logic*, which Heidegger cites in the lecture, the most abstract thought of all, pure being without any further determination, turns out to be indistinguishable from nothing:

> But this mere Being, as it is mere abstraction, is therefore the absolutely negative: which, in a similarly immediate aspect, is just Nothing.
>
> — Hegel, *Encyclopaedia Logic* § 87 (tr. William Wallace, *The Logic of Hegel*)

Hegel even adds that "The Nothing which the Buddhists make the universal principle, as well as the final aim and goal of everything, is the same abstraction" (§ 87), an early and much-disputed European reading of Buddhist emptiness that [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md) discusses. Heidegger accepts Hegel's formula that pure being and pure nothing are the same, but for a different reason: not because both are empty abstractions, but because being, which is not any being, shows itself to finite creatures as a kind of nothing.

## Anxiety and the slipping away of the whole

If the nothing cannot be reached by logic, how is it reached? Heidegger's answer: through a *mood* (*Stimmung*). In *Being and Time*, moods are not private coloring added to neutral perception. They are basic ways in which the world is disclosed to us (*Befindlichkeit*, "attunement" or "state-of-mind," literally how one "finds oneself"). A world is always already given to us as threatening, boring, inviting, dull.

Some moods reveal beings *as a whole*. In profound boredom (the subject of his 1929–30 lecture course, *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics*), when "it is boring for one" on a Sunday afternoon and everything recedes into a gray indifference, things as a whole show up together, as a whole that does not matter. Joy in the presence of someone you love does the same in another key. But the mood that reveals the *nothing* is anxiety (*Angst*).

Heidegger distinguishes anxiety from fear (*Furcht*), following Kierkegaard ([Chapter 8](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md)). Fear is always fear *of* some definite thing: a dog, a diagnosis, a deadline. Anxiety has no definite object. When it passes, we say "it was nothing," and, Heidegger insists, we are exactly right. In anxiety, beings as a whole slip away from us. Nothing particular threatens; rather, the whole web of significance in which things matter (this task is for that goal, which is for my career, which is for…) loses its grip. We "hover." Even we ourselves slip away, so that in anxiety it is not "you" or "I" who feels uncanny, but "one." Heidegger's term is *das Unheimliche*, the uncanny, literally the not-at-home.

What remains when everything slips away? Not an empty void we can look at, but the nothing that repels us from beings as a whole and thereby makes them, for once, stand out as beings at all: as that which *is* rather than is not. This is the sentence that made the lecture famous and infamous. In Krell's translation:

> The nothing itself nihilates.
>
> — Heidegger, "What Is Metaphysics?" (tr. David Farrell Krell, *Basic Writings*)

The German is *Das Nichts selbst nichtet*: the nothing "noths." Heidegger coined a verb because no existing verb would do. The nothing is not an object and does not exist; nor is it an empty concept. It "occurs" as the event in which beings as a whole are shown as a whole, against their possible not-being.

### Dasein held out into the nothing

From this Heidegger draws a definition of human existence. His term for the being that we are is *Dasein*, literally "there-being," the being for whom its own being is an issue. Dasein, he now says, means being held out into the nothing (*Hineingehaltenheit in das Nichts*). Because we are always, beneath our busy dealings, already beyond beings as a whole, we can ask about them, wonder at them, relate to them as beings. Heidegger calls this being-beyond *transcendence*. Without the original openness of the nothing, there would be no selfhood and no freedom.

Two consequences matter for this course.

1. **The nothing is not nihilism.** Heidegger reverses the old metaphysical maxim *ex nihilo nihil fit* ("from nothing, nothing comes"): from the nothing, all beings as beings come to be (*ex nihilo omne ens qua ens fit*). The nothing is not the enemy of meaning but its condition. A creature that could not experience the possible not-being of everything could not experience the being of anything.
2. **Anxiety is usually covered over.** Mostly we flee it into busyness. Only rarely does original anxiety awaken; but, Heidegger says, it is always there, only sleeping, and it can wake at any moment without any special occasion.

The lecture closes with the question of Leibniz, rephrased: Why are there beings at all, and why not rather nothing?

## Carnap's objection: a sentence with no sense

In 1932 Rudolf Carnap, a leading member of the Vienna Circle, published "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language" in the journal *Erkenntnis*. Its centerpiece is a dissection of sentences from Heidegger's lecture, including "the nothing itself nihilates."

Carnap's argument, in outline:

1. A sentence is meaningful only if it is well formed according to logical syntax and its terms have empirical criteria of application (the *verification principle*, more fully discussed in [Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md)).
2. In ordinary language, "nothing" is not a name but a quantifier: "nothing is outside" means "there is no x such that x is outside."
3. Heidegger uses "nothing" as a noun naming an entity that performs an action ("nihilates").
4. This violates logical syntax: it treats a quantifier as a name, and the verb "nihilate" has no conditions of application.
5. So "the nothing nihilates" is a pseudo-statement (*Scheinsatz*): not false, but meaningless.

Carnap added a diagnosis. Metaphysics does express something real: an "attitude toward life" (*Lebensgefühl*). But it expresses it in the wrong medium, a theoretical one, where it masquerades as knowledge. The proper medium for an attitude to life is art. Metaphysicians, in his memorable phrase, are musicians without musical ability. (He singled out Nietzsche as the rare thinker who chose the right form: the poetry of *Zarathustra*.)

### Heidegger's reply, and who won

Heidegger answered indirectly, in the 1943 Postscript to the lecture. He had already said that logic was not the final court: the claim that the nothing precedes the "not" was precisely a claim that the formal analysis of negation is derivative. To refute him by appeal to logical form is to beg the question. In the Postscript he added a constructive gloss: the nothing is not a separate thing, but *being itself* as experienced by a thinking that knows only beings. The nothing is "the veil of being."

Who won? For a generation, in Anglophone departments, Carnap did; "the nothing nihilates" became a standard joke. More recent scholarship (Michael Friedman's *A Parting of the Ways*, 2000, on the 1929 Davos debate between Heidegger and Ernst Cassirer, which Carnap attended; Stephen Mulhall; and essays by Lee Braver) is more even-handed. Carnap was right that the sentence does not express a theoretical claim about an entity, and Heidegger agreed. Heidegger was right that Carnap's criterion of meaning was itself a substantive philosophical position, and the verification principle collapsed within two decades under its own weight (it is neither analytic nor empirically verifiable). The real question is whether there is a kind of understanding, phenomenological rather than propositional, that can be expressed without being a theory. Carnap said no; Heidegger and the whole phenomenological tradition said yes. The disagreement is a fork in twentieth-century philosophy, explored in [the Philosophy course, Chapter 18](../learn-philosophy/18-the-analytic-turn.md) and [Chapter 19](../learn-philosophy/19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md).

## Being and Time: the "they," anxiety, and death

"What Is Metaphysics?" compresses ideas worked out in *Being and Time* (*Sein und Zeit*, 1927). Three of them bear directly on nihilism.

### Das Man: the anonymous "they"

In everyday life, says Heidegger, I am not primarily an individual subject facing a world of objects. I am absorbed in tasks, using things for purposes that make sense in light of what "one" does. One drives on the right, one dresses like this for a funeral, one finds this film overrated. The subject of everyday existence is not "I" but *das Man*, literally "the one," usually translated "the they." In Macquarrie and Robinson's rendering of § 27:

> Everyone is the other, and no one is himself.
>
> — Heidegger, *Being and Time* § 27 (tr. John Macquarrie and Edward Robinson, 1962)

*Das Man* is not a group of people you could point to; it is the anonymous public norm by which we all measure ourselves. Its characteristic modes are *idle talk* (*Gerede*, passing on what is said without understanding it), *curiosity* (*Neugier*, restless interest in novelty for its own sake) and *ambiguity* (*Zweideutigkeit*, nobody can tell any more what was genuinely understood). Together they make up "falling" (*Verfallen*): Dasein's tendency to lose itself in the world of the they.

If this sounds familiar, it should. It is Kierkegaard's "public" and "leveling" made into an existential structure. Heidegger even writes of a "leveling down" (*Einebnung*) of all possibilities of being. Two differences matter. First, for Heidegger *das Man* is not a modern corruption but a permanent structure of existence: we cannot simply step out of it, because it gives us our first intelligibility of the world. Second, authenticity is not achieved by a relation to God but by a modification of how Dasein holds itself in the world.

### Anxiety individualizes

What breaks the grip of the they? Anxiety (§ 40). In anxiety the significance supplied by the they collapses; the world offers "nothing" to hold on to. That is precisely what throws Dasein back on itself as a being that must choose how to be. Anxiety, says Heidegger, individualizes: it discloses Dasein as *solus ipse*, alone with its own being-possible, its freedom.

### Being-toward-death

The second great disclosure is death (§§ 46–53). Heidegger is not interested in death as a biological event or in what comes after. He is interested in how my own death, as my ownmost possibility, structures my life *now*. Death is (i) *ownmost*: no one can die my death for me; (ii) *non-relational*: in it, all my relations to others drop away; (iii) *not to be outstripped*: there is no possibility beyond it; (iv) *certain*, yet (v) *indefinite* as to when. Heidegger calls it "the possibility of the impossibility of any existence at all."

The they tranquilizes this possibility: "one dies," eventually, sometime, not now, not me. Authentic *being-toward-death* (*Sein zum Tode*) does not mean brooding on mortality; it means "anticipating" (*Vorlaufen*) death as the horizon that makes my finite life a whole. In the face of that horizon, the they's options appear as what they are, and I can choose for myself. Heidegger calls this *resoluteness* (*Entschlossenheit*), and the voice that calls Dasein back from the they he calls conscience, a call that "says nothing," only summons Dasein to its own guilt in the sense of its being the "null basis of a nullity": thrown into a situation it did not choose, limited by possibilities it did not take.

Compare the Stoic meditation on death, *premeditatio malorum* and Seneca's "rehearse death" (see [the Stoicism course, Chapter 12](../learn-stoicism/12-stoic-practices.md)). Both use the thought of death to cut through distraction. The difference is that the Stoic rehearsal aims at tranquility by aligning the will with Nature's rational order, whereas Heidegger's anticipation aims at owning one's finitude, with no cosmic order to align with.

### Is resoluteness empty?

Here is where many critics locate a nihilism inside Heidegger. *Being and Time* tells you *that* you must resolve, and *how* (by owning your thrownness and finitude), but not *for what*. Karl Löwith, Heidegger's former student, recorded a joke among the students of the 1920s: "I am resolved, only I don't know to what." Hans Jonas, another former student, argued in "Gnosticism, Existentialism and Nihilism" (1952; later an epilogue to *The Gnostic Religion*) that Heidegger's picture of a human being thrown into an alien world, without a cosmos that supports its values, is a modern Gnosticism without the Gnostic God: the self is cut off from nature, and value becomes a matter of sheer decision. Jonas thought this "decisionism" helped explain how Heidegger could resolve, in 1933, for Hitler.

Defenders (Dreyfus, John Haugeland, William Blattner) reply that formal emptiness is a feature, not a bug. *Being and Time* describes the structure of any authentic choice; it would be inauthentic for a philosopher to supply its content, since content comes from the concrete "heritage" of possibilities one is thrown into (§ 74). Whether that answer reassures or alarms you will depend on what heritage you think was available to a German professor in 1933.

## Nihilism as the history of Being: Heidegger reads Nietzsche

From 1936 to 1940 Heidegger lectured on Nietzsche in Freiburg, then continued in essays to 1946. The lectures were published in 1961 as two thick volumes; the English edition (ed. Krell) runs to four. Their thesis is the boldest claim about nihilism in this course: Nietzsche is not the overcomer of nihilism but its completion, and nihilism is not a recent cultural event but the essence of Western metaphysics from Plato on.

Heidegger takes as his starting point the opening of Nietzsche's *Nachlass* collection *The Will to Power* (on that compilation's problems, see [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)):

> Nihilism is at our door: whence comes this most gruesome of all guests to us?
>
> — Nietzsche, *The Will to Power* § 1 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

> What does Nihilism mean?—That the highest values are losing their value. There is no bourne. There is no answer to the question: "to what purpose?"
>
> — Nietzsche, *The Will to Power* § 2 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

The German for "are losing their value" is *entwerten sich*: the highest values *devalue themselves*. Nietzsche's preface makes the point that matters most to Heidegger:

> because Nihilism is the only possible outcome of our greatest values and ideals,—because we must first experience Nihilism before we can realise what the actual worth of these "values" was
>
> — Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, Preface § 4 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Nietzsche's story, which Heidegger reconstructs: Platonism and Christianity located the highest values (truth, the good, God) in a "true world" beyond this one. The will to truth, cultivated by that same Christianity, eventually discovers that the true world is a fiction. The highest values then devalue themselves: God dies. The response Nietzsche proposes is a *revaluation of all values* (*Umwertung aller Werte*) grounded in the will to power, the one principle left standing once the beyond has collapsed:

> This world is the Will to Power—and nothing else! And even ye yourselves are this will to power—and nothing besides!
>
> — Nietzsche, *The Will to Power* § 1067 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

### Heidegger's twist: value-thinking is the problem

Heidegger's reading turns on one observation. Nietzsche's cure still thinks in terms of *values*. And for Heidegger, to think of anything as a value is already to have reduced it to a point of view posited by a will for its own preservation and enhancement. A value is valid *for* a subject that does the valuing. So when Nietzsche overturns the old values and posits new ones by the will to power, he does not escape the logic of nihilism; he completes it. Being itself has become a value, that is, a function of will. In "Nietzsche's Word: 'God Is Dead,'" Heidegger argues that the deepest blow against God is not the atheist's denial but the theologian's praise of God as the "highest value," because that makes God an object posited by human valuing.

So Heidegger redefines nihilism. In its essence, nihilism is the history in which "there is nothing to Being itself": Western metaphysics, since Plato, has asked about beings (what they are, what their highest cause is) and forgotten to ask about Being, the way in which beings are given and show themselves at all. Heidegger calls this *the forgetting of Being* (*Seinsvergessenheit*). Plato's Forms, the medieval God, Descartes' subject, Kant's conditions of possibility, Nietzsche's will to power: each names the being-est of beings, the ground of all others, and each in its way fixes Being as a supreme entity. This structure Heidegger calls *onto-theology*. Nietzsche is "the last metaphysician" because will to power plus eternal recurrence is the final form this structure can take: beings as a whole are will willing itself, endlessly.

### The age of technology

Where does the history end? In the present, which Heidegger calls the age of technology, though by "the essence of technology" he does not mean machines. He means a way in which everything shows up: as *standing reserve* (*Bestand*), resources on call for optimal use. His name for this way of revealing is *das Ge-stell*, "enframing." His example in "The Question Concerning Technology" (1954) is the Rhine. For Hölderlin the Rhine was a river of the gods; now a hydroelectric plant is set into it, and even the river as landscape is a reserve on call for the tourist industry. Forests become timber, people become "human resources," the planet becomes inventory. The danger is not that machines will rebel. It is that this way of revealing will become so total that we will no longer be able to experience anything, including ourselves, otherwise.

This is nihilism as Heidegger finally understands it: not people believing nothing, but a world in which everything is available and nothing has standing of its own. It is a diagnosis you may recognize in debates about optimization, attention economies, and "productivity."

The response Heidegger recommends is not resistance by will, since willing is the problem. In the 1955 address *Gelassenheit* ("Releasement," tr. in *Discourse on Thinking*, 1966) he proposes a double attitude: use technical devices, but keep a "yes and no" toward them, letting them not lay claim to our essence; and practice *meditative* rather than merely *calculative* thinking. In a 1966 interview with *Der Spiegel*, published after his death in 1976 at his request, he said something widely quoted and widely mocked:

> Only a god can save us.
>
> — Heidegger, interview with *Der Spiegel*, 1966, published 31 May 1976 (tr. Maria P. Alter and John D. Caputo, *Philosophy Today*, 1976)

(The German, *Nur noch ein Gott kann uns retten*, says more exactly "only a god can *still* save us.") He did not mean a religious revival. He meant that no human program can force a new way for things to show up; the most thinking can do is prepare a readiness for it.

## Jünger and "the line"

Ernst Jünger (1895–1998) was the most decorated young infantry officer of the German army in the First World War, and his war diary, *Storm of Steel* (*In Stahlgewittern*, 1920), is one of its great memoirs: cold, precise and exhilarated. In the essays "Total Mobilization" (1930) and *The Worker* (*Der Arbeiter*, 1932) Jünger described a new world in which all of life is mobilized as energy, and a new human type, "the worker," who masters it with technological discipline. These texts deeply influenced Heidegger, who ran a private seminar on *The Worker* in 1939–40. Heidegger's analysis of technology as total mobilization of resources owes Jünger an explicit debt.

Jünger was a nationalist and anti-liberal, but he kept his distance from the Nazis, refused a seat in the Reichstag and membership in the Academy, and published *On the Marble Cliffs* (1939), an allegory widely read as an attack on tyranny. He served as an officer in occupied Paris and was close to the circle of the July 1944 plot against Hitler without taking part.

In 1950 Jünger contributed an essay to the volume celebrating Heidegger's sixtieth birthday: "Across the Line" (*Über die Linie*). In paraphrase, his argument runs as follows. Nihilism, as Nietzsche, Dostoevsky and others foresaw, is the defining condition of the age. Its symptoms are everywhere: the reduction of everything to function, the disappearance of the wondrous, the spread of specialization and of bureaucratic and technical order, and the cold efficiency of violence in the world wars. But nihilism is not chaos; it can coexist with great order. And there are signs that we are now crossing the "line," the zero meridian at which nihilism reaches its fullest extent, beyond which a new turning toward Being may begin. A year later, in *The Forest Passage* (*Der Waldgang*, 1951), Jünger sketched the figure of the "forest rebel," the individual who withdraws inwardly from the total state while remaining within it.

### Heidegger's reply: writing *about* the line

Heidegger answered for Jünger's own sixtieth birthday in 1955 with an essay later titled "On the Question of Being" (*Zur Seinsfrage*). He played on the German preposition: Jünger's *über die Linie* means "across the line" (*trans lineam*); Heidegger proposed to write *über die Linie* in the sense of "about the line" (*de linea*). Before anyone could cross it, one would have to understand where it lies and what it is. And Heidegger doubted that it could be crossed by any act of will or vision. Since the essence of nihilism is the forgetting of Being, and since that forgetting shapes even the language in which we try to overcome it (including the language of "will," "value," "figure" and "worker" that Jünger shared with Nietzsche), the line is less a border to step over than a zone we inhabit. In that essay Heidegger wrote the word "Being" crossed out with an X, to signal that even the word, taken as a name for something, misleads. Jacques Derrida later borrowed this gesture for his practice of writing "under erasure" (*sous rature*).

The exchange is a compact lesson in two ways of responding to nihilism. Jünger: diagnose, endure, and look for the signs of passage beyond. Heidegger: do not try to overcome nihilism by an act of will, which would only repeat it; instead learn to think its essence, and wait. Heidegger's later word for this was *Verwindung*, a "getting over" in the sense in which one gets over an illness or a grief: not by defeating it but by working through it. Gianni Vattimo built his "weak thought" on this word ([Chapter 14](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md)).

## The shadow: Heidegger, Jünger and National Socialism

No honest chapter on Heidegger and nihilism can skip this. In April 1933 Heidegger became rector of Freiburg University; on 1 May he joined the National Socialist party, and on 27 May he gave a rectoral address, "The Self-Assertion of the German University," calling for the university to place itself at the service of the German people's spiritual mission. He resigned as rector in April 1934 but remained a party member until 1945. After the war he was barred from teaching until 1951. He never publicly repudiated his involvement in any satisfying way, and his most notorious postwar remark, in a 1949 lecture, set mechanized agriculture alongside the gas chambers as "the same" in essence. The publication from 2014 of the *Black Notebooks*, his private philosophical journals, revealed antisemitic passages connecting "world Jewry" with the calculative, uprooting spirit of the age.

The question for this chapter is whether Heidegger's thinking about nihilism is implicated. Positions fall roughly into three groups:

- **Inseparability.** Victor Farías (*Heidegger and Nazism*, 1987), Emmanuel Faye (*Heidegger: The Introduction of Nazism into Philosophy*, 2005) and, more cautiously, Richard Wolin (*The Politics of Being*, 1990) argue that the critique of liberal modernity, the decisionism of resoluteness and the talk of a people's destiny made Heidegger's philosophy receptive to Nazism, or even an expression of it.
- **Separability.** Julian Young (*Heidegger, Philosophy, Nazism*, 1997) argues that the core philosophy neither entails nor requires the politics, and that the later thought, especially *Gelassenheit*, is if anything anti-totalitarian.
- **Implication without reduction.** Many scholars (Peter Trawny, editor of the *Black Notebooks*; Iain Thomson) argue that Heidegger's history-of-Being narrative, which assigns whole peoples a role in the destiny of Being, invited his political errors and his antisemitism, but that his analyses of anxiety, the they and technology can be tested independently.

Leo Strauss, a Jewish refugee who had heard Heidegger lecture, gave a remarkable lecture in New York in 1941 called "German Nihilism" (published in 1999). Strauss argued that the German nihilism of the 1920s and 30s was not a desire to destroy everything, but a desire to destroy *modern civilization*, the liberal, commercial, pacifist "open society," out of a moral protest on behalf of seriousness, sacrifice and heroism. Its young adherents, says Strauss, knew what they rejected but had no idea what would come next, and the vacuum was filled by the worst. Strauss's diagnosis links this chapter back to the Russians of [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md): political nihilism is often not the absence of morality but a fierce morality of negation.

The lesson for you, as a reader, is not "never read Heidegger." It is to read him with open eyes. A diagnosis of nihilism can be insightful and its author's response to it disastrous. The critique of the they can be accurate and still be used to sneer at democracy. Resoluteness without content can resolve on anything.

## Was Heidegger a nihilist?

You can now see three answers, each defended by serious readers.

1. **Heidegger as overcomer.** Nihilism is the forgetting of Being; Heidegger is the first to think this forgetting as such, and his later thought (releasement, dwelling, the fourfold of earth, sky, mortals and divinities) points to a non-nihilistic way of being. (Young, Thomson, Dreyfus in his later work, Vattimo with qualifications.)
2. **Heidegger as nihilist.** His early decisionism and his later fatalistic waiting both abandon the rational, universal standards that could resist nihilism. (Jonas, Löwith, Habermas in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*, 1985, and Wolin.)
3. **Heidegger as phenomenologist of meaning-loss.** Whatever the merits of his history of Being, his descriptions of how meaning works (moods, the they, the ready-to-hand, anxiety) give the most precise account available of what it is like when meaning collapses, and of why meaning was never a property of things to begin with. (Dreyfus's *Being-in-the-World*, Haugeland, and much recent work on depression and phenomenology by Matthew Ratcliffe.)

The third answer is the most useful for your own purposes. It suggests that the experience of nihilism (everything slipping away, nothing mattering) is not evidence that life is meaningless. It is evidence that meaning was always a relation between a finite being and a world, and that the relation can go slack.

## Living it

**Attend to your moods as disclosures.** For a week, when you notice a strong mood (boredom, dread, unexpected joy), ask Heidegger's question: not "what caused this?" but "what does the world look like in this mood, as a whole?" Profound boredom, Heidegger thought, shows you that things as a whole are not mattering; that is information about how you are living, not just a feeling to be fixed with a phone. Write one sentence per mood.

**Audit the they.** Make a list of ten things you did yesterday. Next to each, write the honest reason: "because one does," "because I chose it," or "because I don't know." Heidegger does not say you should eliminate the first category; you couldn't, and it would be madness to try. The question is whether any of your life is lived in the second.

**Anticipate, don't brood.** Heidegger's being-toward-death is not a morbid exercise. Try this version: write down the three projects that would most obviously be unfinished if you died this year. Then ask, of each, whether it is yours or the they's. The Stoic version of this exercise asks what is in your control; Heidegger's asks what is your own. Do both and compare.

**Let one thing not be a resource.** Pick something you normally treat as standing reserve (a walk you take to "get steps in," a meal you eat while working, a friend you see when it is useful). Once this week, let it be what it is, with no further purpose. Heidegger's later essays ("Building Dwelling Thinking," "The Thing") are about exactly this: a jug as a jug, a bridge as gathering a landscape. If this sounds slight, notice how hard it is.

## Summary

- In "What Is Metaphysics?" (1929) Heidegger argues that the sciences presuppose the nothing they dismiss; the nothing is prior to logical negation and is revealed in the mood of *anxiety*, in which beings as a whole slip away. "The nothing itself nihilates." Dasein is "held out into the nothing," and this is the condition of selfhood, freedom and the question "why are there beings rather than nothing?"
- Carnap (1932) called the sentence a pseudo-statement violating logical syntax; Heidegger replied that logic is not the final court. The dispute marks the analytic/continental fork; the verification principle did not survive, but neither is Heidegger's claim a theory of an entity.
- *Being and Time*: everyday existence is absorbed in *das Man* (idle talk, curiosity, ambiguity, falling); anxiety individualizes; authentic being-toward-death and resoluteness own one's finitude. Critics (Löwith, Jonas) find this resoluteness empty, a "decisionism."
- Heidegger reads Nietzsche as the completion, not the overcoming, of nihilism: thinking in *values* reduces Being to the posit of a will. Nihilism in essence is the *forgetting of Being* in Western *onto-theology*, culminating in the technological *enframing* (*Ge-stell*) of everything as standing reserve.
- Jünger's "Across the Line" (1950) claims we are crossing nihilism's zero meridian; Heidegger replies that the line must first be thought, and cannot be crossed by will; *Verwindung*, not overcoming.
- Heidegger's Nazism and the *Black Notebooks* force the question of implication; positions range from inseparability (Farías, Faye) to separability (Young). Strauss's "German Nihilism" analyzes the moral protest behind political nihilism.

**Socrates:** And was that "nothing" the same as the nothing in "nothing is in the fridge"?

**You:** No. The fridge is just empty. The other thing was more like the whole world going flat.

**You:** But Heidegger is claiming more than a feeling. He says that experience reveals something true: that things could not be, and that this is why we can notice that they are.

**Socrates:** Is that a claim about a fact in the world?

**You:** Not exactly. It's a claim about the conditions under which there is a world for us at all.

**Carnap:** And how would one verify it?

**You:** Maybe by describing the experience carefully enough that others recognize it. That's what phenomenology does.

**Socrates:** So we have three positions. Carnap says the sentence is meaningless as a statement and harmless as poetry. **You:** Right. I still think Heidegger could have written more clearly.

**Socrates:** On that, I suspect even Heidegger's friends would agree.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether you are living as "one" lives

**Heidegger:** You told me that you chose your career, your city and your opinions freely. Let us see. Why this career?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Heidegger in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Because it pays well, it's respected, and I'm reasonably good at it.

**Heidegger:** "Respected." By whom?

**You:** People. Society. My family.

**Heidegger:** Can you name the person whose respect decided it?

**You:** Not one person. It's just generally regarded as a good career.

**Heidegger:** "Generally regarded." So the one who chose was the one who regards things generally. That is what I call *das Man*. I do not say this to accuse you. Everyone starts there. Without the they you would not know what a career is.

**You:** Then what's the problem?

**Heidegger:** The problem is not that you live by what one does. It is that you take it to be your own choice without ever having faced the question. Have you ever, even for a moment, felt that the whole of it, career, city, opinions, might simply fall away and leave you with nothing to hold on to?

**You:** Once, after a friend died. For a few weeks nothing seemed worth doing.

**Heidegger:** What did you do with that feeling?

**You:** I got over it. I went back to work. Everyone said that was healthy.

**Heidegger:** "Everyone said." Do you see how quickly the they returns to tranquilize? I do not say you should have stayed in that state. I say it showed you something: that your life is not secured by anything outside it, that you are finite and must choose. That is anxiety's gift.

**You:** But Kierkegaard would say what it showed me was my need for God. And a Stoic would say it showed me that the death of others isn't in my control.

**Heidegger:** Each interprets the disclosure. I claim only to describe it: in that state you were thrown back on your own being as a possibility you must take over.

**You:** Fine. Suppose I take it over. Suppose I'm "resolved." Resolved to do what?

**Heidegger:** I cannot tell you. That would be to replace the they with another authority.

**You:** Then your philosophy gave students in 1933 no reason not to resolve on something terrible. Löwith says they joked that they were resolved, but didn't know to what.

**Heidegger:** …

**You:** So here's where I land. You've shown me something true about how I live: most of what I call my choices were made by "one." And anxiety and death can break that spell. But you haven't shown me how to choose well once the spell is broken, and you can't just say content comes from "heritage," because heritages include monstrous things.

**Heidegger:** Then you will need other teachers for the second question.

**You:** Yes. Maybe Beauvoir, who says my freedom has to will the freedom of others too.

**Heidegger:** That is a different path. But you will at least walk it as yourself.

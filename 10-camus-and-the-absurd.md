# Chapter 10 — Camus and the Absurd

In 1942, in occupied Paris, Gallimard published two books by a little-known 28-year-old journalist from Algiers. One was a short novel about a clerk who goes to his mother's funeral, starts an affair the next day, shoots a man on a beach and is condemned to death, as far as one can tell, for not having cried. The other was a philosophical essay that opened by declaring that the only serious question in philosophy is whether to kill yourself. Albert Camus called the problem both books share *the absurd*. He never claimed to have refuted nihilism. He tried something odder and more useful: to accept the nihilist's premises (no God, no given meaning, no appeal beyond this life) and still refuse the nihilist's conclusions (that nothing matters, that suicide is reasonable, and later that murder is permitted). This chapter follows that attempt from *The Myth of Sisyphus* and *The Stranger* through *The Plague* to *The Rebel*, and ends with the quarrel with Sartre that broke the friendship. Previous: [Chapter 9 — Heidegger and the Nothing](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md). Next: [Chapter 11 — Sartre and Beauvoir](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md).

## The boy from Belcourt

Camus was born in 1913 in Mondovi, French Algeria. His father was killed at the Marne the following year. His mother, partly deaf and unable to read, cleaned houses in the working-class Algiers district of Belcourt. A primary-school teacher, Louis Germain, got him a scholarship to the lycée, where the philosopher Jean Grenier became his mentor. At seventeen he coughed blood: tuberculosis, which ended his hopes of a teaching career and his time as a goalkeeper, and gave him an early, physical sense of being sentenced without a verdict.

He wrote a thesis on Plotinus and Augustine, was briefly a Communist, ran a theater troupe and reported for *Alger républicain*. The "cycle of the absurd" (*The Stranger*, *The Myth of Sisyphus*, the play *Caligula*) appeared in 1942–44, while he edited the Resistance paper *Combat*. The "cycle of revolt" followed: *The Plague* (1947), *The Just Assassins* (1949), *The Rebel* (1951). He won the Nobel Prize in 1957 and died in a car crash on 4 January 1960, an unused train ticket in his pocket.

Two things matter for the philosophy. Camus was a working-class Mediterranean whose touchstones were the sea, the sun, the body and poverty, and he distrusted systems built in libraries. And he denied being an existentialist; in a 1945 interview he said *The Myth of Sisyphus* had been written *against* them. His question is not "what is human existence?" but "given that the world makes no sense, how should one live?"

## "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem"

The essay opens with a provocation:

> There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide.
>
> — Albert Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus*, "Absurdity and Suicide" (tr. Justin O'Brien)

Whether the world has three dimensions, or the mind nine or twelve categories, comes afterward; these are games. What comes first is judging whether life is or is not worth living. Camus notes that he has never seen anyone die for the ontological argument, but has seen many people die because they judged life not worth living, and others, paradoxically, get themselves killed for the ideas that give them a reason for living. The question of meaning is the most urgent of all questions because it is the one people actually act on.

This is a methodological claim as much as a rhetorical one. Philosophy, for Camus, must be judged by whether a person could *live* its conclusions. The nihilist who announces that life is meaningless and then goes home to dinner owes us an explanation. So, Camus thinks, does the philosopher who refutes nihilism with an argument nobody could feel the force of. The essay therefore proceeds from experience, not from definitions.

### How the absurd announces itself

Camus begins not with an argument but with the moments when the feeling of absurdity ambushes us.

1. **Weariness.** Rising, streetcar, four hours of work, meal, streetcar, four hours of work, meal, sleep, on the same rhythm. One day the "why" arises.
2. **Time.** We live on the future, "later," "when you have made your way," until we notice that time is our worst enemy and that hoping for tomorrow is hoping to get closer to death.
3. **The strangeness of the world.** A stone or a landscape becomes foreign; the meanings we projected onto it drain away.
4. **The strangeness of people.** A man talking on the telephone behind a glass partition: you see only his pointless pantomime and wonder why he is alive.

These experiences are what the absurd feels like. Then comes the definition, the most important sentence in the book:

> The absurd is born of this confrontation between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world.
>
> — Albert Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus*, "Absurd Walls" (tr. Justin O'Brien)

The world by itself is not absurd; it is merely silent. A human being by himself is not absurd; he merely longs for clarity and unity. The absurd is the *relation* between them, the "divorce" between a mind that demands sense and a world that does not supply it. Remove either term and it vanishes. This structural point carries the whole book.

### The walls of reason

Knowledge cannot close the gap. Science describes and classifies, then explains the atom by an image of planets and so ends in poetry; it never says *why*. Paradoxes show that the mind's demand for total unity contradicts itself once stated. The phenomenologists and existentialists have rediscovered the irrational in existence itself. Everyone who thinks honestly arrives at the same wall. What interests Camus is what each does there.

## Philosophical suicide

Camus now turns on the thinkers he most admired. Each recognizes the absurd and then leaps over it into something that abolishes it: "philosophical suicide."

- **Karl Jaspers** finds that reason fails before the "encompassing," and concludes that this failure is itself the sign of transcendence. The shipwreck of thought becomes a cipher of God.
- **Lev Shestov**, a Russian émigré in Paris, attacks reason with a ferocity Camus admired, and concludes that God must be sought precisely in the impossible. His early aphorisms show the temper:

  > The only way to guard against positivism … is to cease to fear any absurdities, whether rational or metaphysical, and systematically to reject all the services of reason.
  >
  > — Lev Shestov, *All Things Are Possible*, Part I (tr. S. S. Koteliansky)

  Shestov does not *live with* the absurd; he uses it as a springboard, and the absurd becomes God's name.
- **Søren Kierkegaard** is the most honest of the leapers and so the most dangerous. In *Fear and Trembling* Abraham believes

  > on the strength of the absurd; for there was no question of human calculation any longer.
  >
  > — Kierkegaard, *Fear and Trembling*, "Preliminary Expectoration" (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

  and the pseudonym is precise that this absurd is not a mere improbability:

  > Now the absurd is not one of the categories which belong to the understanding proper.
  >
  > — Kierkegaard, *Fear and Trembling*, "Preliminary Expectoration" (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

  For Kierkegaard the absurd is the condition of faith; for Camus it is the condition of honesty. Kierkegaard wants to be cured; Camus wants to stay lucid. (See [Chapter 8](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md) for the double movement.)
- **Edmund Husserl** commits the opposite leap, not into the irrational but into eternal reason: the "essences" that phenomenology discovers become a new heaven of timeless truths.

In each case the leap resolves the tension by suppressing one term: the world turns out to have a meaning after all (God, essences), or the demand for meaning is declared illegitimate. Camus's question is whether there is a way *not* to escape.

> **Is the charge fair?** Kierkegaard would reply that Camus has misread the leap: faith does not abolish the absurd but holds it fast, which is why it is "fear and trembling." The paradox remains a paradox for the believer, every day. Camus's reading is closer to Shestov's than to Kierkegaard's. A good exam question: is there any difference between "living with the absurd" and "believing by virtue of the absurd," other than the object of hope?

## Physical suicide, and why Camus rejects it

If philosophical suicide is cheating, what about the real thing? Camus's discussion is compressed, but there is an argument:

1. The absurd is a relation between two terms: the human demand for meaning and the world's silence.
2. A relation exists only as long as both terms exist.
3. Suicide abolishes one term, the human mind.
4. So suicide does not *respond* to the absurd; it dissolves it. Like the leap, it is a way of escaping the confrontation rather than sustaining it.
5. The only honest response to a truth is to keep it in view.
6. So the honest response to the absurd is to live, consciously, in its presence. Suicide, like hope, is a form of acceptance: it "settles" the absurd.

The gap is at step 5. The nihilist can grant steps 1–4 and reply: "Good, suicide dissolves the absurd. I did not ask to be lucid; I asked whether life is worth living." Camus needs a value, *lucidity*, that he has not derived from anything, in a book that began by denying that the world supplies values. Two defenses are available.

- **The internal defense.** Anyone who asks "is life worth living?" in earnest has already committed himself to taking the answer seriously, which means to lucidity. The question presupposes the value that answers it (Dialogue 1 tests this).
- **The phenomenological defense.** Camus is describing what it is like to be the kind of creature that cannot stop asking. The demand for clarity is not a choice; it is "the human need." To kill it is not to answer it but to mutilate it. On this reading the argument is less a proof than a refusal to accept a cure that removes the patient.

Neither is a knockdown. But Camus has granted everything the nihilist says about the world and shown that the nihilist's conclusion (death is as good as life) needs a hidden premise: that a life can matter only if the world underwrites it. Drop that premise and the question changes from "is there a meaning?" to "how should one live without one?"

## Revolt, freedom, passion

From the absurd Camus draws three "consequences."

**Revolt.** Revolt is the permanent confrontation between the mind and its own obscurity. It is not hope (which is a leap) and not resignation (which is suicide in slow motion). It is lucid refusal: I will not pretend this makes sense, and I will not stop demanding that it should. Camus compares it to the condemned man's attitude: not giving up, and not pretending the sentence has been commuted.

**Freedom.** The absurd destroys the freedom of the "eternal" kind (the free will that might earn salvation) but delivers another. Before the absurd, a person lives with aims, a concern for the future, a sense of being on a career path; he is a slave to his own hopes. Once he understands that there is no tomorrow in the metaphysical sense, he is released from those rules. Camus compares this to the freedom of the slave of antiquity who no longer belongs to himself, or of the condemned man the morning of the execution: indifferent to everything except the pure flame of life.

**Passion.** If no experience is privileged by an external scale, then what matters is not the *quality* of experience (judged by a scale that does not exist) but its *quantity* and intensity: to live as much and as consciously as possible. Camus's "absurd men" (Don Juan, the actor, the conqueror, the creator) are figures who exhaust the possible rather than aiming at the eternal. Don Juan does not collect women out of a search for the one true love; he loves each one with the same passion because he knows there is no "one."

The epigraph to the book comes from Pindar's third Pythian ode, and states the whole ethic in a line:

> Desire not thou, dear my soul, a life immortal, but use the tools that are to thine hand.
>
> — Pindar, *Pythian Odes* III (tr. Ernest Myers)

The "quantity" ethic is the weakest part of the book, and Camus himself moved away from it. If no scale exists, why is more experience better than less? Why is the conqueror more admirable than the clerk? Camus's later work quietly replaces quantity with something else: solidarity.

## Sisyphus

The final four pages give the book its title. Homer knew Sisyphus as the cleverest of mortals:

> Sisyphus lived, who was the craftiest of all mankind.
>
> — Homer, *Iliad* VI (tr. Samuel Butler)

He betrayed Zeus's secrets, chained up Death so that no one died, and, once dead, talked his way back to the upper world. His punishment is the one Odysseus sees in the underworld:

> And I saw Sisyphus at his endless task raising his prodigious stone with both his hands. With hands and feet he tried to roll it up to the top of the hill, but always, just before he could roll it over on to the other side, its weight would be too much for him, and the pitiless stone would come thundering down again on to the plain. Then he would begin trying to push it up hill again, and the sweat ran off him and the steam rose after him.
>
> — Homer, *Odyssey* XI (tr. Samuel Butler)

The gods thought nothing more dreadful than futile, hopeless labor. Camus is interested in one moment: not the pushing but the walk back down. With nothing to push, Sisyphus has time to think, and he knows exactly what his situation is. The clarity the gods meant as part of the punishment becomes his victory over them: a fate fully seen and scorned no longer rules the one who sees it. Then the last line:

> One must imagine Sisyphus happy.
>
> — Albert Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus*, "The Myth of Sisyphus" (tr. Justin O'Brien)

- **Not "Sisyphus enjoys rolling rocks."** The happiness is that of someone who has stopped expecting the task to mean anything beyond itself. The fate now belongs to Sisyphus; the rock is his affair, not the gods'. The effort of climbing, Camus says, is enough by itself to fill a human heart.
- **"One must imagine."** Camus does not report that Sisyphus is happy. Read it as an imperative (if you can imagine him happy, you can imagine yourself so) or as a near-tautology (a man who has given up hope cannot be crushed by hope's defeat).

Thomas Nagel's "The Absurd" (1971) agrees that life is absurd but relocates the absurdity. It comes not from the world's silence but from our capacity to step back from any commitment and see it *sub specie aeternitatis*, as one more contingent fact about one more organism. We cannot help taking our lives seriously, and we cannot help seeing that there is no ultimate reason to. The absurd is a product of self-consciousness, so Camus's fist-shaking at a universe that cannot notice is romantic and a little self-pitying. The fitting response is *irony*.

Richard Taylor (*Good and Evil*, 1970) offers a third reading. Suppose the gods, in mercy, implant in Sisyphus a compulsive desire to roll stones. Now his life is filled with exactly what he wants. Is it meaningful? Taylor's uncomfortable answer is that this is roughly our situation, and that the meaning of our lives is simply that we want to do what we do. [Chapter 15](15-beyond-nihilism.md) returns to Taylor, Nagel and the meaning-in-life debate.

## *The Stranger*: a man who will not lie

*The Stranger* (*L'Étranger*: also "the foreigner," "the outsider") should be read first as a novel, but it is the absurd lived from the inside.

Meursault, a shipping clerk in Algiers, receives a telegram saying his mother has died; he is unsure whether it was today or yesterday. At the old people's home he declines to view the body, smokes and drinks coffee during the vigil and does not cry. The next day he swims, meets Marie, sees a comedy and sleeps with her. Asked whether he loves her, he says it doesn't mean anything, but probably not. He drifts into helping a neighbor, Raymond, who has beaten his Arab mistress. On a beach, in blinding sun, he meets the woman's brother, and with Raymond's revolver fires once, then four more times into the motionless body.

At the trial the prosecution barely discusses the killing. It discusses the funeral: the coffee, the cigarettes, the lack of tears, the comedy the next day. He is condemned to be beheaded. In his cell a chaplain insists that he must hope, and Meursault explodes for the only time in the book: none of the chaplain's certainties is worth a hair of a woman's head; he at least is sure of his life and his coming death. Afterward, calm under the stars, he opens himself to the indifference of the world, finds it like himself, and realizes that he has been happy and is happy still.

### How to read Meursault

Camus's 1955 preface to an American student edition summarized the novel provocatively: in our society, a man who does not weep at his mother's funeral risks being sentenced to death. Meursault is condemned because he will not play the game; he refuses to say more than he feels. Camus called him, half seriously, "the only Christ we deserve." The reading has been challenged on three fronts.

1. **Lucid or numb?** Sartre's 1943 "Explication de *L'Étranger*" admired the technique: short, disconnected sentences mimicking an experience without causal links. But for most of the book Meursault is not revolting against anything; he is drifting. The awakening comes only under sentence of death.
2. **The man he kills has no name.** He is "the Arab" throughout. Since Conor Cruise O'Brien (1970) and Edward Said's *Culture and Imperialism* (1993), many read this as a colonial symptom: the colonized are scenery, and the court cares more about a Frenchman's manners than an Algerian's death. Kamel Daoud's *The Meursault Investigation* (2013) answers through the victim's brother, who names him Musa.
3. **Is honesty enough?** Meursault never lies. He also helps a pimp humiliate a woman and kills a man, apparently without feeling. If the absurd licenses this, what does it not license? Camus's next major work turns from the solitary absurd man to the plague-stricken city.

## *The Plague*: decency without God

*The Plague* is set in Oran in "194-". Rats come out to die; then people do. The authorities hesitate to name the thing, then close the gates, and for most of a year the city is cut off. It was read at once as an allegory of the Occupation, and since as a book about any collective catastrophe. The characters are positions in an argument about how to act in a meaningless world.

- **Dr. Bernard Rieux**, the narrator, simply treats the sick. Fighting the plague, he says, is not heroism but common decency, which for him means doing his job.
- **Jean Tarrou**, who once watched his prosecutor father demand a death sentence, organizes volunteer sanitary squads and refuses any cause that justifies killing. His question is whether one can be a saint without God.
- **Raymond Rambert**, a journalist trapped in the city, plots for months to escape to the woman he loves, then stays: he would be ashamed to be happy alone.
- **Father Paneloux**, a Jesuit, first preaches that the plague is God's just punishment. After watching a child die in agony, his second sermon says the suffering of a child cannot be understood, only accepted entirely or denied entirely, and the Christian must accept all.
- **Joseph Grand**, a clerk, spends the year rewriting the first sentence of a novel. Rieux calls him the true hero: a man with a little goodness and an apparently absurd ideal, Sisyphus in a municipal office.

The child's death deliberately echoes Ivan Karamazov's argument that no future harmony could justify the torture of one child:

> It's not God that I don't accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket.
>
> — Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book V, ch. 4 (tr. Constance Garnett)

Rieux tells Paneloux that he will refuse to the end to love a scheme of things in which children are tortured. This is not atheism as a metaphysical thesis but *rebellion*: a moral refusal of the world as it is, joined to practical work against suffering inside it. The absurd hero was alone; now revolt is collective, unglamorous labor, and quantity of experience has given way to decency. The book refuses triumph: as the city celebrates, Rieux knows the plague bacillus never dies.

## *The Rebel*: from the absurd to "we"

*The Rebel* (*L'Homme révolté*, 1951) is Camus's longest and most ambitious book, and his most direct engagement with nihilism by name. Its question is this: *The Myth of Sisyphus* asked whether suicide is permitted in an absurd world. In the century of the camps, the more pressing question is whether *murder* is permitted. If nothing has meaning, if no value is given, then why not kill? The essay sets out to show that rebellion, properly understood, itself contains a limit.

### The slave who says no

Camus begins with a phenomenology of revolt. A slave who has taken orders all his life suddenly refuses a new command. What does his "no" mean? It means: this far and no further; you are going too far. In refusing, the slave affirms that there is something in him that is not to be violated, a limit. And he does not merely defend his own interest; he would accept death rather than submit, which means he is defending something he takes to be worth more than his own life. That something is not his alone. In saying no to the master, the slave appeals to a value that he shares with every human being, including those who are not present. Rebellion thus discovers human solidarity. Camus compresses it into a parody of Descartes: *Je me révolte, donc nous sommes*, "I rebel, therefore we exist." What the *cogito* was for the individual mind, revolt is for the human community.

This is the step that carries Camus beyond the absurd. The absurd man found only himself and the silent world. The rebel finds others. And because revolt is founded on a shared limit, it cannot coherently be used to justify crossing that limit. A rebellion that kills indiscriminately betrays its own origin.

### Metaphysical and historical rebellion

Most of the book is a history of how revolt went wrong.

**Metaphysical rebellion** is revolt against the human condition and creation itself. Camus traces it through Sade, the Romantic dandies, Ivan Karamazov, Stirner and Nietzsche. Each begins with a legitimate protest against injustice and ends somewhere darker. Ivan's "everything is lawful" (see [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md)) is the turning point: if God is not, man must take his place, and a man who takes God's place may decide who lives. Dostoevsky saw where this led through Kirillov, the engineer in *The Possessed* who plans to kill himself to prove that he is free:

> If there is no God, then I am God.
>
> — Dostoevsky, *The Possessed*, Part III, ch. 6 (tr. Constance Garnett)

Kirillov's logic, already analyzed in *The Myth of Sisyphus*, is that humanity has lived on a fiction:

> Man has done nothing but invent God so as to go on living, and not kill himself; that's the whole of universal history up till now.
>
> — Dostoevsky, *The Possessed*, Part III, ch. 6 (tr. Constance Garnett)

So the first man to see through the fiction must assert his total freedom at its highest point, by killing himself without any reason at all, "to prove my independence and my new terrible freedom." Camus admires Kirillov's consistency but sees an absurd hero who has drawn the wrong conclusion, taking the absurd as permission rather than as a condition to be lived. Pyotr Verkhovensky, who uses the suicide to cover a political murder, shows how quickly metaphysical revolt becomes political instrument.

**Historical rebellion** is the attempt to realize the rebel's demands in history: the regicide of 1793, the Russian terrorists of the nineteenth century, and finally the totalitarian states of the twentieth. Camus distinguishes *rebellion*, which keeps its limit, from *revolution*, which sacrifices living people to a future totality. His model of a rebel who kept his limit is Ivan Kalyayev, the Socialist Revolutionary who in February 1905 was assigned to throw a bomb at the Grand Duke Sergei's carriage, saw that the Grand Duke's niece and nephew were with him, and did not throw. Two days later he killed the Grand Duke alone, and then accepted his own execution as the price. For Camus, Kalyayev's willingness to die for the murder he committed is a recognition that even justified killing cannot be justified: he paid a life for a life. Camus put him on stage in *The Just Assassins* (1949). The "fastidious assassins" stand in contrast to the revolutionary who kills by the thousands with a clear conscience because history will absolve him.

### Thought at the meridian

The book ends with a call for *measure* (*mesure*), which Camus associates with the Mediterranean, with the Greek goddess Nemesis, and with the noon sun ("the meridian") that casts no shadows. Absolute justice requires the suppression of freedom; absolute freedom permits the strong to crush the weak. Rebellion holds the two in tension, refusing to sacrifice either to the other. It also refuses to sacrifice the present to the future. Real generosity toward the future lies in giving everything to the present.

## The quarrel with Sartre

*The Rebel* attacked the postwar French left, which was close to the Communist Party and often willing to excuse Stalin's crimes as historically necessary. In May 1952 Sartre's *Les Temps modernes* ran a hostile review by Francis Jeanson, who accused Camus of a "Red Cross morality" that stood above history and so served the existing order. Camus replied with a letter addressed, pointedly, to "Monsieur le Directeur." Sartre answered in the same issue, ending a decade of friendship: Camus had become a prisoner of his moralism, refused to dirty his hands, and had never been a rigorous philosopher.

The philosophical question is still live. Sartre held that no one stands outside history: refusing to choose a side is a choice, and moral purity amid oppression is a luxury. Camus held that if the end justifies the means, nothing justifies the end; a cause that requires the camps has refuted itself. On the Soviet camps history sided with Camus. Algeria is harder. Camus, appalled by terrorism on both sides, called for a civilian truce in 1956 and then fell largely silent, while Sartre backed independence. A remark he made in Stockholm in 1957, reported in several versions, is usually summarized as preferring his mother to a justice that planted bombs in the trams of Algiers. Admirers hear a refusal to sacrifice real people to abstractions; critics hear the limits of a *pied-noir* imagination.

[Chapter 11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md) gives Sartre and Beauvoir their own hearing. For the ethics of revolt and its critics, see [learn-ethics, Chapter 16 — Existentialist Ethics](../learn-ethics/16-existentialist-ethics.md).

## Is Camus a nihilist?

- **Yes, at the foundation.** Camus accepts every premise of existential nihilism. In the *Letters to a German Friend* (1943–44) he tells his imaginary Nazi correspondent that they began from the same premise: the world has no ultimate meaning.
- **No, in the conclusion.** The German concluded that everything is permitted and force decides. Camus concluded that something in the world does have meaning, the human being, the only creature that insists on having it. From the absurd, two paths: to the camps, or to solidarity.
- **A third thing.** Camus described *The Rebel* as an attempt to go beyond nihilism with nihilism's own means. Meaning is not given after all; but the shared *demand* for it is a fact with moral consequences. This is usually called *absurdism*.

The deepest worry is the one raised against the suicide argument. Camus derives lucidity, solidarity and measure from the bare structure of a human being facing the world. Can that structure bear so much weight, or are these the values of one man, the poor boy from Belcourt who loved the sea, generalized into a philosophy? He died at 46 without writing the ethics that might have answered.

## Living it

Camus is the most livable philosopher in this course, and the easiest to sentimentalize. Three practices close to the texts:

**The walk back down.** Pick one repetitive task in your week, the commute, the dishes, the inbox. For a week, give your attention to the moment *after* it is done, Sisyphus's descent. Do not try to find a meaning in the task. Just notice that you are the one doing it, that you could refuse, and that you choose not to. Camus's claim is that this small act of lucidity changes the task from a fate into "your thing." At the end of the week, write down whether anything changed, and if not, why Camus might be wrong.

**The Rieux question.** When you next read about a catastrophe you can do nothing about, ask Rieux's question instead of Paneloux's: not "why is this happening?" but "what is the decent thing to do from where I stand?" It may be small: a donation, a phone call, doing your own job well. The exercise trains the move from asking the universe for an explanation to asking yourself for a response.

**The limit.** Camus's rebel discovers a limit in his own refusal. Write down one thing you would refuse to do even if everyone you respected told you it was necessary. Then ask the question Camus asked of Kalyayev: if you ever crossed it, what would you owe? The line between conviction and fanaticism runs through office politics as surely as through revolutions.

## Summary

- Camus accepts the nihilist's premises (no God, no given meaning, death is final) and rejects the nihilist's conclusions (suicide, indifference, "everything is permitted").
- *The Myth of Sisyphus* opens with suicide as the one serious problem. The *absurd* is a relation: the confrontation between the human need for clarity and the unreasonable silence of the world. It is felt before it is thought (weariness, time, strangeness).
- *Philosophical suicide*: Jaspers, Shestov, Kierkegaard and Husserl acknowledge the absurd and then leap beyond it. Physical suicide is also a way of dissolving the absurd. Camus's alternative is to live it lucidly. The argument relies on an underived value of lucidity.
- The three consequences are *revolt*, *freedom* and *passion*. "One must imagine Sisyphus happy": his fate belongs to him. Nagel replies that the absurd comes from our capacity for detachment and calls for irony, not scorn; Taylor asks whether a desire implanted by the gods would make the labor meaningful.
- *The Stranger*: Meursault is condemned for not playing society's game; he refuses to lie and at the end opens himself to the indifference of the world. Critics question his lucidity and the novel's erasure of the Arab victim (Daoud, Said).
- *The Plague* moves from the solitary absurd to collective revolt: Rieux's decency, Tarrou's sanctity without God, the child's death echoing Ivan Karamazov.
- *The Rebel* asks whether murder is permitted. The rebel's "no" affirms a shared limit: "I rebel, therefore we exist." Metaphysical rebellion (Ivan, Kirillov, Nietzsche) and historical revolution lose this limit. Kalyayev is the model of rebellion with a limit; *measure* is the meridian ethic.
- The 1952 quarrel with Sartre turned on whether one can refuse revolutionary violence without serving oppression. Camus is best read as an *absurdist*: a nihilist about the world, but not about human beings.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether the absurd argues against suicide

**Socrates:** You have read the essay. Camus says the absurd is a relation, and that killing oneself destroys one of its terms. Do you think that is a reason not to do it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I think it is. If the absurd is the clash between me and the world, suicide doesn't answer the clash, it just removes me from it. It's running away, not responding.

**Socrates:** And is running away always wrong?

**You:** No. You run away from a burning building.

**Socrates:** Then why not from a burning life? Suppose a man says: "Camus has persuaded me. The absurd will last as long as I do. I have looked at it lucidly for forty years, and I am tired." What does Camus say to him?

**You:** That the lucidity is the value. That you should keep looking.

**Socrates:** Where does Camus find that value? In the world?

**You:** No, he says the world is silent.

**Socrates:** In God?

**You:** No.

**Socrates:** Then in the man himself. It is the man's own demand for clarity. But our tired man says: "I demanded clarity. The world refused it. I have done my duty to the demand. Why must I go on demanding forever?"

**You:** Maybe Camus would say the demand isn't optional. It's what a human being is. You can't stop asking any more than you can stop breathing.

**Socrates:** But the tired man proposes to stop breathing as well. You have shown that the demand is natural, not that it is binding. Is everything natural to us binding on us?

**You:** No. Anger is natural.

**Socrates:** So the argument needs something more. Let me offer Camus a better one, and you tell me whether he would accept it. Anyone who asks seriously, "Is life worth living?", has already committed himself to taking the answer seriously, has he not?

**You:** Yes, otherwise he wouldn't be asking.

**Socrates:** And to take an answer seriously is to want to see clearly. So the asker already values lucidity. He cannot use the question to abolish the value the question presupposes.

**You:** That's better. But it only binds people who ask the question philosophically. Most people who kill themselves aren't conducting an inquiry. They're in pain.

**Socrates:** Then Camus's book is not addressed to them, and he would be foolish to pretend otherwise. Perhaps it is addressed to the person who thinks that the *philosophical* conclusion of nihilism is death, and who would otherwise be tempted to believe that pain had logic on its side.

**You:** So the argument doesn't prove you must live. It shows that nihilism doesn't prove you must die.

**Socrates:** Is that a small result?

**You:** No. For someone who thought the second was proved, it's everything.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether revolt contains its own limit

**Jean-Paul Sartre:** Your friend Camus says that the rebel's "no" discovers a limit, a value shared by all, and that revolution betrays it. I say that is a beautiful soul's morality, written by a man who wants clean hands. Defend him.

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Sartre in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** The slave who refuses an order is willing to die rather than obey. That means he values something more than his life, and it isn't just his own interest. It's something like human dignity, which every human shares. So if he goes on to murder freely, he contradicts the reason he rebelled.

**Jean-Paul Sartre:** Charming. But the slave has a master. Does the slave's limit forbid him to kill the master?

**You:** Not necessarily. Camus admires Kalyayev, who killed the Grand Duke.

**Sartre:** So killing is allowed. Then the limit is not "never kill." What is it?

**You:** Not to kill innocents, like the children in the carriage. And to pay for it yourself, to accept that even justified killing leaves a debt.

**Sartre:** And who decides who is innocent? The Grand Duke's children are innocent; are his soldiers? His tax collectors? The workers who build his palaces?

**You:** That's the hard part. Camus doesn't give a formula.

**Sartre:** Of course not, because there is none. Every line is drawn by someone, in a situation, for reasons. Your Camus pretends his line is drawn by human nature. I say he draws it himself, and then pretends it was given, which is bad faith.

**You:** But you also draw lines. You'd condemn the camps.

**Sartre:** I condemn them as a man engaged in history, choosing a future against another future. I do not pretend I stand above history, judging both sides from the meridian.

**You:** Then what do you say to the person who's told that the camps are necessary for the future?

**Sartre:** I say: show me. Then I judge, and I am responsible for my judgment.

**You:** Camus would say that by the time you've been shown, it's too late. People who justify the camps always have a future to point to. Having a limit in advance is the only protection against being talked into anything.

**Sartre:** A limit in advance is a rule. Rules are excuses. They let you avoid choosing.

**You:** Or they're what you've already chosen, so that you don't have to choose again under pressure. Isn't that what commitment means? You of all people should accept that.

**Sartre:** Hm. You are better at this than he was.

**You:** I'm not sure. I think you were both right about something. You're right that no line is written in nature. He's right that people who have no line are dangerous.

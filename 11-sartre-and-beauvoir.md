# Chapter 11 — Sartre and Beauvoir

In the winter of 1945 the word *existentialism* was suddenly everywhere in Paris: in newspapers, in cafés, in sermons, in the mouths of people who had never opened a philosophy book. Its two most famous representatives were a couple who never married, never lived together, and wrote in the same cafés on Saint-Germain-des-Prés: Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir. Their critics, Catholic and Communist alike, agreed about one thing: existentialism was nihilism in a black turtleneck. It taught that there is no God and no human nature, that values are invented, and that life is "a useless passion." Sartre and Beauvoir answered that they were the first philosophers to take human freedom seriously, and that a philosophy which strips away every excuse is the opposite of nihilism. This chapter asks who was right. It reads Sartre's novel *Nausea* as an experience of contingency, *Being and Nothingness* as a theory of where nothingness comes from, and Beauvoir's *Pyrrhus and Cineas* and *The Ethics of Ambiguity* as the most serious attempt in the existentialist tradition to build an ethics without foundations. Previous: [Chapter 10 — Camus and the Absurd](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md). Next: [Chapter 12 — Moral Nihilism and Error Theory](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md).

This chapter is coordinated with [learn-ethics, Chapter 16 — Existentialist Ethics](../learn-ethics/16-existentialist-ethics.md), which covers *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, the student's dilemma, bad faith and Beauvoir's gallery of evasions in detail. Here the emphasis is on nothingness, contingency, and whether a philosophy of radical freedom is a form of nihilism or a cure for it.

## Two agrégés at the café

Sartre was born in Paris in 1905. Fatherless before he was two, he grew up among his grandfather's books; his memoir *The Words* (1963) portrays a child who decided that only writing could justify his existence. Beauvoir was born in 1908 into a Catholic family of declining fortune. She lost her faith at fourteen and resolved to be a philosopher. They met in 1929, studying for the *agrégation*, the national competitive exam for philosophy teachers. Sartre placed first and Beauvoir second; at 21 she was one of the youngest ever to pass it. They made a pact: theirs would be the "essential" love, others "contingent." It lasted, with much damage to third parties, until Sartre's death.

In 1933–34 Sartre spent a year at the French Institute in Berlin reading Husserl and, more cautiously, Heidegger. He returned with phenomenology: the method of describing experience exactly as it presents itself, without assuming any theory about what lies behind it. His novel *Nausea* (1938) applied that method to the experience of existence itself. After a year as a prisoner of war he published *Being and Nothingness* (1943); Beauvoir published *Pyrrhus and Cineas* in 1944.

After the Liberation they became celebrities. Sartre's lecture *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (October 1945) was mobbed, and they founded *Les Temps modernes*. Beauvoir wrote *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947), then *The Second Sex* (1949), which made her the founding theorist of modern feminism. Sartre moved toward Marxism, broke with Camus in 1952 (see [Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)), wrote the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960) and refused the Nobel Prize in 1964 on the grounds that a writer should not let himself be turned into an institution. Beauvoir died in 1986 and is buried beside him in Montparnasse.

## *Nausea*: the discovery of contingency

*Nausea* is the diary of Antoine Roquentin, a thirty-year-old who has settled in Bouville ("Mudtown," modeled on Le Havre) to write a biography of an eighteenth-century adventurer, the Marquis de Rollebon. Something is happening to him. A pebble on the beach feels sickening in his hand. A beer glass, the suspenders of a café owner, his own hand on the table become strange, obscene, too much there. He calls the feeling the Nausea.

The climax comes in a public park. Roquentin is sitting on a bench, looking at the root of a chestnut tree, and suddenly understands. The categories we use to tame the world ("root," "black," "tree," "bench") slide off things like labels. Underneath them is just *existence*: a soft, monstrous, shapeless mass, with no reason to be there. Nothing that exists has to exist. Everything is *de trop*, superfluous, in excess. Sartre gives Roquentin the formula:

> Every existing thing is born without reason, prolongs itself out of weakness, and dies by chance.
>
> — Jean-Paul Sartre, *Nausea* (tr. Lloyd Alexander)

The word that sums it up is *absurdity*: not a logical contradiction but something that simply is, without a why. A circle is not absurd, Roquentin notes; it is explained by the rotation of a segment. But a circle doesn't exist. The root exists, and cannot be explained.

This is metaphysical rather than moral nihilism, and it is worth distinguishing it from Camus's absurd ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)), with which it is often confused. For Camus, the absurd is a *relation* between a mind that demands meaning and a world that does not supply it. For Roquentin, absurdity belongs to *existence itself*: the world is gratuitous, unjustified, sheerly there. Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason is not merely unproved; it is experienced as false. "Why is there something rather than nothing?" has no answer, not because it is hidden but because there isn't one.

Roquentin's discovery destroys three ways of hiding from it, and each is a recognizable modern consolation.

- **History.** His biography of Rollebon was a way of borrowing a life: of feeling that his own existence was justified by resurrecting someone else's. He abandons it. The past does not exist; only things exist, and they don't need him.
- **Humanism.** The Self-Taught Man (*l'Autodidacte*) is reading his way through the municipal library in alphabetical order and declares his love of humanity. Roquentin finds him repulsive, and Sartre's satire is savage: the humanist loves "Man" in general, an abstraction, and so sentimentalizes away the brute existence of actual people. (Sartre would later call his own 1945 lecture a defense of humanism; he never resolved the tension.)
- **Adventures and perfect moments.** Roquentin's former lover Anny has spent her life trying to create "perfect moments," situations shaped like art, with a beginning, middle and end. Roquentin had believed in "adventures." Both are attempts to give life the necessity of a story. But lived time is not a story; stories are told backward, from their endings.

The novel ends on an ambiguous note of hope. In the café Roquentin listens to a ragtime record, "Some of These Days," and feels that the melody does not *exist* in the way the root does: it is *beyond* existence, necessary, each note following from the last. He imagines the songwriter and the singer justified, a little, by having made it. Perhaps he could write a novel, a story "beautiful and hard as steel," that would make people ashamed of their existence, and through which he could accept his own. The way out of nausea is not a discovery but a creation.

## *Being and Nothingness*: where nothingness comes from

*Being and Nothingness* (1943) is long, dense and brilliant, and much of it is beyond this course. But its core is a theory of nothingness that directly answers Heidegger's lecture "What Is Metaphysics?" ([Chapter 9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md)) and reshapes the meaning of nihilism.

### Two kinds of being

Sartre distinguishes two regions of being.

- **Being-in-itself** (*l'être-en-soi*) is the being of things: the root, the pebble, the inkwell. It simply is what it is: full, solid, without any gap, without relation to itself. It is, in the novel's term, *de trop*.
- **Being-for-itself** (*l'être-pour-soi*) is the being of consciousness. Consciousness is always consciousness *of* something other than itself; it is never simply what it is. It is a kind of hole or gap in being, a perpetual distance from itself.

Sartre's provocative formula is that the for-itself "is what it is not and is not what it is." You are never identical to your past (you can always take it up differently), never identical to your roles (you play them), never identical even to your present emotions (you are aware of them, and that awareness is not the emotion). This non-coincidence with oneself is what Sartre calls *nothingness*, and it is the same thing as freedom.

### Pierre is not in the café

The most famous example in the book is a small one. I have an appointment with Pierre at four o'clock. I arrive fifteen minutes late. Pierre is always punctual; will he have waited? I look around the café: the mirrors, the smoke, the customers, the waiters. Each of these, as I scan them, dissolves into background; each face is checked and found to be not-Pierre. The whole café organizes itself as the ground against which Pierre's absence appears. Pierre's absence is not a thought I have *about* the café; it haunts the café; I *see* it.

"Wellington is not in the café" is also true, but it is an empty judgment. Pierre's absence is an *event*, because I was expecting him. Sartre's conclusion is that there are real negativities in the world (absences, lacks, distances, destructions, which he calls *négatités*), but that they appear only for a being that questions, expects and projects. A storm does not destroy anything by itself; it redistributes masses of matter. Destruction exists only for a being who cares that the city was there.

Hence the central thesis: *nothingness comes into the world through human reality.* Heidegger had said that "the nothing itself nihilates," as if nothingness were an independent power that anxiety discloses. Sartre relocates it. Nothingness is not out there in the void; it is in the heart of the being who questions.

This changes the shape of nihilism. If nothingness is the structure of consciousness, then the "nothing" that the nihilist discovers at the bottom of the world is not a property of the world at all. It is himself. The world was always plenitude without lack, *de trop*. The values that seem to vanish were never in things; they were in the projects of the for-itself, which projected them onto things. To discover that "nothing has value in itself" is just to discover what the for-itself is: the being through which value enters the world.

### Anguish and the vertigo of freedom

Anguish (*angoisse*) is the reflective awareness of this freedom. Sartre's examples are better known than his definitions.

- **The precipice.** Walking along a narrow path above a drop, I feel fear: I might slip. But then I feel something else: nothing prevents me from *throwing* myself over. The vertigo is not fear of falling; it is anguish before my own possibility. My resolution to be careful is not a cause that guarantees my behavior; it is a possibility I must take up again at each step.
- **The gambler.** A man has sincerely resolved never to gamble again. He approaches the gaming table, and his resolution, "all intact," is suddenly useless. It is there, but it does not bind; it belongs to the past self that made it. He must make it again, now, from nothing. That is anguish.

Anguish is not the feeling that the world is meaningless. It is the feeling that *nothing* stands between me and my choices: no nature, no past, no resolution, no value, that does the choosing for me. The nihilist's discovery and the existentialist's freedom are the same discovery seen from two sides.

### Bad faith, briefly

Most of the time we flee anguish. Sartre calls this flight *bad faith* (*mauvaise foi*): a lie to oneself, in which one takes oneself to be a thing (a waiter, a coward, a woman whose hand is merely resting in her companion's) in order to escape the burden of being free. The examples of the café waiter and the woman on a date are discussed in [learn-ethics, Chapter 16](../learn-ethics/16-existentialist-ethics.md). For our purposes the point is that bad faith is the existentialist's diagnosis of *passive nihilism*: the person who says "nothing matters, so I'll just go along" is not facing the void but hiding from it in the role of a thing to which nothing can be imputed.

### A useless passion

Why do we flee? Because the for-itself is a lack, and every lack wants to be filled. Consciousness desires to be both free *and* solid, both a for-itself *and* an in-itself: a being that would be its own foundation, fully what it is and yet conscious of being it. That combination, the *in-itself-for-itself*, is precisely what theologians mean by God. So, Sartre concludes, the fundamental human project is to be God. And since the combination is contradictory (consciousness can never coincide with itself without ceasing to be consciousness), the project must fail. The section on "existential psychoanalysis" ends:

> Man is a useless passion.
>
> — Jean-Paul Sartre, *Being and Nothingness*, Part Four, ch. 2, § I (tr. Hazel E. Barnes)

On this point Sartre looks like the most thoroughgoing nihilist of the century. Every human desire, at bottom, aims at something impossible. It amounts to the same thing, he adds a few pages later, whether one gets drunk alone or is a leader of nations. Yet the book ends not with despair but with a promise: an ethics, which would consider what freedom becomes once it ceases to aim at being God and instead takes itself as its own value. The promised ethics never appeared in Sartre's lifetime. His notes for it were published after his death as *Notebooks for an Ethics* (1983). The person who actually wrote the existentialist ethics was Beauvoir.

## "Existence precedes essence": the charge of nihilism

In *Existentialism Is a Humanism* Sartre compressed the theory into a slogan. For a manufactured object, such as a paper knife, essence precedes existence: the artisan has a concept of the knife before he makes it. If God created human beings, the same would be true of us: a divine idea of human nature would precede each person. But if there is no God, there is at least one being whose existence comes before its essence: the human being, who first exists, turns up in the world, and only afterward defines himself by what he does. There is no human nature, because there is no God to conceive it.

The consequences for value follow directly. There is no table of values written in heaven, and no human nature from which values could be read off. Dostoevsky had made Ivan Karamazov's associates draw the frightening conclusion; Rakitin sums up Ivan's theory with contempt:

> if there's no immortality of the soul, then there's no virtue, and everything is lawful.
>
> — Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book II, ch. 7 (tr. Constance Garnett)

Sartre calls this the starting point of existentialism. (His version, "If God did not exist, everything would be permitted," is a paraphrase that appears nowhere in Dostoevsky in that form; see [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md).) Everything is permitted, and so man is forlorn: he can find nothing to depend on, within or outside himself, and has no excuses.

Critics on both sides heard this as nihilism, and said so.

- **Catholic critics** (Gabriel Marcel among them) charged that if values are chosen, a Nazi's are as valid as a resistance fighter's.
- **Communist critics** saw a bourgeois philosophy of the isolated individual wallowing in anguish; Georg Lukács's *Existentialism or Marxism?* (1947) diagnosed the death-throes of a class.
- **Heidegger** replied, in the "Letter on Humanism" (1946), that Sartre had merely inverted the old metaphysical claim. To say that existence precedes essence is still to think in terms of essence and existence, the categories of the metaphysics whose culmination is nihilism ([Chapter 9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md)). Reversing a metaphysical statement remains a metaphysical statement.

Sartre's answer, in the lecture and after, has three parts. First, to choose is always to affirm the value of what one chooses; one cannot choose evil *as* evil. Second, in choosing, I choose an image of what a human being ought to be, and so legislate for everyone. Third, the freedom that grounds all values must itself be willed as a value: once a person sees that values depend on him, he can will only freedom, his own and that of others. Whether these steps work is examined in [learn-ethics, Chapter 16](../learn-ethics/16-existentialist-ethics.md). Here notice the shape of the reply. Sartre does not deny that values lack foundations. He denies that a lack of foundations makes them *arbitrary*, because the very structure of choosing commits the chooser to something.

Two further criticisms bear directly on nihilism.

- **Iris Murdoch** (*Sartre: Romantic Rationalist*, 1953; "The Idea of Perfection," 1964) objected that Sartre's picture of the moral agent as a solitary will choosing in a void misdescribes moral life. We do not leap from nothing; we see situations more or less truly, and moral change is mostly a slow work of attention. If Murdoch is right, the "void" from which existentialist choice springs is an artifact of a bad theory of the self.
- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty** (*Phenomenology of Perception*, 1945) objected that freedom is never absolute because it is always embodied and situated. A person tired from a long climb cannot simply decide that a rock is surmountable; his freedom works within a "field" of meanings that he did not create. Sartre himself, under pressure from Beauvoir and Marxism, came to say that freedom is always freedom in a situation, and that some situations crush it.

## Beauvoir: *Pyrrhus and Cineas*

Beauvoir's first philosophical essay takes its title from an anecdote in Plutarch. King Pyrrhus of Epirus is preparing to invade Italy. His adviser Cineas asks him a series of questions.

> And having subdued Italy, what shall we do next?
>
> — Plutarch, "Life of Pyrrhus" (tr. John Dryden, rev. Arthur Hugh Clough)

Sicily, says Pyrrhus. And then Libya and Carthage, and then Macedon and all of Greece. Cineas keeps asking. At last:

> and when all these are in our power, what shall we do then?"  Said Pyrrhus, smiling, "we will live at our ease, my dear friend, and drink all day, and divert ourselves with pleasant conversation."
>
> — Plutarch, "Life of Pyrrhus" (tr. John Dryden, rev. Arthur Hugh Clough)

To which Cineas makes the obvious reply:

> And what hinders us now, sir, if we have a mind to be merry, and entertain one another, since we have at hand without trouble all those necessary things, to which through much blood and great labor, and infinite hazards and mischief done to ourselves and to others, we design at last to arrive?
>
> — Plutarch, "Life of Pyrrhus" (tr. John Dryden, rev. Arthur Hugh Clough)

It troubled Pyrrhus but did not change his plans. Cineas's question is the nihilist's in its most portable form: *and then what?* If the point of action is the state at the end, why not skip to the end? And if there is no end, why begin?

Beauvoir's answer is the founding move of her ethics. Cineas assumes that a project is worth only what it *arrives at*, so that its value lies in a final state of rest. But for a being like us, who exists by surpassing every given situation toward a future (Sartre's *transcendence*), there is no final state. The value of a project is not deposited at the finish line; it lies in the surpassing itself. Pyrrhus at rest would not be Pyrrhus fulfilled; he would be Pyrrhus dead. Cineas is right that conquest is futile if its point is rest. He is wrong that the alternative is to do nothing, since doing nothing is also a project, and a poorer one.

But this answer raises a second question, and it is the one that makes Beauvoir more than Sartre's disciple. If every project is surpassed, why does any project matter? Here Beauvoir rejects both of the traditional consolations.

- **Not God.** A God would either be indifferent to my acts or have already willed everything; either way my acts would add nothing to him.
- **Not "Humanity."** Like the Self-Taught Man's, this is an abstraction, and an abstraction cannot receive anything from me.

What remains is *other people*: particular, free, finite others, who can take up my projects, carry them further, turn them to new ends, or reject them. My act matters because it makes a difference to the situation of others, and others can make something of it. A project that no other freedom ever took up would be like a word spoken in an empty room. So my freedom *needs* the freedom of others, not as a moral add-on but as a condition of my actions mattering at all. And it needs them to be in a position to take up what I do: healthy, educated, not crushed by want or oppression. Beauvoir's ethics of liberation begins here, in a reply to Cineas.

## Beauvoir: *All Men Are Mortal*

Beauvoir's 1946 novel tests the argument with a thought experiment. Raimon Fosca, a thirteenth-century Italian prince, drinks an elixir and becomes immortal. He sets out to build a just and lasting city, then to unify Italy, then to serve the Emperor Charles V and unite the world, then to help the poor of Paris through the Revolution. Each project fails, or succeeds and is undone. Over centuries he discovers that nothing he does can matter to him the way it matters to mortals, because for him nothing is ever at stake: he can always try again. His love affairs, his friendships, his causes all wear thin. The actress who frames the story wants his love so as to live forever in his memory, and despairs on learning she is one of countless others.

The novel is an argument in narrative form, and it anticipates the philosopher Bernard Williams's famous essay "The Makropulos Case" (1973), which argued that an immortal life would necessarily become boring and meaningless. Beauvoir's point is slightly different. It is *finitude* (the fact that time runs out, that choices exclude alternatives, that others die) which makes action weighty. The nihilist complains that because we die, nothing matters. Fosca shows the reverse: if we did not die, nothing would.

Montaigne's essay on death, which Beauvoir set at the head of *The Ethics of Ambiguity*, puts the same thought from the other end:

> The perpetual work of your life is but to lay the foundation of death.
>
> — Montaigne, *Essays*, Book I, ch. 19 (tr. Charles Cotton)

## Beauvoir: *The Ethics of Ambiguity*

*The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947) is the existentialist ethics Sartre promised. It begins by accepting the worst Sartre had said: man is a useless passion, the project to be God fails. Beauvoir asks what follows. Her answer is that the failure is not a refutation but the very condition of ethics. Only a being that is *not* already what it ought to be, that lacks its own foundation, can have something to do. A God could forgive and make good our faults; without one, Beauvoir observes, nothing can undo what we have done, and that is exactly why what we do matters. Ethics exists because we are not gods.

### Ambiguity

The human condition is *ambiguous*. We are free consciousness and also bodies, objects in the world; we are sovereign subjects and also things for others; we live the present and are already part of the past. Every philosophy that has tried to escape this ambiguity, by reducing us to pure spirit or to pure matter, has also, Beauvoir argues, escaped ethics. Existentialism's virtue is to refuse the escape. We must assume our ambiguity, not dissolve it.

### The nihilist as a disappointed serious man

Beauvoir's typology of attitudes (sub-man, serious man, nihilist, adventurer, passionate man) is discussed in [learn-ethics, Chapter 16](../learn-ethics/16-existentialist-ethics.md). Her account of the nihilist is worth isolating here, because it is one of the sharpest psychological analyses of nihilism in this course.

The nihilist, Beauvoir says, is a *serious man* who has been disappointed. The serious man had subordinated his freedom to an absolute value: the Party, the Nation, the Church, Science, the Family. He believed the value was given, not chosen, and so felt no responsibility for it. Then the absolute collapsed: the Party betrayed him, the Church lost its authority, the war made the Nation's claims absurd. The nihilist concludes that since his idol was not absolute, *nothing* is worth anything. But this conclusion, Beauvoir points out, still accepts the serious man's premise: that a value must be given from outside or it is nothing. The nihilist is still waiting for an absolute; he simply sulks because it has not arrived. Real freedom would drop the premise.

Beauvoir is careful not to dismiss him. The nihilist's refusal contains a truth the serious man ignored: no value is given. Nihilism is a moment in the conquest of freedom, a necessary disillusionment, and Beauvoir notes that many of the young people of her generation passed through it. It becomes a vice only when it is made into a resting place, when the denial of all value becomes a new excuse for doing nothing, or (in its active form) for destroying. She cites the young men who joined fascist movements out of a nihilist taste for destruction: having found nothing worth affirming, they affirmed the act of denial itself.

### To will oneself free

The positive argument of the book develops the reply to Cineas. My freedom is not a thing I possess but a movement toward the future, and that future is always shared with others. To will freedom genuinely, and not merely to use the word, I must will the conditions under which freedom can be exercised, and those conditions include the freedom of others. Beauvoir's formula is the hinge of existentialist ethics:

> To will oneself free is also to will others free.
>
> — Simone de Beauvoir, *The Ethics of Ambiguity*, Part II (tr. Bernard Frechtman)

This yields concrete conclusions that Sartre's lecture had not reached. Oppression is evil not because it violates a law written in heaven but because it reduces free beings to things and cuts off the future in which my own projects could be taken up. The oppressor attacks the condition of all meaningful action, including his own. And the oppressed are not simply "free to choose" in Sartre's absolute sense. A slave, or a woman confined to the household in a society that offers her no other prospect, lives in a world whose horizons have been closed by others. Her apparent acceptance is not bad faith; it is the effect of a situation. (Two years later this analysis became the backbone of *The Second Sex*.)

Is this a successful answer to the moral nihilist? It has the same structure as Sartre's: it does not supply an external foundation but argues that anyone who values anything at all is already committed, by the structure of valuing, to valuing freedom, and that valuing freedom coherently means valuing others' freedom. The weak point is the step from "my projects need others to take them up" to "I must will that all others be free." A tyrant's projects need others too, as subjects, functionaries, admirers. Beauvoir would reply that the tyrant needs others only as instruments, and that a project taken up by instruments is not taken up at all; only free recognition confers meaning. That reply is powerful, but it depends on a thick claim about what makes an action *meaningful*, and the nihilist may simply decline the claim. [Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) asks what is left when that happens.

## Were they nihilists?

Lay the evidence side by side.

| Nihilist-sounding claim | Sartre or Beauvoir | What they meant |
|---|---|---|
| Existence is gratuitous, unjustified, *de trop* | *Nausea* | There is no sufficient reason for anything; metaphysical rationalism is false |
| Nothingness is at the heart of human being | *Being and Nothingness* | Consciousness is never coincident with itself; this gap is freedom |
| There is no human nature and no given value | *Existentialism Is a Humanism* | Values exist only through human projects |
| "Man is a useless passion" | *Being and Nothingness* | The project to be self-founding (God) necessarily fails |
| Every project is surpassed; "and then what?" | *Pyrrhus and Cineas* | Value lies in surpassing, and in others taking up what we do |

In each row the nihilist premise is accepted and a non-nihilist conclusion is drawn. That is the existentialist strategy, the same one Camus used with the absurd: accept the negation of foundations, deny that this negation is the last word. Whether the strategy works depends on a single question, which every reader must answer for herself: can a value that depends entirely on being chosen still *bind* the one who chose it? Sartre's answer was yes, because choosing is not like picking an item off a shelf; it is a commitment that constitutes who you are. Beauvoir's answer was yes, because no one chooses alone. Their critics' answer was no. [Chapter 15](15-beyond-nihilism.md) returns to the question with the tools of the meaning-in-life debate.

## Living it

**The café test.** Next time you wait for someone who is late, notice Pierre's absence: how the room organizes itself around a person who is not there. Then notice that the absence exists only because you expected someone. Sartre's claim is that every lack you perceive in the world, including the lack of meaning, has the same structure. For one day, each time you think "something is missing," ask: *missing for whose project?*

**The Cineas question.** Pick one major project in your life (a degree, a career goal, a relationship, a move). Ask Cineas's question repeatedly: and then what? Write the chain until you reach the place where you would rest. Then ask what stops you from resting now. Beauvoir's prediction is that you will find that the value of the project was never at the end of the chain. If she is right, the exercise should change not *whether* you pursue the project but *how*: less as a means to a future state, more as a movement whose meaning is in the doing and in the people it reaches.

**Who takes it up?** For one project you care about, list the particular people who will receive, continue, use or contest it. If the list is empty, Beauvoir would say the project is a word spoken in an empty room. If the list is full of people who are not free to take it up (who are too poor, too busy, too constrained), she would say that your project is limited by their situation, and that working on their situation is part of working on your project.

## Summary

- Sartre and Beauvoir were accused of nihilism by Catholics and Communists alike; they claimed to be its antidote, a philosophy of freedom without excuses.
- *Nausea*: Roquentin discovers *contingency* in the chestnut root. Existence is gratuitous and *de trop*; absurdity belongs to existence itself (unlike Camus's relational absurd). History, humanism and "perfect moments" are exposed as consolations; the novel ends with the hope of creation.
- *Being and Nothingness*: *being-in-itself* (things) and *being-for-itself* (consciousness). Nothingness comes into the world through human reality (Pierre's absence from the café), against Heidegger's "the nothing nihilates." Nothingness is freedom; *anguish* is the awareness of it (the precipice, the gambler); *bad faith* is flight from it.
- The fundamental project is to be God (in-itself-for-itself); it is contradictory, so "man is a useless passion." The promised ethics never came from Sartre.
- "Existence precedes essence": no God, no human nature, no given values. Critics: Catholics (arbitrariness), Lukács (bourgeois decadence), Heidegger (inverted metaphysics), Murdoch (the empty will), Merleau-Ponty (situated freedom).
- Beauvoir's *Pyrrhus and Cineas* answers "and then what?": value lies in surpassing, and in others taking up our acts; neither God nor Humanity can confer it.
- *All Men Are Mortal*: immortality would drain action of weight; finitude makes things matter.
- *The Ethics of Ambiguity*: failure is the condition of ethics; the *nihilist* is a disappointed serious man still waiting for an absolute; "to will oneself free is also to will others free"; oppression attacks the conditions of all meaning.

### Dialogue 1 — On "and then what?"

**Cineas:** I hear you are working very hard at your studies. Good. What will you do when you have your degree?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Cineas in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Get a job, ideally one I find interesting. Something in research, maybe.

**Cineas:** And when you have the job?

**You:** Do it well. Get better at it. Earn enough to live comfortably, travel, maybe have a family.

**Cineas:** And when you have the family and the comfort and the travel?

**You:** I suppose I'd want to be content. Enjoy it. Spend time with people I love.

**Cineas:** You already have people you love. What prevents you from spending time with them now, instead of working through the night?

**You:** That's the Pyrrhus trick. But it isn't fair. I can't enjoy the things I want later without doing the work now. You need money to travel.

**Cineas:** Very well, but you did not say "travel" at the end. You said "content." Is contentment also bought with money?

**You:** Not exactly. Partly it comes from having done something worthwhile.

**Cineas:** Then the work is not only a means to contentment. It is part of it.

**You:** I suppose so. Some of it, anyway.

**Cineas:** And when you are content, what will you do?

**You:** I don't know. Keep going, I guess. People don't actually stop.

**Cineas:** So there is no final "then," where you rest and the point of everything is collected at last?

**You:** No. That's what Beauvoir says. The value isn't at the end; it's in the surpassing.

**Cineas:** But that is what troubles me. If there is no end, then every project is a step toward another step. Is that not the very image of futility?

**You:** Only if you think a step is worth something only because of where it leads. A conversation doesn't lead anywhere, and it's still worth having. Your king wanted to end with conversation; he could have had it all along.

**Cineas:** You are turning my own argument against me.

**You:** Beauvoir does. She says you're right that conquest for the sake of rest is absurd, but wrong that the alternative is to do nothing. Doing nothing is also a project, just a poorer one.

**Cineas:** And how will you know which projects are poorer and which richer, without some final end to measure them by?

**You:** By whether they open things up for other people, or close them down. Your king's wars closed everything down. They burned Italy so that he could drink at leisure.

**Cineas:** That is a measure I did not think of. Plutarch says my king was troubled by my questions but did not change his mind. Will you?

**You:** Not about studying. But maybe about why.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether you could have done otherwise

**Jean-Paul Sartre:** You told me you gave up on the hike yesterday, two hours before the summit, because you were too tired. Could you have gone on?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Sartre in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** No. I was exhausted. My legs were shaking. It wasn't a choice; my body gave out.

**Sartre:** Your friends were also tired. Did they stop?

**You:** No, they went on. But they're fitter than me.

**Sartre:** Perhaps. Or perhaps they lived their fatigue differently. For one person, fatigue is an enemy to be beaten. For another it is a signal, the welcome sign that he can stop. The muscles may be the same; the meaning is not. Where does the meaning come from?

**You:** From me, I suppose. But I don't choose it. I just felt it.

**Sartre:** Did you choose to start the hike?

**You:** Yes.

**Sartre:** And why did you start it?

**You:** Because my friends were going. I didn't want to be left out.

**Sartre:** And why did you stop?

**You:** Because I was tired. And... because I didn't really care about the summit. I cared about being with them, and by then they'd already gone ahead.

**Sartre:** So your fatigue had a history. It belonged to a way of being with others, of letting them carry the meaning of the day. Could you have gone on?

**You:** Physically, maybe, yes. But it would have cost a lot.

**Sartre:** That is my answer too. You could have done otherwise, but at what price? To go on, you would have had to become someone for whom the summit mattered. That is not a small change. It touches your whole way of being in the world.

**You:** Then you're not saying I'm free in the sense of being able to do anything at any moment.

**Sartre:** I am saying that what you call your nature, your tiredness, your character, is not a thing that happens to you. It is a project you are living, and could live otherwise. That is anguish: no fact about you settles it.

**You:** Merleau-Ponty would say you're ignoring the body. My legs really were shaking.

**Sartre:** He would, and he would have a point. Freedom is always freedom in a situation, and shaking legs are a situation. But notice: you did not tell me "my legs shook, and so I stopped." You told me "I was too tired." The word "too" is not in your legs.

**You:** Then what's the nihilism here? You haven't told me anything is meaningless.

**Sartre:** Only this: the summit had no value in itself, and neither did rest. Each was worth what your project made it worth. Some find that terrifying. I find it the only dignity we have.

# Chapter 12 — Moral Nihilism and Error Theory

Most people who fear nihilism fear one thing above all: that if there is no God, or no objective morality, then nothing is really wrong, and so (they assume) anything goes. This chapter treats that fear with the tools of twentieth-century analytic philosophy, which turned "is anything really wrong?" from a cry of anguish into a thesis that could be stated precisely, argued for and argued against. The thesis is *moral nihilism*: there are no moral facts, so no moral claim is true. Its most developed form is J. L. Mackie's *error theory*, which says that ordinary moral thought is committed to something that does not exist, as talk of witches is. Its relatives are A. J. Ayer's emotivism, Gilbert Harman's relativism and Sharon Street's Darwinian dilemma. And its most interesting question is not whether it is true but what anyone should do if it is: abolish morality, keep it as a useful fiction (Richard Joyce), or carry on as before. Previous: [Chapter 11 — Sartre and Beauvoir](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md). Next: [Chapter 13 — Pessimism and Antinatalism](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md).

This chapter is written as a companion to [learn-ethics, Chapter 17 — Metaethics](../learn-ethics/17-metaethics.md). That chapter maps the whole field (Moore, expressivism and the Frege–Geach problem, realism, constructivism) and sets out Mackie's two arguments and Street's dilemma step by step. Read it first, or alongside. Here the same theories are approached from the nihilist's side: what exactly moral nihilism claims, where it comes from, how its defenders live with it, and why "everything is permitted" is the one thing it cannot say.

## What moral nihilism says, and what it doesn't

"Moral nihilism" is used loosely for at least four different things, and most confused arguments about it come from sliding between them.

| Position | Claim | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Amoralism** (practical nihilism) | I am not moved by moral considerations and see no reason to be | Thrasymachus, Callicles, Smerdyakov |
| **Error theory** (metaethical nihilism) | Moral claims try to state facts, but there are no moral facts, so all positive moral claims are false | Mackie, Joyce, Olson |
| **Non-cognitivism** | Moral claims do not try to state facts at all; they express attitudes or issue prescriptions | Ayer, Stevenson, (in sophisticated form) Blackburn, Gibbard |
| **Relativism** | Moral claims can be true, but only relative to an agreement, culture or set of attitudes | Harman; see [learn-ethics, Chapter 3](../learn-ethics/03-relativism-and-egoism.md) |

Only the second is moral nihilism in the strict sense used by contemporary philosophers: the view that nothing is morally right or wrong, good or bad, required or forbidden, because the facts that would make it so do not exist. Walter Sinnott-Armstrong defines it simply as the claim that no moral belief is true. The other three are its neighbors. The amoralist is a type of *person*, not a theory; a convinced realist can be an amoralist (he knows what is right and doesn't care), and a convinced error theorist can be scrupulously kind. Non-cognitivists, especially modern expressivists, insist they are not nihilists: once you understand what moral talk is for, they say, you are entitled to call cruelty wrong and mean it, so nothing has been lost. And relativists say that moral claims are true, only not absolutely.

Two corollaries follow, and they are the most important points in this chapter.

1. **Moral nihilism is not a moral view.** It does not say "everything is permitted," because "permitted" is itself a moral term. If there are no moral facts, then it is no more true that murder is permitted than that it is forbidden. The error theorist denies both. "Everything is permitted" is not nihilism; it is a very permissive (and very strange) first-order morality.
2. **Moral nihilism is not practical advice.** Whether you should keep your promises, if the error theory is true, is a question that the error theory itself cannot answer *morally*. It can be answered only in terms of non-moral reasons: what you want, what you care about, what kind of life you can stand. This is where the real interest begins.

## Three ancient prototypes

The modern positions have ancient ancestors, and each still shapes the debate.

**The amoralist.** In Book I of the *Republic*, the sophist Thrasymachus bursts into a polite conversation about justice with a definition:

> I proclaim that justice is nothing else than the interest of the stronger.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* I, 338c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Rulers make laws in their own interest and call obedience "justice"; the just man is a dupe who serves another's good. Thrasymachus is not denying that justice exists. He is giving a debunking account of it: what passes for justice is really something else (the advantage of the powerful), and once you see this you will be a fool to be bound by it. This is the template for every later "unmasking" of morality, from Nietzsche's genealogy ([Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md)) to Marx's ideology.

**The inventor.** In Plato's *Protagoras*, the sophist Protagoras tells a myth. The gods made the animals and gave each a means of survival; by the time they reached human beings the gifts had run out, so Prometheus stole fire and the arts. But humans who gathered into cities still wronged one another, and began to scatter and perish.

> Zeus feared that the entire race would be exterminated, and so he sent Hermes to them, bearing reverence and justice to be the ordering principles of cities and the bonds of friendship and conciliation.
>
> — Plato, *Protagoras* 322c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Strip away the gods and the myth says: morality is a social technology, devised to make cooperation possible among creatures who would otherwise destroy one another. That is exactly what the title of Mackie's book, *Inventing Right and Wrong*, means.

**The contractarian.** Thomas Hobbes put the Protagorean idea without the myth. In the state of nature there is no common power, and so

> To this warre of every man against every man, this also is consequent; that nothing can be Unjust. The notions of Right and Wrong, Justice and Injustice have there no place. Where there is no common Power, there is no Law: where no Law, no Injustice.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan*, ch. 13

For Hobbes, justice is created by covenants backed by a sovereign. Before that, there is nothing for "unjust" to name. Hobbes is not a nihilist about the commonwealth, but he is something close to a nihilist about the state of nature, and he shows how an "invented" morality can still be binding: not because it describes a mind-independent fact, but because rational people have overwhelming reason to create and keep it.

## Hume: reason the slave

The modern story begins with David Hume, whose sentimentalism is set out in [learn-ethics, Chapter 17](../learn-ethics/17-metaethics.md) and whose skepticism you met in [Chapter 3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md). Two of his sentences from that chapter are the ones the moral nihilist quotes most often: reason "is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions," and "It is not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger" (*Treatise* 2.3.3).

Hume's point is about *rationality*, not morality. Reason discovers matters of fact and relations of ideas; it can tell you that a means will not achieve your end, or that your passion rests on a false belief. But a preference as such is neither true nor false, so no preference, however monstrous, is irrational. If moral demands were demands of *reason*, they would have to bind everyone whatever their passions. Hume denies that there are any such demands.

Notice what does not follow. Hume did not think the destruction of the world was acceptable, or that morality was a fiction. He thought moral distinctions are grounded in human sentiment, especially sympathy, which is nearly universal and can be corrected by taking a "general point of view." Hume is a sentimentalist, not a nihilist. But he supplied the premise every later moral nihilist needs: that no fact of reason alone can make a demand on the will. Add the premise that moral claims *purport* to be such demands, and you have the error theory.

## Ayer: "they do not say anything"

The most notorious modern statement of moral skepticism came from a 25-year-old Oxford lecturer. A. J. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) imported the Vienna Circle's verification principle into English philosophy: a sentence is literally meaningful only if it is true by definition or empirically verifiable. Moral sentences are neither. So:

> sentences which simply express moral judgements do not say anything. They are pure expressions of feeling and as such do not come under the category of truth and falsehood.
>
> — A. J. Ayer, *Language, Truth and Logic*, ch. 6 (2nd ed., 1946)

The details of emotivism ("Boo!" and "Hurrah!"), Stevenson's refinements and the Frege–Geach objection are in [learn-ethics, Chapter 17](../learn-ethics/17-metaethics.md). For our purposes the question is whether emotivism is a form of nihilism.

In one sense this is more radical than the error theory: Mackie says "murder is wrong" is false, Ayer says it is not even false. In another sense it is less radical. Expressing a feeling is a respectable thing to do, and a feeling does not weaken by being correctly classified; the emotivist can go on condemning cruelty with full conviction.

Ayer seems to have lived that way. A widely repeated story (told in Ben Rogers's biography, *A. J. Ayer: A Life*, 1999) has the 77-year-old philosopher, at a New York party in 1987, telling the heavyweight champion Mike Tyson to stop pestering the young model Naomi Campbell. When Tyson asked whether Ayer knew who he was, Ayer reportedly replied that he was the former Wykeham Professor of Logic, that they were both pre-eminent in their fields, and that they should talk about it like rational men. The emotivist's disapproval was evidently not diminished by his metaethics.

The deeper worry about emotivism is not behavioral but descriptive. When you agonize over whether it is wrong to put a parent in a nursing home, you seem to be trying to find out what is *true*, not what you feel. That is the phenomenon Mackie set out to respect.

## Mackie: a theory of error

John Leslie Mackie (1917–1981), an Australian who taught at Oxford, begins *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* with the bluntest first sentence in the literature: there are no objective values. His view differs from Ayer's in one crucial respect. Mackie agrees with the realist about *meaning*: ordinary moral judgments really do purport to describe objective, action-guiding features of the world. When people say "that was cruel and wrong," they mean to say something true about the act, something that would hold whatever anyone felt, and that gives every agent a reason not to do it, whatever the agent wants. Mackie calls this *objective prescriptivity*. It is built into our moral concepts, and philosophers from Plato to Kant to Sidgwick have tried to capture it.

The error is that nothing in the world answers to it. The arguments from relativity and queerness are laid out in [learn-ethics, Chapter 17](../learn-ethics/17-metaethics.md). The heart of the second is a single sentence:

> If there were objective values, then they would be entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe.
>
> — J. L. Mackie, *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, ch. 1, § 9

Plato's Form of the Good is Mackie's illustration: knowing it would not merely inform you but *move* you; its "to-be-pursuedness" would be part of its nature. Nothing else we know of is like that. A fact about mass or charge never tells anyone what to do. So the most economical hypothesis is that there are no such things, and that moral language, as ordinarily used, systematically fails to refer. It is in the position of talk about witches, or phlogiston: meaningful, sincere, and false.

### Why we believe it anyway

An error theory owes an account of how the error arose. Mackie gives three explanations.

- **Projection.** Following Hume, we "objectify" our attitudes: we feel disgust at cruelty and experience the cruelty as *disgusting*, as if the disgustingness were in the act.
- **Social function.** Moral demands do their work best when people experience them as non-negotiable. A rule that says "don't steal, if you happen to want what stealing would cost you" is useless; a rule that says "don't steal, period" is socially invaluable. Communities that taught their members to experience morality as objective outcompeted those that didn't.
- **Linguistic inheritance.** Moral concepts may derive from a time when they were understood as commands of God or of the sovereign, and kept the form of a command after the commander disappeared. (Elizabeth Anscombe had made a similar point in "Modern Moral Philosophy," 1958, without Mackie's conclusion.)

### Inventing right and wrong

Here is the part of Mackie that is usually skipped and that matters most for nihilism. Having shown that morality has no objective foundation, Mackie does not stop. He spends the rest of the book doing *normative ethics*: arguing about what moral rules we should have, defending a version of rule-based cooperation, discussing the rights of future generations and the problem of the "self-referential altruism" of ordinary morality.

Is that coherent? Mackie thinks so, for reasons that go back to Protagoras and Hobbes. Morality is a human device to counteract the *limited sympathies* that make cooperation fragile: our benevolence is real but confined to a small circle, and without rules that override inclination we would drift toward Hobbes's war. The error theory shows that no rule is objectively required. It does not show that we have no reason to *have* rules, any more than we lack reason to have laws or traffic codes. We must decide what morality to have and defend it in terms of human interests. Hence "Inventing" right and wrong, not "Abolishing."

### The first-order question

There is a puzzle here that worries even sympathetic readers. When Mackie argues that we *ought* to adopt certain rules, is that "ought" an objective prescriptive one? If so, he has contradicted himself. If not, what kind of "ought" is it? Mackie's answer is that it is a hypothetical "ought": given what we all want (security, cooperation, the ability to plan), we have reason to adopt these rules. That is an instrumental reason, not a categorical one. But then morality's grip on any individual is only as strong as that individual's desire for the outcomes. The person who doesn't care about cooperation, or who can free-ride undetected, seems to escape. That is Thrasymachus's position, and it is the nerve that the error theory exposes. Richard Joyce's work is largely an attempt to face it squarely.

## Nietzsche's version

Before turning to Joyce, notice that the core claim of the error theory was stated almost a century before Mackie, in a book written in a week in 1888:

> that there are no such things as moral facts. Moral judgment has this in common with the religious one, that it believes in realities which are not real.
>
> — Nietzsche, *Twilight of the Idols*, "The 'Improvers' of Mankind," § 1 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Nietzsche goes on to say that morality is "only an interpretation of certain phenomena," more precisely a *misinterpretation*. That is an error theory. But Nietzsche's interest, unlike Mackie's, was not in the semantics of moral terms but in their *value*: what kind of life the belief in moral facts serves and what kind it damages. His answer, explored in [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) and [Chapter 7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md), is that the belief in moral facts has served the weak against the strong, and that its collapse is the event called nihilism. Mackie's error theory is the same diagnosis without the drama and without the revaluation.

## Harman: explanation and relativity

Gilbert Harman's *The Nature of Morality* (1977) opens with what has become known as Harman's *explanatory challenge*. You round a corner and see children pouring gasoline on a cat and setting it alight. You do not need to *infer* that what they are doing is wrong; you see it. But does the wrongness explain your observation? Harman argues not: your moral upbringing and the non-moral facts (pain, intention, gasoline) explain why you judge as you do, and the supposed moral fact does no additional work. In science, by contrast, the hypothesis that there is a proton in the cloud chamber does explain the vapor trail. If moral facts never need to be cited in the best explanation of anything we observe, we have no evidence for them. (Nicholas Sturgeon's reply and the literature on "moral explanations" are summarized in [learn-ethics, Chapter 17](../learn-ethics/17-metaethics.md).)

What is less often noticed is where Harman himself went next. He did not become an error theorist. In ch. 2 of the same book, "Nihilism and Naturalism," he distinguishes *extreme nihilism*, which concludes that morality is an illusion and should be abandoned, from views that try to find a naturalistic place for moral facts. His own solution, in "Moral Relativism Defended" (1975), is a form of relativism. Moral judgments of the "inner" kind ("he ought not to have done it," "it was wrong of her") presuppose that the agent shares, or has reason to share, an implicit agreement with the speaker and audience. Where no such agreement exists, inner judgments are out of place, though other negative judgments ("he is evil," "it was a terrible thing") remain available.

Harman's examples show the cost. If a professional criminal has no reason to accept our norms about harming outsiders, it is odd to say he *ought not* to have killed the bank guard, as if he had ignored a reason he possessed. We can call him a monster, not a man who went against his own reasons. Harman notoriously says the same of Hitler. Many readers find this a *reductio* of relativism; Harman finds it a precise statement of something we half-know already, that some people are not wrong-doers but enemies. Either way, it shows one retreat from nihilism: moral facts may be real but local, constituted by the agreements of particular groups.

## Debunking: from Darwin's doubt to Street's dilemma

The most powerful contemporary argument for moral nihilism (or something like it) is not about the metaphysics of moral properties at all. It is about the *origins of moral beliefs*.

Darwin saw both the threat and its scope. In a letter of 1881, discussing whether the universe shows signs of design, he wrote:

> with me the horrid doubt always arises whether the convictions of man's mind, which has been developed from the mind of the lower animals, are of any value or at all trustworthy. Would any one trust in the convictions of a monkey's mind, if there are any convictions in such a mind?
>
> — Charles Darwin, letter to William Graham, 3 July 1881, in *The Life and Letters of Charles Darwin*, vol. 1

Notice the scope. Darwin's "horrid doubt" is not limited to morality; it concerns *all* the convictions of the human mind. Alvin Plantinga later built his "evolutionary argument against naturalism" (in *Warrant and Proper Function*, 1993) on this very letter. The doubt shows the central difficulty of every debunking argument: how to debunk *some* beliefs without debunking all of them, including the beliefs used to run the argument.

Sharon Street's "Darwinian dilemma" (2006), set out in full in [learn-ethics, Chapter 17](../learn-ethics/17-metaethics.md), is the most careful attempt. In outline: natural selection has shaped the content of our basic evaluative attitudes (care for offspring, reciprocity, punishment of cheaters). If there are evaluative truths independent of those attitudes, then either selection tracked them or it didn't. If it didn't, it would be a coincidence if our values were close to the truth, so realism collapses into skepticism. If it did, the realist owes a scientific story of evaluative tracking, and the "adaptive link" account (our ancestors who valued these things left more descendants, whether or not the values were true) is simpler and better. So the realist faces either skepticism or bad science.

Where does nihilism enter? The first horn of the dilemma is, in effect, an argument that if realism were true, *we would have no moral knowledge*: moral skepticism. Joyce (*The Evolution of Morality*, 2006) presses this into an argument for the error theory. If our moral beliefs are fully explained by a process that would have produced them whether or not they were true, then those beliefs are *unjustified*, and an unjustified belief in a queer kind of fact should be abandoned.

Street, though, is not a nihilist. Her conclusion is that we should give up *realism* (the view that evaluative truths are independent of our attitudes) and adopt *constructivism*, on which evaluative truths are constituted by what follows from our attitudes on reflection. Then there is no gap for evolution to open, because there is no independent truth for it to miss. This is the general shape of the most popular escape from moral nihilism today: concede to the nihilist that there are no attitude-independent moral facts, and argue that attitude-dependent moral facts are facts enough.

Realists resist (David Enoch's "third-factor" reply, Michael Huemer's appeal to moral progress), and Darwin's doubt returns as a "companions in guilt" reply: the debunker must show that selection tracks the truth about logic and perception but not about value. Street argues that good science shows exactly this. The debate is unresolved.

## Living as an error theorist: abolish, pretend, or carry on?

Suppose you are convinced. There are no moral facts. Every judgment of the form "X is wrong" is false. What now?

Jonas Olson, Richard Joyce and others have mapped the options. They are summarized here with their best defenders and their main problems.

| Option | Proposal | Defenders | Main problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Abolitionism** | Stop using moral language and thinking in moral terms; rely on desires, sympathies and negotiation | Ian Hinckfuss, *The Moral Society* (1987); Richard Garner, "Abolishing Morality" (2007); Joel Marks, *Ethics without Morals* (2013) | Morality may be indispensable for cooperation; abolition may be impossible or catastrophic |
| **Fictionalism** | Keep using moral language, but as a fiction you "accept" without believing | Richard Joyce, *The Myth of Morality* (2001) | Can a fiction motivate? Can a fiction bind when you know it is one? |
| **Conservationism** | Keep believing moral claims in everyday life, despite knowing in the study that they are false | Jonas Olson, *Moral Error Theory* (2014) | Seems to recommend a kind of compartmentalized irrationality |
| **Revisionism** | Change the meaning of moral terms so that they no longer imply objective prescriptivity | Mackie (in practice); constructivists; some naturalists | Has the error theorist simply become a non-nihilist? |

### Abolitionism

The abolitionist argues that morality has done more harm than good: it turns conflicts of interest into crusades, licenses cruelty against "the wicked" and makes compromise look like betrayal. Better, Garner argues, to say "I don't want you to do that, and here is why it will hurt us both" than "that is evil." Joel Marks, a former Kantian, announced his conversion to "amoralism" in a 2010 *New York Times* essay and reported losing none of his compassion.

The objection is that morality's "objective" packaging may be exactly what makes it work. Mackie's own social-function argument suggests that rules people experience as non-negotiable are more reliable than rules they know to be negotiable. The abolitionist bets that human sympathy, enlightened self-interest and negotiation can do the job; history offers mixed evidence.

### Fictionalism

Joyce's proposal is subtler. Think of how you engage with a novel. You do not believe that Sherlock Holmes lived on Baker Street, but you are not lying when you say he did, in the context of discussing the stories; you are speaking *within the fiction*. Joyce suggests that the error theorist can treat morality similarly: *accept* moral propositions, use them in deliberation and conversation, feel their pull, without *believing* them. When you are in the philosophy seminar (the "critical context"), you step outside the fiction and say there are no moral facts. In ordinary life you stay inside.

Why bother? Because, Joyce argues, moral thinking is a valuable *precommitment device*. Human beings are prone to weakness of will. If you calculate, each time, whether keeping a promise serves your interests, you will be tempted by short-term gains that you will regret. If you think of promise-breaking simply as *wrong*, you will be more reliable, and more reliable people do better in the long run. Joyce compares it to a person who, trying to get fit, tells himself that he *must* do fifty sit-ups each morning: there is no such "must," but thinking in terms of it gets the sit-ups done.

Critics ask whether the trick can work: a known fiction may lose its force exactly when temptation is strongest, and fictional "acceptance" may not be psychologically distinct from belief at all.

### Conservationism

Olson's alternative is to go on *believing* moral claims in daily life while knowing, on reflection, that they are false, much as people go on seeing colors as "in" objects after learning the physics. The cost is that theory and practice come apart completely, and a critic may ask what the theory is *for*.

### Back to Ivan

Dostoevsky's *Brothers Karamazov* is, among other things, a test case for the claim that moral nihilism is harmless. You met the story in [Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md): Ivan argues, as an intellectual exercise, that without God and immortality there is no virtue; Smerdyakov takes him at his word, murders their father, and tells Ivan he did it "chiefly because 'all things are lawful.'"

The error theorist has an answer, and it is the point made at the start of this chapter. Smerdyakov has not drawn a consequence of moral nihilism. He has drawn a *moral* conclusion ("all things are lawful," a claim of permission) from a metaethical premise, and that inference is invalid. If there is no virtue, there is also no permission. What remains is the question of what Smerdyakov wanted, and what he could live with. He hangs himself the same night.

But Dostoevsky's point survives the logic lesson. Most people do not distinguish metaethics from ethics, and a theory that is harmless in the seminar may not be harmless when it reaches the servants' quarters. That is the strongest argument for fictionalism, and the most uncomfortable thing about it: it recommends that the philosophers keep the truth to themselves.

## Is moral nihilism self-defeating?

Several arguments claim that the error theory undermines itself or cannot even be stated.

- **Dworkin's argument.** Ronald Dworkin ("Objectivity and Truth: You'd Better Believe It," 1996; *Justice for Hedgehogs*, 2011) argues that there is no neutral, "Archimedean" standpoint outside morality from which to declare it false. The claim "nothing is morally wrong" is itself a substantive moral claim, a claim *within* morality, and must be defended by moral argument. External skepticism collapses into internal skepticism, which is just one more first-order moral view, and an implausible one. Error theorists reply that "there are no moral facts" is no more a moral claim than "there are no witches" is a witchcraft claim.
- **Companions in guilt.** If categorical moral reasons are "queer," so are epistemic reasons: reasons to believe what the evidence supports, whatever you happen to want. If the error theory is true of those as well, there is no reason to believe the error theory (Terence Cuneo, *The Normative Web*, 2007). The error theorist must show that epistemic normativity is different in kind. Many have tried; none has convinced everyone.
- **The Moorean response.** You are more certain that torturing a child for fun is wrong than you are of any premise in any argument for the error theory. A valid argument with a conclusion you are certain is false shows only that one of its premises is false. This is not a refutation, but it is a reminder that the burden of proof is not obviously on the realist.
- **Parfit's worry.** Derek Parfit, in *On What Matters* (2011), argued that if there are no irreducibly normative truths, then *nothing matters* in any sense, not even agony, and wrote much of that book to show that this nihilism is false.

## Living it

**Find your metaethics.** Pick a moral judgment you hold with total conviction (for instance, that torturing children for fun is wrong). Ask: what kind of fact, if any, makes it true? Is it a fact about the world, about God, about your feelings, about human agreements, about what any rational being would accept? Write a paragraph defending your answer, and then a paragraph of objections from the strongest critic you can imagine. Most people discover that their metaethics is less settled than their ethics, and that this does not seem to weaken the ethics at all. That gap is itself evidence, and it is worth thinking about what it shows.

**The abolitionist week.** For one week, try to avoid moral vocabulary in your own speech and thought: no "should," "wrong," "fair," "deserve," "ought." Replace each with what you want, what you predict and what the consequences will be ("I would rather you didn't, because I'll be hurt, and our friendship will suffer"). Keep a note of what was lost in translation and what, if anything, was gained. Garner predicts you will be calmer and more persuasive; Joyce predicts you will be less reliable. Which happened?

**The precommitment test.** Recall a promise you kept when breaking it would have been easy. Did you calculate, or think simply that you "must"? Would the "must" have held had you believed, then, that it was a fiction? Your answer is a small piece of evidence on the fictionalism debate.

## Summary

- *Moral nihilism*, strictly, is the view that there are no moral facts and so no true moral claims. It must be distinguished from *amoralism* (a practical stance), *non-cognitivism* (moral claims are not fact-stating) and *relativism* (moral claims are true relative to a standpoint).
- Moral nihilism does not say "everything is permitted": "permitted" is itself a moral term. The real question is what non-moral reasons remain.
- Ancient prototypes: Thrasymachus's debunking of justice as the interest of the stronger; Protagoras's myth of morality as a social invention; Hobbes's claim that before the commonwealth nothing is unjust.
- Hume: reason is the slave of the passions; no preference is contrary to reason. Hume himself is a sentimentalist, but supplies the key premise of the error theory.
- Ayer's emotivism: moral sentences express feelings and are neither true nor false. It is more radical than the error theory semantically, less radical practically.
- Mackie's *error theory*: moral judgments purport to state objectively prescriptive facts; there are none (relativity, queerness); so they are systematically false. We believe them because of projection, social function and linguistic inheritance. Mackie then *invents* right and wrong as a device against limited sympathies.
- Nietzsche anticipated the error theory ("there are no such things as moral facts") and asked a different question: what is the belief in moral facts worth?
- Harman's explanatory challenge; his relativism about "inner judgments" (the criminal, Hitler) as a retreat from nihilism.
- Debunking: Darwin's "horrid doubt" shows that evolutionary debunking threatens to overgeneralize. Street's dilemma argues against realism, but leads her to constructivism, not nihilism; Joyce uses debunking to support the error theory.
- After the error: *abolitionism* (Hinckfuss, Garner, Marks), *fictionalism* (Joyce), *conservationism* (Olson), *revisionism*. Smerdyakov shows both the invalidity of "no God, so all is lawful" and the social risk that motivates fictionalism.
- Objections: Dworkin (no Archimedean point), companions in guilt (epistemic reasons), the Moorean response, Parfit's worry that if nothing is normative, nothing matters.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether anything changes if nothing is wrong

**J. L. Mackie:** Let us suppose that my argument persuades you. There are no objective values. The wrongness of cruelty is not "out there." Tell me: what will you do differently tomorrow?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Mackie in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Honestly, probably nothing. I'll still be kind to my friends, still pay for things, still be appalled by cruelty. But it feels as though I should change something, or else the theory is idle.

**Mackie:** Why should you change?

**You:** Because before, I thought being kind was required of me, by the nature of things. Now I think it isn't. Surely a belief that drops out should take some behavior with it.

**Mackie:** Did you become kind because you believed it was objectively required?

**You:** No. I became kind because I was raised that way, and because I care about people.

**Mackie:** Then your kindness did not rest on the belief, and need not fall with it. The belief was a theory *about* your kindness, a flattering one. You can drop the theory and keep the practice, as you can drop a false theory of digestion and keep eating.

**You:** But surely the belief did some work. When I'm tempted to be unkind, part of what stops me is thinking "that would be wrong," not just "I wouldn't like it."

**Mackie:** That is an excellent observation, and it is the one Joyce builds on. Thinking "wrong" adds weight, a feeling of necessity. Now that you know the necessity is not real, does it still weigh?

**You:** I'm not sure. Maybe less. Maybe it will slowly fade.

**Mackie:** Then you have discovered what the theory does. It does not tell you what to do. It may change *how much your reasons weigh* in moments of temptation. That is not nothing.

**You:** So what should I do about that? Should I pretend?

**Mackie:** I wrote a book about what rules we have reason to invent, given what we all want. You have reason to want a world in which people keep promises and refrain from cruelty. You therefore have reason to support practices that produce such people, including, perhaps, the practice of speaking as if these things were objectively required.

**You:** That sounds like a noble lie.

**Mackie:** Every law is spoken as if it were simply binding, and everyone knows laws are made. Perhaps one day everyone will know that morality is made, and it will still bind, the way laws do. That is my hope. Joyce is less optimistic.

**You:** What would you say to someone who says: "Fine, but I don't want the world you want. I don't care about cooperation"?

**Mackie:** I would say he is dangerous, and that we should arrange things so that he has reason to cooperate anyway. What I would not say is that he has made a *mistake* about the universe. That was the old error.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether all things are lawful

**Smerdyakov:** Your philosophers tell me there are no moral facts. Master Ivan told me the same, in his way: if there is no God, there is no virtue, and all things are lawful. So I took him at his word. Tell me, where did I go wrong?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Smerdyakov in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** You made a logical mistake. If there are no moral facts, then nothing is forbidden, but nothing is "lawful" either, in the moral sense. You turned a denial of morality into a permission, and permission is a moral idea.

**Smerdyakov:** Very clever. But I did not need a permission. I needed only that nothing would stop me. And nothing did.

**You:** Your own fear almost did. And Ivan's horror afterward. Those were real, even if there are no moral facts.

**Smerdyakov:** Feelings. I had feelings too: I wanted the money, and I hated the old man. Why should Ivan's horror count for more than my wanting?

**You:** It doesn't, in the universe's eyes. But I'm not the universe. I'm a person who lives with others, and I have every reason to want a world where people like you are stopped.

**Smerdyakov:** So you will stop me because you want to, as I killed because I wanted to. Then we are the same.

**You:** No. I want a world where everyone, including you, can live without fear of being murdered. You wanted money for yourself. Those are different wants, and mine is one almost everyone shares, including people who might someday want to murder *you*.

**Smerdyakov:** That is an argument from prudence. Hobbes's argument. I know it. It tells me to cooperate when I might be caught. I was not going to be caught.

**You:** That's the real problem, isn't it? The person who can get away with it.

**Smerdyakov:** It is the only problem. Your error theorist can tell me I made no mistake about the universe. Your Hobbes can tell me I might be caught. Neither can tell me I was *wrong*.

**You:** No. But I can tell you what you did to yourself. You couldn't spend the money. You gave it back. You hanged yourself that night.

**Smerdyakov:** You think that proves something?

**You:** Not about the universe. About you. You couldn't live with it. Whatever "wrong" is or isn't, you were the kind of creature for whom it mattered, and you thought a theory could make that go away.

**Smerdyakov:** And you? Do you think your fictionalism will hold when you are where I was?

**You:** I don't know. That's the honest answer. Maybe that's why people keep believing in moral facts even after philosophers tell them not to.

</details>

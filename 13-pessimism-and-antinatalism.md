# Chapter 13 — Pessimism and Antinatalism

Nihilism says that nothing has value. Pessimism says something different and in a way more disturbing: that things *do* have value, and that on balance the value is negative. Life is not meaningless but bad; existence is not empty but a burden; and so, some pessimists conclude, it would have been better never to have been born, and it would be kind to stop bringing anyone else into the world. That last thesis, *antinatalism*, was once a cry from Greek tragedy and Schopenhauer's study. It is now defended with the full apparatus of analytic philosophy by David Benatar, fueled by the aphorisms of E. M. Cioran and the Norwegian Peter Wessel Zapffe, and dramatized in the "cosmic pessimism" of Thomas Ligotti and Eugene Thacker, which reached a mass audience through a television crime drama. This chapter takes the arguments seriously, because they are better than most people expect, and asks what follows if they are sound. Previous: [Chapter 12 — Moral Nihilism and Error Theory](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md). Next: [Chapter 14 — Nihilism in Modern Culture](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md).

A note before beginning. This chapter discusses suicide, despair and the claim that life is not worth living. Those are philosophical topics here, and the pessimists themselves, as will become clear, mostly argued against suicide. If the material weighs on you personally, put it down and talk to someone: a friend, a doctor, or a crisis line in your country.

## Pessimism is not nihilism

It is common to lump pessimists and nihilists together, and the pessimists of this chapter are often shelved under "nihilism" in bookshops. But the distinction matters, and it is easy to state.

| | Nihilism | Pessimism |
|---|---|---|
| Core claim | Nothing has value (or meaning, or moral truth) | The bad in existence outweighs the good |
| Status of value | Denied or deflated | Affirmed, and found to be negative |
| Typical mood | Emptiness, indifference, vertigo | Grief, pity, disgust, dark comedy |
| Practical upshot | Nothing follows, or anything goes | Something follows: compassion, renunciation, not procreating |
| Key figures | Turgenev's Bazarov, the "last man," Mackie (about morality) | Schopenhauer, Leopardi, Zapffe, Cioran, Benatar, Ligotti |

A thoroughgoing nihilist cannot say that life is *bad*, any more than the error theorist of [Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) can say that everything is *permitted*. "Bad" is a value term, and a strong one. The pessimist's claim requires that suffering really is bad, that its badness counts, and that it outweighs whatever good there is. That is why the most rigorous pessimist of our time, Benatar, is a moral realist of a fairly ordinary kind, and why his arguments start from intuitions about harm and duty that almost everyone shares.

It helps to separate several pessimistic theses, because thinkers hold different combinations:

1. **Hedonic pessimism:** in a typical human life, suffering outweighs pleasure or happiness.
2. **Evaluative pessimism:** existence is worse than non-existence, for every being that comes into it (Benatar's strong thesis) or for most.
3. **Existential pessimism:** consciousness itself, our ability to reflect on our condition, is the source of a unique and unredeemable misery (Zapffe, Ligotti).
4. **Cosmic pessimism:** the universe is not merely indifferent to human purposes but makes them absurd, unthinkable or horrifying (Lovecraft, Thacker).
5. **Practical antinatalism:** we ought not to bring new sentient beings into existence.

Thesis 5 is usually argued from 1 or 2, but not always: Shiffrin's argument (below) gets close to it from consent, without claiming life is bad.

So is pessimism a form of nihilism at all? In one sense no. In another sense it is nihilism's shadow: when a culture loses its confidence that existence has a purpose that redeems its suffering (God's plan, history's progress), what remains is a ledger of pleasures and pains with no external auditor. Schopenhauer saw that clearly, and Nietzsche regarded Schopenhauerian pessimism as a symptom of the nihilism he was diagnosing ([Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)). The pessimist, Nietzsche thought, had lost the old god but kept the old judge.

## Before the twentieth century: three voices

The Greek "wisdom of Silenus" (better never to have been born, next best to die soon) is discussed in [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md), and Schopenhauer and Leopardi in [Chapter 5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md). Recall two passages from that chapter, because they set up the specific themes of this one, and add a third.

**Schopenhauer on procreation.** In "On the Sufferings of the World," Schopenhauer asked whether the human race would continue "if children were brought into the world by an act of pure reason alone," or whether a man would rather "spare it the burden of existence." Two key ideas are already here. First, reproduction is not driven by reason but by the will (desire, instinct, the sexual drive), and that is why it continues. Second, the decision to create a person is a decision to *impose* something on someone who cannot consent, and an imposition of a "burden" calls for justification.

**Leopardi's Icelander.** In Leopardi's dialogue, Nature tells the Icelander that she "never gave a thought to the happiness or unhappiness of man." Chapter 5 stressed her indifference. What matters here is the Icelander's reply, the question every antinatalist since has repeated:

> But tell me: why am I here at all? Did I ask to come into the world?
>
> — Giacomo Leopardi, "Dialogue between Nature and an Icelander," *Essays and Dialogues* (tr. Charles Edwardes)

Nature never answers. The dialogue joins the two strands that will matter below: *cosmic* indifference (Nature does not notice us) and the *consent* objection to being born.

**Little Father Time.** The most harrowing antinatalist scene in English literature is in Thomas Hardy's *Jude the Obscure* (1895). Jude and Sue, unmarried and poor, are turned away from lodgings because of their children. Jude's son from his first marriage, a prematurely old boy nicknamed "Little Father Time," asks Sue whether it would be better to be out of the world than in it, and why people have children if they make so much trouble. The next morning, he has hanged his two half-siblings and himself, leaving a note:

> Done because we are too menny.
>
> — Thomas Hardy, *Jude the Obscure*, Part Sixth, ch. II

The doctor's verdict, which Jude reports, is Hardy's own diagnosis of the age:

> He says it is the beginning of the coming universal wish not to live.
>
> — Hardy, *Jude the Obscure*, Part Sixth, ch. II

Hardy's novel was savaged by reviewers (one headline called it "Jude the Obscene"), and he wrote no further novels, turning to poetry for the rest of his life. The scene is melodramatic, and it is meant to be. It shows what the antinatalist argument looks like when it is enacted by a child who has taken the adults' despair literally. It is also, in one way, a refutation of a careless antinatalism: the boy's act does not prevent harm, it inflicts the worst harm imaginable on two people who were already alive. Every serious antinatalist draws exactly this distinction.

## Zapffe: the overequipped animal

Peter Wessel Zapffe (1899–1990) was a Norwegian lawyer, mountaineer (he made first ascents in the mountains of northern Norway and was an early environmentalist), humorist and philosopher. His dissertation, *On the Tragic* (*Om det tragiske*, 1941), is unknown outside Scandinavia. His fame rests on an essay of about ten pages, "The Last Messiah" (*Den sidste Messias*), published in 1933 in the Norwegian magazine *Janus*.

### The tragedy of consciousness

Zapffe's starting point is biological. Every species is adapted to its environment by its organs and capacities. Sometimes a species overdevelops a feature until it becomes a liability: Zapffe mentions the extinct deer whose antlers, useful in fighting, grew so large that they could no longer carry them. (The usual example is the "Irish elk," *Megaloceros*, though modern paleontologists doubt that its antlers caused its extinction.) Human beings, Zapffe argues, are in the same position with *consciousness*. Our intellect gave us mastery over the environment, but it also gave us something no other animal has: an understanding of our own condition. We see that we will die, that our suffering has no purpose, that the universe does not care, that every achievement will be erased. Consciousness "overshoots" its biological function. We are equipped to see more than we can bear.

A tragedy, in Zapffe's technical sense, is a case in which a being's strongest capacity is the cause of its ruin. That is the human tragedy. And if it is true, why are we not all in perpetual despair?

### The four repressions

Zapffe's answer is the most influential part of the essay. Most people avoid despair by artificially limiting the content of consciousness, through four mechanisms:

| Mechanism | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Isolation** | Dismissing disturbing thoughts and feelings from awareness altogether | "Don't think about it"; social taboos on discussing death; tact |
| **Anchoring** | Fixing attention on stable points that give life an apparent structure and purpose | God, Church, State, morality, fate, the law of life, the people, the future, a career, a family |
| **Distraction** | Filling consciousness with impressions so that there is no room for reflection | Work, entertainment, sport, gossip, constant activity |
| **Sublimation** | Turning the suffering itself into something valuable, typically art | The poet who makes beautiful verses about despair; Zapffe's own essay, as he admits |

The table should look familiar. Anchoring is close to what Kierkegaard's Anti-Climacus calls the despair of not knowing one is in despair ([Chapter 8](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md)); distraction is Pascal's *divertissement* and Heidegger's "they" ([Chapter 9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md)); and Camus's revolt ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)) might, from Zapffe's perspective, be described as a brave and elegant form of sublimation. Zapffe's distinctive move is to treat all of them as *defenses*, psychological devices for keeping an intolerable truth at bay, and not as answers.

Zapffe is frank that his own essay is sublimation: the pain of the insight turned into the pleasure of a well-made argument. The pessimist is not exempt from the mechanisms; he is only aware of them.

### The last messiah

The essay ends with a parable. A messiah arises whose message is the opposite of every previous messiah's: not salvation, but the end of the species by voluntary childlessness. His final injunction is the most quoted line Zapffe wrote:

> Know yourselves – be infertile, and let the earth be silent after ye.
>
> — Peter Wessel Zapffe, "The Last Messiah" (tr. Gisle R. Tangenes)

He is, of course, killed: his listeners fall upon him, led by the people whose job it is to keep the species going. The parable is a satire of Christianity and a prediction of how antinatalism will be received. Zapffe himself never had children. In later life he was a beloved Norwegian public figure, celebrated for his humor. He died aged ninety.

## Cioran: the lucidity of insomnia

Emil Cioran (1911–1995) was born in a Transylvanian village, the son of an Orthodox priest. As a young man in Bucharest he suffered from chronic insomnia, which he later called the decisive experience of his life: nights without sleep, he said, take away the forgetting that makes each day a fresh start, and leave the mind face to face with time itself. His first book, *On the Heights of Despair* (1934), was written in that state.

Cioran's early career has a dark chapter that any honest account must include. In the 1930s he admired Hitler and wrote in support of the Romanian fascist Iron Guard; his book *The Transfiguration of Romania* (1936) is nationalist and in places antisemitic. He later called this period a madness and expurgated the book when it was reissued. Critics debate how far he ever reckoned with it. Pessimists are not immune to political fanaticism, and Cioran's first great French book, *A Short History of Decay* (1949), opens with an attack on fanaticism that reads, in part, as self-criticism: the man who believes he possesses the truth is ready to kill for it, and the skeptic, the idler and the decadent are humanity's safeguard against the zealot.

From 1937 Cioran lived in Paris, poor, writing in French: a language he compared to a straitjacket that disciplined his Romanian lyricism. His French books are collections of aphorisms and short essays: *A Short History of Decay*, *The Temptation to Exist* (1956), *The Fall into Time* (1964) and *The Trouble with Being Born* (1973).

### The disaster of birth

The title of that last book states its thesis. Most people fear death; Cioran's scandal is to locate the catastrophe at the other end. It is not death that is terrible, he suggests, but birth: being cast into time, into a body, into a self, into the necessity of wanting things. Death merely ends what birth began. One of his aphorisms makes the point by reversing the usual direction of fear: what we are running from is not the end of life but the disaster of having been born.

Cioran is not a systematic thinker and would have hated being made into one. His pessimism is a mood worked into a style: compressed, ironic, often very funny. He liked to present himself as an idler and a dilettante of despair. He is closer to the Ecclesiastes of [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md) than to Benatar.

### The consolation of suicide

What makes Cioran important for this course is his treatment of suicide. He thought about it constantly and never did it, and he explained why, in one of his most famous lines:

> It is not worth the bother of killing yourself, since you always kill yourself too late.
>
> — E. M. Cioran, *The Trouble with Being Born* (tr. Richard Howard)

The joke conceals an argument. The harm of existence has already been done; suicide cannot undo birth. And Cioran insists, repeatedly, that the *idea* of suicide is a liberation: knowing that you could leave makes it possible to stay. Here he is very close to Nietzsche:

> The thought of suicide is a great consolation: by means of it one gets successfully through many a bad night.
>
> — Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil* § 157 (tr. Helen Zimmern)

Camus would say that Cioran's life was a lived refutation of the inference from absurdity to suicide ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)), but Cioran reaches that position not by revolt but by a kind of exhausted irony. He lived to eighty-four, a fixture of the Latin Quarter, famous for his conversation. Visitors reported that the most pessimistic man in Paris was also one of the most cheerful company. That is not hypocrisy. It is perhaps the most interesting datum in this chapter: theoretical pessimism and personal gloom come apart.

## Benatar: the asymmetry

David Benatar is a South African philosopher at the University of Cape Town. *Better Never to Have Been* (2006) is the most rigorous defense of antinatalism ever written, and it begins with a sentence that announces its thesis without hedging:

> Each one of us was harmed by being brought into existence.
>
> — David Benatar, *Better Never to Have Been*, Introduction

Benatar's claim is not that most lives are bad, or that lives go worse than people think, though he argues that too. It is that *every* life, even the best, is a harm to the one who lives it, compared with never existing. To get there he uses an asymmetry between pain and pleasure.

### The argument

Consider two scenarios: in one, a person X exists; in the other, X never exists.

| | Scenario A: X exists | Scenario B: X never exists |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | (1) Presence of pain: **bad** | (3) Absence of pain: **good**, even if no one enjoys that good |
| Pleasure | (2) Presence of pleasure: **good** | (4) Absence of pleasure: **not bad**, unless there is someone deprived of it |

Compare the columns. In respect of pain, B is better than A (good beats bad). In respect of pleasure, A is not better than B in the way one might expect: A has a good, but B has nothing bad. So B has an advantage over A, and A has no corresponding advantage over B. Never existing is therefore better than existing, whatever the balance of pleasure and pain in X's life.

Everything depends on the asymmetry between (3) and (4). Why accept it? Benatar argues that it is the best explanation of four other asymmetries that most people already accept:

1. **The duty asymmetry.** We think there is a strong duty not to create a person who will suffer terribly, but no duty to create a person who would be happy. (Jan Narveson's slogan: we are in favor of making people happy, but neutral about making happy people.)
2. **The reasons asymmetry.** It is odd to cite the interests of a possible child as a reason *to* create it ("we should have a baby, since it would be good for the baby"), but not odd to cite them as a reason *not* to ("we should not, because the child would inherit a terrible disease").
3. **The distant-suffering asymmetry.** We can regret, for their sake, that people on a distant island are suffering. We do not regret, for anyone's sake, that there are no happy people on uninhabited Mars.
4. **The retrospective asymmetry.** We can regret, for the child's sake, having created a child who suffers. We do not regret, for anyone's sake, having failed to create a happy child who now never exists.

If (3) and (4) are true, all four are explained. If they are false, Benatar argues, we lose our grip on these widely shared judgments.

### Lives are worse than we think

The second argument is empirical. Even if you reject the asymmetry, Benatar argues that ordinary lives contain much more bad than people admit. He cites the "Pollyanna principle" (people systematically remember the good more readily than the bad), adaptation (people adjust their expectations to their circumstances and report satisfaction with lives they would once have dreaded) and comparison (people judge their lives against others', not against what a life could be). Then he lists the everyday sufferings we ignore: hunger, thirst, discomfort from heat and cold, fatigue, boredom, frustrated desires, itches, irritations, illness, aging and, at the end, death. A life that seemed good, judged without the rose-tinting, is "very bad," and creating it is a serious harm.

The empirical literature on optimism bias (people overestimate their chance of good outcomes) gives this some support. The related hypothesis of "depressive realism" (that mildly depressed people judge more accurately) has fared worse: the original 1979 finding by Alloy and Abramson has proved hard to replicate.

### What follows

Benatar draws three practical conclusions.

- **Do not procreate.** Each new person is harmed. The best outcome would be the gradual, voluntary extinction of humanity (he discusses "phased extinction" scenarios).
- **But do not kill yourself, or anyone.** Benatar distinguishes between a life *worth starting* and a life *worth continuing*. The standard for the first is much higher, because a person who exists has interests in continuing to live that a merely possible person lacks. Most lives are not worth starting but are worth continuing. In *The Human Predicament* (2017) he adds that death is itself a harm. This is what he calls our predicament: life is bad, and death is bad too.
- **Animals too.** The argument applies to every sentient being, which makes Benatar's antinatalism a critique of breeding animals as well.

### Objections

Most critics attack the asymmetry.

- **Why "good" in (3)?** If no one exists, there is no one for whom the absence of pain is good. Benatar says the absence is good "even if that good is not enjoyed by anyone." Critics reply that the symmetrical description is more natural: absent pain is "not bad" and absent pleasure is "not bad," in which case there is no asymmetry and the comparison depends on the actual balance of each life (Elizabeth Harman, Thaddeus Metz, David DeGrazia).
- **The four asymmetries can be explained otherwise.** For instance, the duty asymmetry may reflect a general asymmetry between harming and benefiting (we have stronger duties not to harm than to benefit), which says nothing about the value of existence.
- **The conclusion is too strong.** If Benatar is right, a life of ninety years of rich happiness with a single pinprick is worse than never existing. Many take that to be a *reductio*. Benatar accepts the implication and replies that our resistance to it is the Pollyanna principle at work.
- **Self-assessment.** Most people, asked whether they are glad to have been born, say yes. Benatar argues that their judgment is biased. Critics reply that this makes his view unfalsifiable: any contrary evidence is reclassified as bias.

### The consent argument

A distinct route to something like antinatalism, which does not depend on the asymmetry, was developed by the legal philosopher Seana Shiffrin (1999). She asks us to imagine a wealthy eccentric who, to share his fortune, drops cubes of gold from an airplane onto a crowd. Most people receive a windfall; some are injured. Even though he meant well and gave real benefits, he seems to have done wrong: he imposed serious risks on people without their consent, for benefits they did not need. Shiffrin argues that procreation has the same structure: it imposes on a person, without consent, a life with serious and unavoidable harms, in order to confer benefits that a non-existent person did not need. She does not conclude that procreation is impermissible, but that parents bear a special responsibility for the harms their children suffer. Others push her argument further. It is the philosophical form of the Icelander's question.

## Antinatalism in the world

Antinatalism has moved from the seminar into public life. The Voluntary Human Extinction Movement, founded in Oregon in 1991 by Les U. Knight, cheerfully urges people to stop reproducing for the planet's sake. In 2019 an Indian man, Raphael Samuel, made headlines by announcing that he would sue his parents for having him without his consent. And in 2017 the German philosopher Thomas Metzinger imagined a benevolent superintelligence that studies the human condition and concludes that non-existence is in our best interest. His point was not that the machine would be right, but that our certainty that existence is good may be an evolved bias of exactly the kind Benatar describes, with no neutral way to check.

## Cosmic pessimism: Lovecraft, Ligotti, Thacker

The pessimism discussed so far is about *suffering*. A second strand is about *scale*: the horror of discovering that human beings are an insignificant accident in a universe that is not merely indifferent but incomprehensible. Its founding text is a horror story.

### Lovecraft

H. P. Lovecraft (1890–1937), a reclusive writer from Providence, Rhode Island, called his outlook "cosmicism": human values and concerns, he wrote in his letters, have no validity or significance in the vast universe at large. "The Call of Cthulhu" (1928) opens with the most famous statement of it:

> The most merciful thing in the world, I think, is the inability of the human mind to correlate all its contents. We live on a placid island of ignorance in the midst of black seas of infinity, and it was not meant that we should voyage far.
>
> — H. P. Lovecraft, "The Call of Cthulhu"

The narrator goes on to predict that the "piecing together of dissociated knowledge" will either drive us mad or send us fleeing into a new dark age. This is Zapffe's thesis in pulp form: knowledge is the danger, and ignorance (isolation, in Zapffe's terms) the protection. Lovecraft's monsters are not evil; they are simply indifferent to us, as we are to bacteria. The horror is ontological, not moral. (Lovecraft's fiction is also shot through with racism, which recent critics, and writers such as Victor LaValle in *The Ballad of Black Tom*, 2016, have confronted directly.)

### Ligotti

Thomas Ligotti (b. 1953) is an American writer of horror stories, often compared to Poe, Kafka and Lovecraft, who has suffered from anxiety and anhedonia since his late teens. *The Conspiracy against the Human Race* (2010) is his only work of nonfiction, a book-length essay that draws together Schopenhauer, Mainländer, Zapffe, Cioran, Benatar and the philosopher of mind Thomas Metzinger.

Ligotti's central thesis is Zapffe's, radicalized. Consciousness is not a gift that turned into a burden; it is a blunder of evolution that has no use for us except to make us aware of our own horror. We are, in Ligotti's view, puppets of biological processes that we did not choose, deluded by an illusion of selfhood (here he leans on Metzinger's thesis that the self is a model the brain constructs, not a thing). The "conspiracy" of his title is the collective, unspoken agreement to repress this knowledge, through all of Zapffe's mechanisms, so that life can go on. Ligotti endorses the antinatalist conclusion, but his real interest is the *experience* of the truth: the moments in which ordinary reality cracks and its puppet-like character shows through. That, for him, is what horror fiction is for.

### Thacker

Eugene Thacker, a professor at The New School in New York, approaches the same territory from philosophy and media theory. *In the Dust of This Planet* (2011), the first volume of his *Horror of Philosophy* series, distinguishes three "worlds":

- The **world-for-us**: the world as it appears to human beings and is shaped by our interests and meanings.
- The **world-in-itself**: the world as it is apart from us, which, as soon as we think about it, becomes a world-for-us again (Kant's problem).
- The **world-without-us**: the world as it would be, and largely is, independent of any human perspective at all; Thacker calls it the *planet*.

The world-without-us is what horror fiction tries, and necessarily fails, to show: the black ooze, the formless thing, the cosmic indifference that cannot be represented without being humanized. Thacker reads horror as a genre of *thought*: the attempt to think the unthinkable limit of human thought. *Cosmic Pessimism* (2015) and *Infinite Resignation* (2018) continue the project in fragments that owe much to Cioran.

Thacker's pessimism is less an argument than an attitude of refusal and irony. Its kinship with Ray Brassier's scientific nihilism is taken up in [Chapter 14](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md).

### True Detective

In January 2014 HBO broadcast the first episode of *True Detective*, a crime drama by Nic Pizzolatto. In a car on a Louisiana highway, Detective Rust Cohle (Matthew McConaughey) explains his philosophy to his partner: human consciousness was a tragic misstep in evolution; we became too self-aware; the honorable thing would be for the species to stop reproducing and walk together into extinction. Viewers who knew the literature recognized Zapffe and Ligotti almost line by line. Pizzolatto acknowledged in interviews that he had been reading Ligotti, Thacker, Brassier, Cioran and Zapffe. Later that year critics accused him of plagiarizing Ligotti; he denied it, and HBO defended him. Sales of Ligotti's book and Thacker's surged, and an episode of the radio program *Radiolab*, "In the Dust of This Planet," traced the strange career of *In the Dust of This Planet* as it passed from an academic press into mass culture.

It is worth asking why cosmic pessimism sounds so magnificent from a charismatic detective, and whether the seduction is itself one of Zapffe's mechanisms: sublimation, turned into entertainment.

## Can a pessimist live consistently?

The usual charge against pessimists is hypocrisy. Schopenhauer preached renunciation and ate well, kept poodles and played the flute daily. Cioran praised suicide and lived to eighty-four. Zapffe climbed mountains for joy. Benatar has an ordinary career.

The pessimists' reply, as shown above, is that their arguments are about whether to *start* a life, not whether to continue one, and that the pleasures of a life already begun do not refute the claim that it would have been better not to begin. There is no inconsistency in thinking that you were harmed by being born and that, having been born, you should make the best of it.

A deeper reply is offered by Joshua Foa Dienstag in *Pessimism: Philosophy, Ethic, Spirit* (2006). Dienstag argues that pessimism is not a doctrine about the balance of pleasure and pain but an *ethic*: a way of living without the expectation that history, progress or providence will redeem suffering. Its characteristic virtues are lucidity, irony, compassion and a refusal of consolation. The optimist, by contrast, is always in danger of sacrificing present people to a promised future. On this reading, pessimism is one of the responses to nihilism, not one of its forms, and an unexpectedly humane one.

One complication remains. Zapffe suggests that the mechanisms of repression are necessary for life, so the pessimist who goes on living is, by his own account, partly self-deceived. He sees enough to know he is not seeing everything.

## Living it

**The asymmetry test.** Write down your honest answers to Benatar's four asymmetries. Do you believe there is a duty not to create a miserable child but no duty to create a happy one? Do you regret that Mars has no happy inhabitants? If you accept all four, you must either accept Benatar's asymmetry or find a better explanation of them. Spend a page trying to find one.

**A Zapffe audit.** For one day, notice each time you use one of the four mechanisms: isolating (putting a thought aside), anchoring (reaching for a stable point of meaning), distracting (picking up your phone), sublimating (turning a pain into a project or a joke). At the end of the day, ask: which of these would you give up if you could? Which do you think a good life *requires*? Zapffe thought all four were defenses; Camus might call some of them revolt; Stoics might call some of them discipline ([learn-stoicism, Chapter 12 — Stoic Practices](../learn-stoicism/12-stoic-practices.md)).

**Read in the right mood.** Read twenty aphorisms of Cioran late at night, then twenty the next morning after breakfast. Note which ones strike you as profound and which as melodramatic, and whether the time of day changed the verdict. Cioran would have found the experiment funny and instructive.

## Summary

- **Pessimism** is not nihilism: it affirms value and judges existence to be on balance bad. It includes hedonic, evaluative, existential and cosmic theses and, in practice, **antinatalism**.
- **Precursors:** Schopenhauer's "act of pure reason" thought experiment; Leopardi's Icelander ("Did I ask to come into the world?"); Hardy's Little Father Time and "the coming universal wish not to live."
- **Zapffe:** consciousness as an overdeveloped capacity that makes human life tragic; the four mechanisms (isolation, anchoring, distraction, sublimation); the last messiah's call to infertility.
- **Cioran:** birth, not death, as the catastrophe; aphoristic, ironic pessimism; the *idea* of suicide as a consolation that makes life bearable (compare Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil* § 157); the shadow of his fascist youth.
- **Benatar:** the asymmetry between absent pain (good) and absent pleasure (not bad), supported by four widely accepted asymmetries; the quality-of-life argument (Pollyanna, adaptation, comparison); lives not worth starting can be worth continuing.
- **Objections:** the asymmetry can be described symmetrically; the supporting asymmetries have other explanations; the conclusion seems a *reductio*; the bias argument risks unfalsifiability.
- **Shiffrin's consent argument:** procreation imposes unconsented risk, as in the gold-cube case.
- **Cosmic pessimism:** Lovecraft's "placid island of ignorance"; Ligotti on consciousness as evolutionary blunder and the "conspiracy" to repress it; Thacker's world-for-us, world-in-itself, world-without-us; *True Detective* as pop-cultural transmission.
- **Living consistently:** life-starting vs life-continuing; Dienstag's pessimism as an ethic; Zapffe's paradox that the lucid pessimist must also repress.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether never existing is better

**David Benatar:** You told me that you hope to have children one day, and that they will have good lives. Let me ask you a simple question. If you decided not to have a child, who would be harmed by that decision?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Benatar in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Nobody, I suppose. There'd be no child to be harmed. Maybe I would be, because I'd miss out on being a parent.

**Benatar:** Leave yourself aside for now. So the non-existent child is not deprived of anything. And if you do have a child, and its life contains pain, as every life does, is that child harmed by the pain?

**You:** Yes, by the pain. But it also gets the pleasures.

**Benatar:** Certainly. So compare. If you don't have the child, there is no pain, which is good, and no pleasure, which is not bad because no one is deprived. If you do, there is pain, which is bad, and pleasure, which is good. Where is the advantage of existing?

**You:** In the pleasures. They're good. The non-existent child doesn't have them.

**Benatar:** But you've just agreed that the non-existent child isn't deprived of them. Their absence is bad for no one. Whereas the absence of pain is good.

**You:** Good for whom, though? You said there's no one there.

**Benatar:** That's the right question. Do you think it would be wrong to create a child knowing it would suffer terribly from birth to death?

**You:** Yes, obviously.

**Benatar:** Why? Who would be wronged if you refrained?

**You:** No one would be wronged, but the absence of that suffering would be... good. I see. I'm calling it good even though there's no one to enjoy it.

**Benatar:** And do you think you are obliged to create a child who would be very happy?

**You:** No.

**Benatar:** So you accept the asymmetry. Absent suffering is good; absent happiness is not bad.

**You:** But maybe the reason I'm not obliged to create a happy child isn't that its absent happiness is "not bad." Maybe it's that I don't have a duty to benefit people in general, only not to harm them. That's a difference between harming and benefiting, not between pain and pleasure.

**Benatar:** That is the best reply, and many philosophers make it. Then tell me about Mars. Do you regret, for anyone's sake, that there are no happy Martians?

**You:** No.

**Benatar:** And would you regret, for their sake, that there were suffering Martians, if there were?

**You:** Yes. But that's because the suffering Martians would exist. The happy ones don't.

**Benatar:** Precisely my point. Absent happiness is regretted only where someone exists to lack it. Absent suffering is welcome even where no one exists to be spared.

**You:** Even if I grant all that, I'm much more confident that my grandmother's life wasn't a harm to her than I am of any premise in your argument.

**Benatar:** Then you have told me how strong your intuition is. You haven't told me whether it is reliable. Ask your grandmother about the last ten years.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether philosophy is an anchor

**Peter Wessel Zapffe:** You have read my essay. You saw your own life in my four mechanisms, I suspect. Which one do you rely on most?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Zapffe in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Distraction, mostly. My phone, work, music. And anchoring, if I'm honest: my family, my plans for the future.

**Zapffe:** And now that you know they are defenses, will you give them up?

**You:** I don't think I can. And I'm not sure I should. Why are they defenses rather than just... a life?

**Zapffe:** Because they work by narrowing what you are aware of. When you reach for your phone at the moment a thought of death arises, you are not answering the thought. You are making it go away.

**You:** But some anchors aren't like that. Loving my family isn't a way of not thinking about death. It's what makes death worth thinking about.

**Zapffe:** A beautiful sentence. Do you see what you just did?

**You:** What?

**Zapffe:** You turned the pain into something that sounds valuable. That is sublimation, the rarest of the four. You are a natural.

**You:** You're saying every response to your argument is just another mechanism.

**Zapffe:** I am saying every response is *also* a mechanism. It may also be true. But you cannot tell from the inside.

**You:** Then the same applies to you. Your essay, your pessimism. Isn't your whole philosophy an anchor? "Life is tragic" is a stable point too. It organizes everything.

**Zapffe:** Yes. I said so in the essay. My writing is sublimation; I am not exempt.

**You:** Then why should I trust your view over mine? If we're both using mechanisms, you have no special access to the truth.

**Zapffe:** Only this: I know what I am doing. And I do not ask the universe to approve of it.

**You:** That sounds like Camus.

**Zapffe:** Camus wanted to revolt. I prefer to climb mountains and tell jokes. But I agree that is not much of a difference.

**You:** And children? You never had any.

**Zapffe:** That was the one place where I did not think a mechanism was enough. You may decide otherwise. But decide it awake.

</details>

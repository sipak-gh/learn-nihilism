# Chapter 14 — Nihilism in Modern Culture

In a note of 1885–1887 Nietzsche wrote: "Nihilism is at our door: whence comes this most gruesome of all guests to us?" (*The Will to Power* § 1, tr. Anthony M. Ludovici; see [Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)). In the twentieth century the guest came in and made itself at home, not mainly in philosophy seminars but in cabarets, novels, theaters, cinemas, record shops and, finally, comment threads. This chapter follows it there. It starts with Dada's war on meaning in a Zurich nightclub in 1916, Kafka's courts and castles, and Beckett's two tramps waiting by a tree. It then samples film and music, from *The Seventh Seal* to *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, from punk's "no future" to black metal. Three late-twentieth-century philosophers tried to say what nihilism had become: Gianni Vattimo, for whom it is our best chance; Jean Baudrillard, for whom it is the condition of a world of images; and Ray Brassier, who wants to drive it to its scientific conclusion. The chapter ends with the cheerful "optimistic nihilism" of the internet, and asks whether it is nihilism at all. Previous: [Chapter 13 — Pessimism and Antinatalism](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md). Next: [Chapter 15 — Beyond Nihilism](15-beyond-nihilism.md).

## From doctrine to mood

Philosophical nihilism is a thesis: there are no values, no meanings, no truths of a certain kind. Cultural nihilism is something else: a *mood* and a set of *forms* that express the experience of living after the thesis has sunk in. A painting cannot argue that nothing has meaning, but it can refuse to mean in the expected way. A play cannot prove that God is absent, but it can show two men waiting for someone who never comes. Culture is where a society tests what it would be like if the philosophers were right.

Three patterns recur throughout this chapter, and it is worth having them in view from the start.

1. **Negation becomes a style.** Every attempt to destroy meaning produces a new form, which is then admired, collected and imitated. Dada wanted to abolish art and ended up in museums.
2. **Despair becomes comedy.** Kafka laughed while reading his work aloud; Beckett's plays are music-hall routines; the internet turned existential dread into memes. Laughter is one of the oldest responses to the void, and possibly the most honest.
3. **The search for meaning survives its own denial.** Almost every great "nihilist" work turns out, on inspection, to be haunted by the values it says are gone. That is either a sign that nihilism cannot be lived, or a sign that it has not yet been finished, depending on whom you ask.

## Dada: art against art

On 5 February 1916, in the middle of the First World War, the German poet Hugo Ball and the performer Emmy Hennings opened the Cabaret Voltaire in the back room of a tavern on the Spiegelgasse in Zurich, a few doors from where Lenin was living in exile. Within weeks a group of refugees, deserters and poets (the Romanians Tristan Tzara and Marcel Janco, the Alsatian Hans Arp, the German Richard Huelsenbeck) was staging nightly performances: simultaneous poems recited in three languages at once, "noise music," African-inspired masks, and Ball's sound poem "Karawane," which he performed in a cardboard costume, chanting nonsense syllables until, by his own account, he fell into the cadence of a liturgy. According to the best-known of several competing origin stories, someone opened a French–German dictionary at random and found the word "Dada": it is French for a hobby-horse, and childish babble in several languages. It meant nothing, which was the point.

Dada was a response to the war. Europe's most civilized nations, with their universities, cathedrals and philosophies, were killing millions of young men in the trenches. If that was what reason, progress and culture produced, then reason, progress and culture deserved no respect. Dada's weapon was nonsense: if meaning had led to the Somme, perhaps meaninglessness was more honest.

Tzara's "Dada Manifesto 1918," read in Zurich that July, is the movement's most famous text. It declares that Dada means nothing, mocks every system including its own, and calls for a great negative work of destruction to be carried out, clearing away logic, morality, memory and the future. It is also very funny, and it insists, in the same breath, on spontaneity, contradiction and life. That double gesture is Dada's signature: destruction as a form of vitality.

In New York, Marcel Duchamp made the movement's most enduring object. In 1917 he bought a porcelain urinal, turned it on its back, signed it "R. Mutt" and submitted it as *Fountain* to the Society of Independent Artists, whose exhibition promised to show any work whose artist paid the fee. The committee hid it. Photographed by Alfred Stieglitz, the lost original became one of the most influential artworks of the century. Duchamp later complained, in a 1962 letter quoted by the artist Hans Richter, that he had thrown his ready-mades in the public's face as a challenge and they now admired them for their beauty. In Berlin, Dada became political: Hannah Höch's photomontages and George Grosz's caricatures attacked the Weimar establishment, and in 1937 the Nazis displayed Dada works in their "Degenerate Art" exhibition.

Was Dada nihilist? It certainly attacked every inherited value. But it did so in the name of something: freedom, honesty, play, the irrational vitality the war had betrayed. Its negations were *performed*, joyfully, for an audience. In Nietzsche's terms ([Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)) it was *active* nihilism: the destruction of old values as a sign of increased power, not exhaustion. And its fate illustrates the first pattern above. The anti-art became art; the manifesto became a genre; replicas of the urinal now stand in the world's great museums. Negation, it seems, cannot remain pure. Every "no" is captured by the system it denies.

## Kafka: the law without meaning

Franz Kafka (1883–1924) spent his working life as a lawyer at the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute in Prague and wrote at night. He published little in his lifetime and asked his friend Max Brod to burn the rest. Brod refused, and published *The Trial* (1925), *The Castle* (1926) and *Amerika* (1927). The adjective "Kafkaesque" now names a whole experience of modern life: being trapped in a system whose rules are unknowable, whose authorities are invisible, and whose judgments cannot be appealed.

### Two openings

Kafka's two most famous openings are among the most famous in literature:

> One morning, when Gregor Samsa woke from troubled dreams, he found himself transformed in his bed into a horrible vermin.
>
> — Franz Kafka, *Metamorphosis*, Part I (tr. David Wyllie)

> Someone must have been telling lies about Josef K., he knew he had done nothing wrong but, one morning, he was arrested.
>
> — Franz Kafka, *The Trial*, Chapter One (tr. David Wyllie)

In both, a catastrophe happens without cause or explanation, and the rest of the story is about how the victim and the people around him respond. Gregor's first worry is that he will miss his train to work. The German word Kafka uses, *Ungeziefer*, is vaguer than "insect": it means vermin, an unclean creature unfit for sacrifice. Kafka told his publisher that the creature itself must not be drawn on the cover. The transformation is not a fantasy to be visualized but a condition: a man whose usefulness to his family has ended, and who becomes, in their eyes and slowly in his own, a thing.

### Before the Law

The heart of *The Trial* is a parable told to Josef K. by a priest in a dark cathedral. A man from the country comes to the door of the Law and asks to enter. The doorkeeper says: not now. The man waits for years, bribes the doorkeeper, studies even the fleas in his fur collar. As he is dying, he asks why, in all these years, no one else has come to ask for admittance. The doorkeeper bellows into his failing ears:

> Nobody else could have got in this way, as this entrance was meant only for you. Now I'll go and close it.
>
> — Kafka, *The Trial*, Chapter Nine, "In the Cathedral" (tr. David Wyllie)

The priest and K. then argue about the meaning of the parable for pages, offering interpretation after interpretation, each plausible and none conclusive. That is the point, or one of them. The Law in Kafka's work is real, powerful and absolutely binding; it is also inaccessible, and its meaning dissolves under interpretation. This is a specific and very modern form of nihilism: not the absence of authority, but the presence of an authority that cannot be understood or justified. It is the world of the error theorist ([Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md)) seen from the inside: commands that claim objective force but have nothing behind them.

### Readings

Kafka has been read as a religious writer (Brod saw him as a seeker after grace), as a prophet of bureaucratic totalitarianism, as a Jewish writer in a German-speaking Prague hostile to Jews, as a son crushed by his father (his unsent *Letter to His Father*, 1919), and as an existentialist. Camus devoted an appendix of *The Myth of Sisyphus* to him ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)), arguing that Kafka's novels are absurd works that nonetheless smuggle in hope. Brod reported a conversation in which Kafka said there was plenty of hope, an infinite amount, only not for us.

Something often missed is that Kafka is funny. Brod recalled that when Kafka read the first chapter of *The Trial* aloud to his friends, they laughed so much that he sometimes could not go on. The officials who arrest K. eat his breakfast; the court's offices are in tenement attics, full of exhausted, airless petitioners; the most powerful lawyer conducts business from his sickbed. This is the second pattern: despair, at its most acute, tips into comedy.

## Interlude: the waste land

The other great modernist text of nihilism is a poem. T. S. Eliot's *The Waste Land* (1922) is a collage of fragments from Dante, Shakespeare, the Upanishads, music-hall songs and overheard conversations, set in a London that has become a city of the dead:

> Unreal City,
> Under the brown fog of a winter dawn,
> A crowd flowed over London Bridge, so many,
> I had not thought death had undone so many.
>
> — T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*, I, "The Burial of the Dead"

The last line echoes Dante's sight of the souls in the vestibule of Hell, those who lived without blame and without praise. The commuters are not wicked; they are spiritually inert. The poem's images of culture are "a heap of broken images," and its speaker offers, in the first section, to show the reader "fear in a handful of dust." Its form enacts its theme: a civilization whose inherited meanings survive only as fragments, quoted out of context. The poem ends with a line about precisely this:

> These fragments I have shored against my ruins
>
> — T. S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*, V, "What the Thunder Said," line 430

Eliot's own response to the waste land was to join the Church of England in 1927. The poem itself does not take that step. It shows what Nietzsche's prophecy of nihilism looked like when it arrived: not a dramatic collapse, but a long, quiet exhaustion of meaning, with the remnants of tradition lying around in pieces.

## Beckett: nothing to be done

Samuel Beckett (1906–1989) was born in Dublin, worked as an assistant to James Joyce in Paris, served in the French Resistance during the Second World War, and, after the war, began writing in French. *En attendant Godot* premiered in a small Paris theater in January 1953. Two tramps, Vladimir and Estragon, wait by a bare tree on a country road for a Mr. Godot, who never comes. They talk, quarrel, contemplate hanging themselves from the tree (the only cord they have, Estragon's belt, breaks), are visited by the tyrant Pozzo and his slave Lucky, and are told by a boy at the end of each act that Godot will come tomorrow. The play's first line, spoken by Estragon as he struggles with his boot, is "Nothing to be done." The Irish critic Vivian Mercier famously summed it up as a play in which nothing happens, twice.

It is tempting to read Godot as God, and the play as an allegory of waiting for a divine meaning that never arrives. Beckett resisted every allegorical reading; he said that if he had known who Godot was, he would have said so in the play. The play's power lies not in a hidden message but in its *structure*: repetition, waiting, habit, the sense that time passes without anything being accomplished. Vladimir and Estragon fill the time with routines (games, insults, reconciliations, hats) that make the waiting bearable. They are Zapffe's mechanisms ([Chapter 13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md)) presented as vaudeville. They do not leave. Each act ends with "Let's go" and the stage direction that they do not move.

The critic Martin Esslin grouped Beckett with Eugène Ionesco, Jean Genet, Arthur Adamov and the young Harold Pinter under the label "the Theatre of the Absurd" (1961), borrowing Camus's term. But Esslin's most famous anecdote, which opens his book, is about an audience. In November 1957 the San Francisco Actors' Workshop performed *Godot* for over a thousand convicts at San Quentin prison. The actors feared the prisoners would be baffled or hostile. Instead they understood it at once: they knew all about waiting for something that never comes. One prisoner, Rick Cluchey, later founded a theater group inside the prison and went on to work with Beckett himself.

Beckett received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1969. Late in life he wrote a short prose piece, *Worstward Ho* (1983), which contains a passage that has had an extraordinary afterlife:

> Ever tried. Ever failed. No matter. Try again. Fail again. Fail better.
>
> — Samuel Beckett, *Worstward Ho* (1983)

In context the passage is bleak: part of a meditation on the impossibility of saying anything adequately, of going on and of stopping. Removed from context, it has become a motivational slogan for entrepreneurs and athletes; the tennis champion Stan Wawrinka has it tattooed on his forearm. That fate is a perfect illustration of the first pattern: the most uncompromising art of negation is turned into a self-help poster. One may also read it as Beckett's version of Camus's Sisyphus: not hope, not despair, but persistence.

## Film and music

Nihilism in film and popular music is a vast subject. A table of landmarks gives a sense of the range; a few cases deserve closer look.

### The Big Lebowski's nihilists

*The Big Lebowski* contains the funniest portrayal of nihilism on film. Three German nihilists (one played by the musician Flea) threaten the Dude, demand money, and insist that they believe in nothing. The Dude's friend Walter, a volatile Vietnam veteran, is appalled, not by their threats but by their emptiness: he observes, in a line usually quoted with relish, that whatever one thinks of the tenets of National Socialism, at least it is an *ethos*. The joke works on several levels. The nihilists are not nihilists at all; they want money, which is a value, and they are frightened when Walter fights back. Real nihilism, the film suggests, is not a creed one can join; anyone who says they "believe in nothing" is usually after something.

### Everything Everywhere All at Once

The most philosophically ambitious recent treatment is *Everything Everywhere All at Once* (2022). Evelyn, a Chinese-American laundromat owner drowning in tax forms, learns that she is one of infinitely many versions of herself across a multiverse. Her daughter Joy, in another universe, has experienced *all* the universes at once and concluded that nothing matters. She has built an "everything bagel," a black hole into which she means to fall, and wants her mother to come with her. The film's answer is given by Evelyn's gentle husband Waymond, who argues that if nothing matters, then the only thing left is to be kind, especially when we don't know what's going on. The answer is close to Camus's ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)), and the film knows it: the cosmic insight is granted in full, and a human response is built on top of it. Its success at the 2023 Academy Awards suggests how widely the question now resonates.

### Music: refusal and its dangers

Punk made nihilism a sound. The Sex Pistols' "God Save the Queen" (1977), released during the Queen's Silver Jubilee, ends with a repeated chant of "no future." Richard Hell's "Blank Generation" (1977) turned emptiness into a pose. For many listeners this was liberating: a refusal of a society that had nothing to offer them.

The darker case is Norwegian black metal of the early 1990s. A small scene of musicians who cultivated an aesthetic of nihilism, satanism and misanthropy produced a wave of church burnings and, in 1993, a murder: Varg Vikernes of the one-man band Burzum killed Øystein Aarseth of Mayhem and was convicted in 1994. Vikernes later became a prominent far-right ideologue. The episode shows something the philosophers of this course have repeatedly warned about: "nihilism" as a cultural pose is often not the absence of values but the presence of very bad ones, cloaked in the glamour of negation. Nietzsche's word for this was *ressentiment*.

## Vattimo: weak thought and accomplished nihilism

The first philosophical attempt to take the measure of nihilism in late modern culture came from Turin. Gianni Vattimo (1936–2023), a student of Hans-Georg Gadamer and a reader of Nietzsche and Heidegger, coined the term *pensiero debole*, "weak thought," in an anthology he edited with Pier Aldo Rovatti in 1983.

Vattimo's starting point is Nietzsche's remark, from the *Nachlass*, that facts are exactly what we lack:

> No, facts are precisely what is lacking, all that exists consists of interpretations. We cannot establish any fact "in itself": it may even be nonsense to desire to do such a thing.
>
> — Nietzsche, *The Will to Power* § 481 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Combine this with Heidegger's history of Being as the history of metaphysics ([Chapter 9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md)), and you get Vattimo's thesis: the history of Western thought is a history of the *weakening* of strong structures, of absolute foundations, objective truths and final authorities. God, Being, Truth, the meaning of History: each has been dissolved into interpretation. That is what nihilism means. And it is, Vattimo argues, *good news*.

In *The End of Modernity* (1985) Vattimo calls this "accomplished nihilism." The accomplished nihilist, he says, has understood that nihilism is his only chance. Strong foundations were always also instruments of violence: whoever possesses the Truth may impose it. A world without them is more plural, more tolerant, more open to dialogue. Vattimo borrows from Heidegger the word *Verwindung*, which means something like recovering from an illness, coming to terms with something or twisting it: we do not *overcome* metaphysics (that would be one more strong claim), we convalesce from it, accept it as our heritage and gradually distort it.

Vattimo's most surprising move came later. In *Belief* (1996) and *After Christianity* (2002), he argued that Christianity itself is the source of this weakening. The incarnation is *kenosis*, God's self-emptying (Philippians 2:7); God becomes weak and human; and secularization is the continuation of that process, not its betrayal. The only remaining commandment, he concluded, is charity. Vattimo was also a politician (a member of the European Parliament) and, late in life, a self-described "hermeneutic communist."

Critics charge that weak thought is too comfortable. If there are no facts, only interpretations, on what grounds do we oppose a lie, or a tyrant? Vattimo's former student Maurizio Ferraris broke with him and launched a "New Realism" (2012), arguing that postmodern talk of interpretation had prepared the ground for "post-truth" politics. Vattimo replied that the remedy for the abuse of interpretation is more conversation, not a return to strong truths. The debate is very much alive.

## Baudrillard: the nihilism of simulation

Jean Baudrillard (1929–2007), a French sociologist turned provocateur, thought the world had moved beyond the problem Nietzsche described. *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981) argues that in media-saturated consumer societies, images no longer represent reality; they precede and replace it. Baudrillard begins with a fable by Borges about an empire that drew a map so detailed it covered the whole territory. Today, he says, the map comes first: the territory rots beneath it, and what is left is the "desert of the real." He calls the result the *hyperreal*: a world of signs referring only to other signs. Disneyland is his famous example: it is presented as imaginary in order to make us believe that the rest of America is real, when in fact all of it belongs to the order of simulation. In 1991 he published three provocative articles collectively titled *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place*, arguing that for the Western public the war existed only as a television spectacle.

The last chapter of *Simulacra and Simulation*, "On Nihilism," argues that the old, heroic nihilism of Nietzsche and the Russian revolutionaries, which destroyed values in order to create, is over. Today's nihilism is a nihilism of *transparency* and *indifference*: meaning is not destroyed but neutralized, absorbed into endless circulation, so that nothing can be shocking any longer. The only remaining stance, Baudrillard suggests with deliberate perversity, is to push the system's logic to its extreme, a kind of theoretical terrorism. The tone of this world is not anguish but melancholy.

Popular culture paid Baudrillard the compliment of proving his point. In *The Matrix* (1999), Neo keeps illegal software hidden in a hollowed-out copy of *Simulacra and Simulation*, opened at the chapter "On Nihilism," and Morpheus welcomes him to "the desert of the real." The cast were reportedly asked to read the book. Baudrillard himself, in a 2004 interview, was unimpressed: he said the film confused the new problem of simulation with the classical problem of illusion, and that it was the sort of film the Matrix might have made about itself. A philosophy of simulation was turned into a simulation of philosophy.

## Brassier: nihilism unbound

The most uncompromising recent defense of nihilism comes from the British philosopher Ray Brassier. *Nihil Unbound: Enlightenment and Extinction* (2007) argues that philosophers since Nietzsche have been trying to *overcome* nihilism (through will, authenticity, Being, revolt, interpretation), and that every such attempt is a failure of nerve. Brassier wants the opposite: to carry nihilism through to its conclusion. His preface puts it in a sentence that has become a slogan:

> Nihilism is not an existential quandary but a speculative opportunity.
>
> — Ray Brassier, *Nihil Unbound: Enlightenment and Extinction*, Preface

Brassier's nihilism is not a mood but a consequence of science. Following the American philosopher Wilfrid Sellars, he distinguishes the *manifest image* (the world as it appears to us, full of persons, purposes and values) from the *scientific image* (the world as described by physics, biology and neuroscience). The scientific image is not just one more interpretation; it is, as far as it goes, *true*, and it has no place for purposes or meanings. Enlightenment means following the scientific image wherever it leads, including to the conclusion that our self-image as meaning-making persons is a useful but ultimately false model (here Brassier draws on the eliminative materialism of Paul and Patricia Churchland and on Thomas Metzinger, whom we met in [Chapter 13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md)).

The book's final chapter, "The Truth of Extinction," turns to cosmology. In some four or five billion years the sun will swell and engulf the Earth; much later, the universe itself will run down. Drawing on Jean-François Lyotard and the French philosopher Quentin Meillassoux, Brassier argues that this future extinction is not merely a distant event. It shows, *now*, that thought and meaning are contingent episodes in a universe that is indifferent to them. In a sense, everything is already dead: the extinction of meaning is a fact about its status, not just its future. Philosophy's task is not to console but to think this truth.

Brassier was one of the four participants in a 2007 workshop at Goldsmiths, London, that launched "speculative realism," a movement of philosophers committed to thinking reality as it is independently of human access (the others were Iain Hamilton Grant, Graham Harman and Meillassoux). Brassier later distanced himself from the label. He has also refined his position, stressing, after Sellars, that the scientific image is itself produced by norms of reasoning and that rational normativity is not eliminated by science. In an interview he summarized his stance by saying that he is a nihilist precisely because he still believes in truth. That is the crucial point. Brassier's nihilism rejects meaning *in* the world in the name of a commitment to truth *about* the world. Whether the commitment to truth is itself a value that survives nihilism, and why, is the question he leaves open, and it is Nietzsche's question in the third essay of the *Genealogy of Morals*.

## Optimistic nihilism

In 2017 the popular German science animation channel Kurzgesagt ("In a Nutshell") posted a short video called "Optimistic Nihilism." Its argument, illustrated by cute birds against the vastness of space, is simple: the universe is unimaginably big and old; we are tiny and brief; nothing we do will matter cosmically; *therefore* we are free. If there are no cosmic stakes, there are no cosmic failures. Enjoy the sunset, be kind, do what you love. The video has been viewed well over ten million times. "Optimistic nihilism" has become an online identity, alongside a large meme culture of cheerful despair (Nihilist Arby's, a parody account that rewrote fast-food advertising in the voice of an existential pessimist, was a brief hit of the mid-2010s).

What should a philosopher make of this? Three observations.

**It is not nihilism.** Optimistic nihilism denies *cosmic* meaning but affirms local values (joy, kindness, curiosity) and treats them as worth pursuing. That is not the view that nothing has value; it is the view that value is human-sized. In the vocabulary of [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md), it is cosmic nihilism combined with a rejection of existential nihilism. Its closest ancestors are Epicurus and Lucretius ([Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md)), who also inferred serenity from an indifferent universe, and Sartre ([Chapter 11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md)), for whom the absence of given meaning is the condition of freedom.

**Its inference is invalid as stated.** "Nothing matters, therefore enjoy yourself" has the same structure as Smerdyakov's "no God, therefore all is lawful" ([Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md)). If nothing matters, then enjoyment does not matter either. What the optimistic nihilist means is that the things that matter do not depend on cosmic significance. That is a respectable position, defended by philosophers such as Thomas Nagel and Susan Wolf ([Chapter 15](15-beyond-nihilism.md)), but it needs an argument the slogan does not give.

**It may be a defense mechanism.** Zapffe would classify it at once: an anchor (cosmic smallness as a stable point of view) combined with sublimation (turning dread into wonder and a nice video). The philosopher Nolen Gertz goes further. In *Nihilism* (2019) and *Nihilism and Technology* (2018) he argues that much of contemporary digital culture is a Nietzschean "passive nihilism" in disguise: technologies that relieve us of the burden of choosing, self-help that turns meaning into a product, and a cheerful indifference that leaves everything as it is. Optimistic nihilism, on this reading, is the last man's philosophy ([Chapter 6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)): we have invented happiness, and we blink.

These criticisms are not decisive. Optimistic nihilism is often a first step: the moment a young person, having lost a religious or cultural framework, realizes that the loss does not make life worthless. That moment is valuable. The question is what comes after it, and that is the subject of the last chapter.

## Living it

**Make a Dada object.** Take an everyday object, give it a title, and present it to someone as art. Observe their reaction, and your own. Did the gesture feel like destruction, play or creation? Duchamp thought it was a challenge; the art world thought it was a masterpiece. What did it turn out to be for you?

**Read "Before the Law" three times.** Read the parable once as a religious allegory (the Law is God), once as a political one (the Law is the state), and once as an existential one (the Law is meaning). Which reading is most disturbing? Then read the argument between the priest and K. that follows, and notice how each interpretation is undermined. Write one paragraph on what it is like to live in a world where commands are binding but their meaning cannot be fixed.

**Audit your feeds.** For one week, note each time you encounter "nothing matters" content: memes, videos, jokes about doom. For each, ask: is this a denial of value, a cosmic perspective, a defense mechanism, or a value in disguise? Gertz would ask a further question: is it helping you choose, or helping you avoid choosing?

## Summary

- Cultural nihilism is a *mood* and a set of *forms*, distinct from philosophical theses. Three patterns recur: negation becomes a style; despair becomes comedy; meaning survives its own denial.
- **Dada** (Zurich 1916; Tzara, Ball, Arp; Duchamp's *Fountain*): art against art as a response to the war; an active nihilism whose negations were absorbed by the institutions it attacked.
- **Kafka**: catastrophes without cause; authority without meaning ("Before the Law"); the inaccessible, self-interpreting Law as a modern form of nihilism; Kafka's comedy.
- **Eliot's *The Waste Land***: a civilization of fragments; "These fragments I have shored against my ruins."
- **Beckett**: *Waiting for Godot* and the structure of waiting; "Nothing to be done"; Esslin's Theatre of the Absurd and San Quentin; "Fail better" and its fate as a slogan.
- **Film and music**: from Bergman to *Everything Everywhere All at Once*; *The Big Lebowski* (nihilists who want money); punk's "no future"; the black metal case as a warning that "nihilism" may cloak very bad values.
- **Vattimo**: weak thought; accomplished nihilism as our chance; *Verwindung*; kenosis and charity; the realist backlash.
- **Baudrillard**: simulacra and hyperreality; nihilism of transparency and indifference; *The Matrix*.
- **Brassier**: nihilism as a "speculative opportunity"; the scientific image; extinction; a nihilist "because he still believes in truth."
- **Optimistic nihilism**: cosmic nihilism with local values; not strictly nihilism; an invalid inference as a slogan; possibly a defense mechanism, possibly a first step.

**Tristan Tzara:** You have read my manifesto. Dada means nothing. We want to sweep everything away: logic, morality, art, the future. Will you join us?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Tzara in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I understand why you want to. The war made a mockery of everything Europe claimed to value. But if you sweep everything away, what's left? Just you, shouting nonsense in a nightclub.

**Tzara:** And is that not better than shouting patriotic slogans in a trench? At least our nonsense kills no one.

**You:** Fair. But you said Dada means nothing. Then why perform it? Why write manifestos? A manifesto is a statement of what you stand for.

**Tzara:** A manifesto against manifestos. A contradiction. Dada is contradiction.

**You:** That sounds clever, but it lets you escape every objection. Whatever I say, you can say "Dada contradicts itself, so what?"

**Tzara:** Exactly. You have understood Dada.

**You:** Then let me try another way. When Ball recited "Karawane," people laughed, but some of them felt something, didn't they? Something like a liturgy.

**Tzara:** Ball felt that. He went back to the Church afterward. I did not.

**You:** But you kept making things. Poems, manifestos, performances. You didn't fall silent. Doesn't that show you valued something?

**Tzara:** Spontaneity. Life. Freedom from the dead systems.

**You:** Then Dada doesn't mean nothing. It means that those things matter more than logic and tradition. That's a value judgment.

**Tzara:** Perhaps. But it is a value judgment made with a laugh, which makes it harder to turn into a system.

**You:** Until the museums buy it.

**Tzara:** Yes. That is the joke on us. Every revolt becomes a style. Will you tell me how to avoid that?

**You:** I don't think you can. Maybe that's not a failure. Maybe the point was never to stay outside, but to make room.

**Tzara:** Room for what?

**You:** For whatever comes next. You cleared the ground. Somebody else has to build.

**Tzara:** Builders! How dull. But perhaps necessary. Go on, then. Build. And we will come back to knock it down.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether nothing matters cheerfully

**Ray Brassier:** You call yourself an optimistic nihilist. You say the universe is meaningless and that this makes you free. Tell me what you mean by "free."

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Brassier in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I mean that nothing is required of me by the universe. There's no cosmic plan I can fail. So I can choose what to care about and enjoy my life without guilt.

**Brassier:** Do you choose what to care about?

**You:** To some extent. I chose to study philosophy. I choose my friends.

**Brassier:** Did you choose to find philosophy interesting? Did you choose to want friends?

**You:** No, I suppose those came from my nature and my upbringing.

**Brassier:** So the "you" that chooses is itself the product of processes you did not choose: evolution, neurology, culture. The scientific image does not stop at the edge of your will.

**You:** Fine. But that doesn't make my enjoyment any less real.

**Brassier:** Real as a neurological event, yes. Does it *matter*?

**You:** It matters to me.

**Brassier:** That is a fact about you, not a fact about value. You began by saying that nothing matters. Now you say that some things matter to you. Those are different claims.

**You:** Then maybe I'm not a nihilist. Maybe I'm someone who thinks value is local rather than cosmic.

**Brassier:** That is a respectable position. But it is not the cheerful one in your video. It requires an account of why local mattering is real mattering and not just a feeling. Otherwise you have only replaced God with your preferences.

**You:** And what do you offer instead? You say everything is already dead. That doesn't sound like an improvement.

**Brassier:** I do not offer consolation. I offer truth. I am a nihilist because I still believe in truth: I think there is a way the world is, and that we can know it, and that it does not care about us.

**You:** Then you value truth. That's a value. Why does it survive your nihilism, when meaning doesn't?

**Brassier:** That is the right question. Nietzsche asked it too: what does the will to truth mean? My answer is that the norms of reasoning are not facts in the world but conditions for describing it. They do not make the universe meaningful. They make *us* capable of knowing it isn't.

**You:** So you're also not a complete nihilist.

**Brassier:** No one is, who argues. The question is only where you draw the line, and whether you draw it honestly.

</details>

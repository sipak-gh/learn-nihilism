# Chapter 15 — Beyond Nihilism

This course began with a question: is nothing worth anything? Fourteen chapters later you have met that question in many forms: Silenus's verdict that it is best not to be born, Jacobi's accusation against Fichte, Bazarov's scalpel, Nietzsche's madman, Kierkegaard's despair, Heidegger's nothing, Camus's absurd, Sartre's freedom, Mackie's error theory, Benatar's asymmetry, Kafka's doorkeeper and the cheerful birds of optimistic nihilism. This final chapter does three things. First, it sets the great *responses* to nihilism side by side (Nietzschean, existentialist, absurdist, religious, Stoic and Buddhist) so that you can compare them as live options. Second, it introduces the analytic debate about *meaning in life* that has flourished since the 1970s, with Thomas Nagel, Susan Wolf and Thaddeus Metz as guides. Third, it offers a method for building values after nihilism, and a graduate-level reading path for going further. Previous: [Chapter 14 — Nihilism in Modern Culture](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md). Next: [Appendix A — Glossary](appendix-a-glossary.md).

This chapter is also a bridge to the other three courses. It links to [learn-stoicism, Chapter 16 — Living a Stoic Life](../learn-stoicism/16-living-a-stoic-life.md), [learn-ethics, Chapter 22 — Building Your Ethics](../learn-ethics/22-building-your-ethics.md) and [learn-philosophy, Chapter 24 — Building a Philosophy of Life](../learn-philosophy/24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md), which approach the same synthesis from different directions.

## Where we have arrived

It helps to restate what nihilism threatens, in the terms of [Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md). There are several distinct claims, and a response to one is not a response to the others.

- **Cosmic nihilism:** the universe has no purpose and does not care about us.
- **Existential nihilism:** human lives have no meaning.
- **Moral nihilism:** nothing is morally right or wrong ([Chapter 12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md)).
- **Epistemological nihilism:** there is no knowledge, or no truth.
- **Political nihilism:** existing institutions deserve only destruction ([Chapter 4](04-russian-nihilism.md)).
- **Pessimism** (strictly not nihilism): existence is on balance bad ([Chapter 13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md)).

Most of the thinkers in this course accept cosmic nihilism, at least for the world as science describes it. Very few accept existential nihilism in the end. The interesting question has never been whether the universe has a purpose, which most modern philosophers doubt, but whether that matters to whether *our* lives do. The responses below are different answers to that question.

## Two crises

Before the theories, two case histories, because nihilism is usually not a conclusion reached in the study but a crisis that happens to a person.

**Mill at twenty.** John Stuart Mill was raised by his father as a utilitarian reasoning machine: Greek at three, logic at twelve, a life devoted to reforming the world. In the autumn of 1826, aged twenty, he fell into a depression and asked himself a question:

> "Suppose that all your objects in life were realized; that all the changes in institutions and opinions which you are looking forward to, could be completely effected at this very instant: would this be a great joy and happiness to you?" And an irrepressible self-consciousness distinctly answered, "No!"
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *Autobiography*, ch. V

The whole foundation of his life, he writes, fell down. He recovered slowly: he wept over a scene in Marmontel's memoirs (proof that he could still feel), and in 1828 found in Wordsworth's poetry a "culture of the feelings" his education had starved. The lesson he drew is one of the most quoted pieces of practical wisdom in English:

> Ask yourself whether you are happy, and you cease to be so. The only chance is to treat, not happiness, but some end external to it, as the purpose of life.
>
> — Mill, *Autobiography*, ch. V

**Tolstoy at fifty.** Leo Tolstoy, at the height of his fame, rich, healthy, the author of *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina*, found himself unable to answer a child's question: what will come of what I am doing today or tomorrow, of my whole life? He hid a rope from himself so as not to hang himself, and stopped going shooting so as not to be tempted by his gun. *A Confession* (1882) tells the story. It retells an Eastern fable: a traveler, fleeing a beast, climbs down into a dry well, sees a dragon at the bottom, and clings to a branch while two mice, one black and one white (night and day), gnaw through it. He notices drops of honey on the leaves and licks them. Tolstoy says the honey of family and art no longer tasted sweet to him, because he could see the dragon and the mice. He looked for an answer in science (which answered other questions) and philosophy (which, from Solomon to Schopenhauer, confirmed his despair). He found it, eventually, in the lives of simple working people, who lived as if life had meaning and died without terror. Their faith, he concluded, was a kind of knowledge he lacked, and he spent the rest of his life trying to acquire a version of it, stripped of what he regarded as the Church's superstitions.

Note the difference. Mill's crisis was answered by *feeling* and by a change in *method* (pursue other things, and happiness will follow). Tolstoy's was answered by *faith*, and by a change of *community*. The responses that follow are elaborations of these two possibilities and several others.

Earlier chapters examined the first four in detail. The last two deserve a closer look here, because they show that nihilism is not only a Western, post-Christian problem.

### The Stoic response

The Stoics anticipated cosmic smallness long before modern astronomy. Marcus Aurelius, emperor of Rome, reminded himself constantly how brief and insignificant everything is:

> The time of a man's life is as a point; the substance of it ever flowing, the sense obscure; and the whole composition of the body tending to corruption. [...] What is it then that will adhere and follow? Only one thing, philosophy.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* II.15 (tr. Meric Casaubon; II.17 in most modern editions)

The "view from above," which Marcus practiced as a spiritual exercise ([learn-stoicism, Chapter 12](../learn-stoicism/12-stoic-practices.md)), looks exactly like the cosmic perspective that, for Nagel, produces the sense of absurdity. The Stoic draws the opposite conclusion: seen from above, fame, wealth and pain shrink to nothing, and only the quality of one's own will (virtue) remains. That is not nihilism but a radical *relocation* of value: from outcomes, which are not in our control, to the character of our choices, which is.

The difficulty, taken up in [learn-stoicism, Chapter 16](../learn-stoicism/16-living-a-stoic-life.md), is that ancient Stoicism rested on physics: the universe is rational, governed by divine *logos*, and to live according to nature is to live according to reason. Without that physics, why should virtue be the only good? Modern Stoics such as Lawrence Becker (*A New Stoicism*, 1998) try to rebuild the ethics on naturalistic foundations. Whether they succeed is the central question for anyone who wants to use Stoicism as an answer to nihilism.

### The Buddhist response

Buddhism begins with something that sounds like pessimism, the first noble truth that life is *dukkha* (suffering, unsatisfactoriness), and with doctrines that sound like nihilism: no permanent self (*anattā*), the impermanence of all things, and in the Mahāyāna, the emptiness (*śūnyatā*) of all phenomena. Nagarjuna's opponents accused him of nihilism, as [Chapter 2](02-ancient-roots.md) showed. Yet the tradition explicitly rejects nihilism. In the Pali canon the Buddha describes his teaching as a middle way between *eternalism* (the self and world exist permanently) and *annihilationism* (*ucchedavāda*: nothing exists, or the self is simply destroyed at death, so nothing matters). The point of seeing impermanence is not despair but release from clinging:

> Look upon the world as a bubble, look upon it as a mirage: the king of death does not see him who thus looks down upon the world.
>
> — *The Dhammapada*, v. 170 (tr. F. Max Müller)

The most sustained attempt to bring this tradition into conversation with European nihilism came from the Kyoto School in Japan. Keiji Nishitani (1900–1990) studied with Heidegger in Freiburg in the late 1930s. In *The Self-Overcoming of Nihilism* (1949) and *Religion and Nothingness* (1961), he argued that Western nihilism, the experience of the "nihility" (*kyomu*) at the bottom of every value, is a real and necessary stage. But it is not the end. If nihility is pushed through to its own ground, it opens onto *śūnyatā*, an emptiness that is not the negation of things but the field in which each thing appears as what it is, without needing an external purpose. Nietzsche's attempt to overcome nihilism by will, Nishitani thought, remained trapped in the ego. Emptiness overcomes it by dissolving the ego that demanded meaning in the first place.

The Buddhist response asks more than any other: not new values, but a transformation of the self that holds them. Its contemporary forms (secular mindfulness, for instance) often keep the techniques and drop the metaphysics, which raises a question parallel to the Stoic one: does the practice work without the worldview?

### Choosing among them

The six responses are not all rivals. Several can be combined: Camus is close to the Stoics in his attention to the present; Nishitani built on Nietzsche and Heidegger; Kierkegaard's religious stage incorporates the ethical. What they share is a refusal of *passive* nihilism, the drift into "nothing matters, so why bother," which Nietzsche identified as the real danger. What divides them is where they locate the source of value: in the will (Nietzsche, Sartre), in revolt (Camus), in God (Kierkegaard, Tolstoy), in reason and nature (Stoics), or in the dissolution of the self (Buddhism).

## The meaning-in-life debate

Since the 1970s analytic philosophers have treated "the meaning of life" as a respectable question, with a literature of its own. Its main achievement has been to separate questions that the nihilist runs together. Thaddeus Metz, whose *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry is the standard survey, distinguishes three families of theory.

| Theory | Meaning comes from | Representative | Main problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Supernaturalism** | A relationship with God or a soul | Tolstoy; William Lane Craig; (in a sense) Kierkegaard | If God's purposes are imposed on us, how do they make *our* lives meaningful? |
| **Subjectivism** | Getting what you want, or caring about something | Richard Taylor ([Chapter 10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)); Harry Frankfurt | Seems to make a life devoted to counting blades of grass meaningful |
| **Objectivism** | Engaging with things of objective value | Metz; Robert Nozick (in part) | Seems to ignore whether the person cares |
| **Hybrid** | Caring about things that are worth caring about | Susan Wolf | Who decides what is "worth" caring about? |

### Nagel: two standpoints

Thomas Nagel's contribution, developed from "The Absurd" (1971) into ch. XI of *The View from Nowhere* (1986), is to locate the problem of meaning in a clash between two standpoints that every reflective person occupies. From the inside, our lives are full of things that matter: this exam, this friendship, this pain. From the outside, the "view from nowhere" that we can always take up, they matter no more than the doings of ants. Neither standpoint can be abandoned. We cannot live only from outside (we would be paralyzed) or only from inside (we would be dishonest). The sense of absurdity is the felt collision between them. Nagel does not think the collision can be resolved, only managed, and he recommends irony and a certain humility: the external view does not show that our concerns are worthless, only that their worth is not cosmic.

### Wolf: when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness

Susan Wolf, in *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters* (2010), argues that meaning is a dimension of a good life distinct from both happiness and morality. Her formula is short:

> Meaning arises when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness.
>
> — Susan Wolf, *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters*, Lecture 1

Both halves are necessary. A person who is passionately engaged with something worthless (Wolf's examples include someone whose life revolves around a pet goldfish and a man who spends his days copying out *War and Peace* by hand; a devoted collector of rubber bands would do as well) has subjective attraction without meaning. A person who does valuable work but is alienated from it (the dutiful doctor who hates medicine) has objective value without meaning. And Wolf's "Blob," who spends his days in front of the television drinking beer, has neither. A meaningful life is one in which a person loves things worthy of love and engages with them in a positive way.

The account has a direct application to nihilism. The optimistic nihilist ([Chapter 14](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md)), in its slogan form, supplies only the subjective half: do what you love. The cosmic nihilist denies that anything can satisfy the objective half. Wolf's reply is that "objective" does not mean "cosmic." The value of friendship, knowledge, art or a just society does not require that the universe care; it requires only that these things are genuinely better objects of love than rubber bands, and Wolf thinks almost everyone, on reflection, agrees that they are. Critics charge elitism: who is the philosopher to tell the rubber-band collector that his life is less meaningful? Wolf replies that she is not ranking persons but describing a distinction that the collector himself would recognize.

### Metz: the fundamentality theory

Thaddeus Metz defends a purely objective account. After surveying dozens of rival theories, he proposes that a life is more meaningful the more it involves using one's reason in positive ways toward the *fundamental conditions* of human existence: the conditions that explain much else about human life. Hence the classic triad of the good, the true and the beautiful: moral achievement that addresses the basic conditions of people's lives (Mandela, Mother Teresa); intellectual discovery that explains the basic structure of reality (Darwin, Einstein); and art that illuminates the basic features of the human condition (Dostoevsky, Picasso). The theory explains why curing cancer confers more meaning than curing hiccups, and why the rubber-band collector's life falls short. It also implies that meaning comes in degrees, and that ordinary lives can have some, through raising children well, for instance, without approaching the paradigms.

### Two further thoughts

**Scheffler's afterlife.** Samuel Scheffler (*Death and the Afterlife*, 2013) proposes a thought experiment that reverses the usual nihilist worry. Suppose you knew that you would live a normal life, but that thirty days after your death the earth would be destroyed by an asteroid. Would your projects still matter to you? Scheffler argues that for most people, many would not: cancer research, writing books, building institutions, even raising children would lose much of their point. The lesson is surprising. We worry that our lives are meaningless because *we* will die. But our values depend far less on our own survival than on the survival of *others* after us: the "collective afterlife." Meaning is less individual, and less cosmic, than both the nihilist and the optimist assume.

**Hägglund's finitude.** The Swedish philosopher Martin Hägglund (*This Life*, 2019) argues against the premise, common from Ecclesiastes to Tolstoy, that transience destroys meaning. On the contrary: only a finite being can care about anything, because only for a finite being is time precious and loss possible. An eternal life would make nothing urgent and so nothing matter. Bernard Williams had argued something similar about the tedium of immortality ([Chapter 11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md)). Nihilism inferred meaninglessness from mortality; Hägglund infers meaning from it.

## Building values after nihilism

The responses and theories above can be turned into a method. What follows is not a doctrine but a sequence of questions, drawn from the whole course, for anyone trying to work out what they value once the old guarantees have gone.

1. **Name your nihilism.** Which kind is troubling you: cosmic, existential, moral, epistemological, political, or pessimism? Each has different arguments and different answers ([Chapter 1](01-what-is-nihilism.md)). Much distress comes from treating a true cosmic claim as if it entailed a false existential one.
2. **Check the inference.** Does "the universe has no purpose" entail "my life has no meaning"? Does "there are no moral facts" entail "everything is permitted"? Usually not, as Chapters [12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) and [14](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md) showed. Find the hidden premise and ask whether you believe it.
3. **Ask Mill's question.** If all your current goals were achieved tomorrow, would that be a great joy? If not, the problem may not be nihilism but a mismatch between your goals and what you actually care about.
4. **Apply Wolf's test.** List what you spend your days on. For each item: do you love it (subjective attraction)? Is it worth loving (objective attractiveness)? Where the answers diverge, something needs to change: either your engagement or your projects.
5. **Apply Scheffler's test.** Which of your projects would still matter if humanity ended thirty days after your death? Those that wouldn't depend on others, and future others, for their point. Value them accordingly, and do something for those others.
6. **Choose a practice, not just a view.** Every response in this chapter came with exercises: Stoic evening review, Buddhist meditation, Kierkegaardian self-examination, Camusian attention to the present, Nietzschean imagining of eternal recurrence. A conviction that isn't practiced does not survive a bad night. See [learn-stoicism, Chapter 12](../learn-stoicism/12-stoic-practices.md) for a practical toolkit.
7. **Include others.** Beauvoir's lesson ([Chapter 11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md)): to will oneself free is to will others free. Mill's lesson: happiness comes from aiming at something outside oneself. A value system built entirely around one's own meaning tends to collapse into the problem it was meant to solve.
8. **Revise.** Expect to repeat this process. William James, who survived his own crisis in his late twenties, noted that whether life seems worth living may depend on the state of your digestion, repeating the old newspaper joke that "it depends on the liver." That is not a reason for cynicism but for patience.

James's own conclusion, addressed to a group of Harvard students in 1895, is the best short statement of the pragmatic answer to nihilism, and a fitting place to end the argument:

> Be not afraid of life. Believe that life is worth living, and your belief will help create the fact.
>
> — William James, "Is Life Worth Living?" in *The Will to Believe* (1897)

James is not saying that wishing makes it so. He is pointing to a class of facts, those about the value of a particular human life, that depend partly on the attitudes and actions of the person living it. A life lived as if it mattered becomes one in which more things matter. That is not a proof against nihilism. It is a reason to doubt that nihilism is the kind of claim that a proof could settle. For more on James and pragmatism, see [learn-philosophy, Chapter 17](../learn-philosophy/17-pragmatism.md).

## A graduate reading path

For readers who want to continue at graduate level, here is a structured path. Each stage assumes the previous one.

**Stage 1. Consolidate the primary texts.** Reread, in full, the books this course excerpted: Nietzsche's *Gay Science* Book V and *Genealogy of Morals*; Kierkegaard's *Sickness unto Death*; Heidegger's "What Is Metaphysics?" and "The Question of Being"; Camus's *Myth of Sisyphus* and *The Rebel*; Beauvoir's *Ethics of Ambiguity*; Dostoevsky's *Brothers Karamazov*. Read Nietzsche in the Kaufmann or Cambridge translations alongside the bundled public-domain ones.

**Stage 2. The history of the concept.**
- Michael Allen Gillespie, *Nihilism Before Nietzsche* (University of Chicago Press, 1995): traces nihilism to late medieval nominalism and the omnipotent God.
- Karen L. Carr, *The Banalization of Nihilism* (SUNY Press, 1992): how nihilism went from crisis to commonplace.
- Stanley Rosen, *Nihilism: A Philosophical Essay* (Yale University Press, 1969; St. Augustine's Press, 2000).
- Donald A. Crosby, *The Specter of the Absurd: Sources and Criticisms of Modern Nihilism* (SUNY Press, 1988).
- Johan Goudsblom, *Nihilism and Culture* (Blackwell, 1980).
- Bernard Reginster, *The Affirmation of Life: Nietzsche on Overcoming Nihilism* (Harvard University Press, 2006): the best analytic study of Nietzsche's response.

**Stage 3. Meaning in life.**
- Thaddeus Metz, *Meaning in Life: An Analysis* (Oxford, 2013).
- Susan Wolf, *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters* (Princeton, 2010).
- Iddo Landau, *Finding Meaning in an Imperfect World* (Oxford, 2017).
- Joshua W. Seachris (ed.), *Exploring the Meaning of Life: An Anthology and Guide* (Wiley-Blackwell, 2012); E. D. Klemke and Steven M. Cahn (eds.), *The Meaning of Life: A Reader*, 4th ed. (Oxford, 2017).
- Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife* (Oxford, 2013); Martin Hägglund, *This Life* (Pantheon, 2019); Kieran Setiya, *Midlife: A Philosophical Guide* (Princeton, 2017).

**Stage 4. Normativity and metaethics.** Christine Korsgaard, *The Sources of Normativity* (Cambridge, 1996), on how practical identity grounds obligation; Jonas Olson, *Moral Error Theory* (Oxford, 2014); Derek Parfit, *On What Matters* (Oxford, 2011–17), vol. 2; Sharon Street, "Constructivism about Reasons," *Oxford Studies in Metaethics* 3 (2008). Pair with [learn-ethics, Chapter 17](../learn-ethics/17-metaethics.md) and [Chapter 22](../learn-ethics/22-building-your-ethics.md).

**Stage 5. Continental and comparative.** Simon Critchley, *Very Little... Almost Nothing: Death, Philosophy, Literature* (Routledge, 1997; 2nd ed. 2004); Ashley Woodward, *Nihilism in Postmodernity* (Davies Group, 2009); Hubert Dreyfus and Sean Dorrance Kelly, *All Things Shining* (Free Press, 2011); Charles Taylor, *A Secular Age* (Harvard, 2007); Keiji Nishitani, *Religion and Nothingness*, tr. Jan Van Bragt (University of California Press, 1982); Jay L. Garfield, *The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way: Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (Oxford, 1995).

**Stage 6. Research questions.** Pick one and write a 6,000-word paper:
- Is the inference from cosmic to existential nihilism valid on any reasonable reconstruction?
- Can moral fictionalism survive the "critical context" objection?
- Does Benatar's asymmetry depend on a confusion about the bearers of value?
- Is Wolf's hybrid view stable, or does it collapse into objectivism?
- Is Nishitani's "self-overcoming of nihilism" a philosophical argument or a religious practice, and does the distinction matter?
- Is Scheffler's "collective afterlife" a solution to existential nihilism or a new source of it, given the possibility of human extinction?

Useful journals: *Journal of Nietzsche Studies*, *The Journal of Ethics*, *Philosophy East and West*, *Religious Studies*, and the open-access *Journal of Philosophy of Life*.

## Summary

- Nihilism comes in several kinds; most modern thinkers accept cosmic nihilism but reject existential nihilism. The decisive question is whether the first entails the second.
- **Two crises:** Mill's (answered by feeling and by pursuing ends beyond happiness) and Tolstoy's (answered by faith and community).
- **Six responses:** Nietzschean affirmation, existentialist self-creation, absurdist revolt, religious faith, Stoic relocation of value to virtue, Buddhist release from clinging. Each has a characteristic objection; several can be combined.
- **Stoicism** shares the cosmic perspective but relocates value; its challenge is to survive without providence. **Buddhism** rejects both eternalism and annihilationism; Nishitani's Kyoto School treats nihilism as a stage to be passed through into emptiness.
- **Meaning in life:** supernaturalism, subjectivism, objectivism and hybrids. **Nagel**: a clash of internal and external standpoints. **Wolf**: meaning arises when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness. **Metz**: the fundamentality theory. **Scheffler**: our values depend on a collective afterlife. **Hägglund**: finitude is the condition of caring.
- **Building values:** name your nihilism, check the inference, ask Mill's question, apply Wolf's and Scheffler's tests, choose a practice, include others, revise.
- **James:** "Believe that life is worth living, and your belief will help create the fact."
- A graduate reading path: primary texts; the history of the concept (Gillespie, Carr, Rosen, Reginster); meaning in life (Metz, Wolf, Landau); normativity (Korsgaard, Olson, Parfit); continental and comparative (Critchley, Nishitani, Garfield).

## Dialogues

### Dialogue 1 — On what will come of it

**Leo Tolstoy:** I had everything: fame, family, health, the respect of all Russia. And I could not answer a question a child might ask. What will come of what I am doing today and tomorrow? What will come of my whole life? Can you answer it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Tolstoy in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** In the long run, nothing will come of it. Your books will be forgotten eventually, and so will mine. But I don't think that's the right measure. Things can matter now without lasting forever.

**Tolstoy:** That is what I told myself for years. And then one day it stopped working. I could see the dragon at the bottom of the well and the mice gnawing the branch. The honey no longer tasted sweet.

**You:** Maybe that's not an argument, though. It sounds like depression. The honey didn't change; your ability to taste it did.

**Tolstoy:** You think my question was a symptom?

**You:** I think it came from a real problem, but the despair had another cause. Mill had the same experience, and he recovered when he could feel again, not when he found an argument.

**Tolstoy:** Mill recovered by reading poetry. I recovered by watching peasants. Perhaps we are not so different. Neither of us found the answer in a book of philosophy.

**You:** But you said the peasants had faith. Do I need faith to answer you?

**Tolstoy:** What do you think faith is?

**You:** Believing in God, I suppose. In something beyond this world.

**Tolstoy:** I came to think faith is the knowledge of the meaning of human life, by which a person does not destroy himself but lives. The peasants did not have arguments. They had a way of living in which the question did not arise, or arose and was answered by the next task.

**You:** That sounds like Zapffe's anchoring. They avoided the question.

**Tolstoy:** Or they had an answer that is lived rather than spoken. Tell me: when you sit with a friend who is dying, do you ask what will come of it?

**You:** No. I just sit with them.

**Tolstoy:** And is it meaningless?

**You:** No. It's one of the most meaningful things I can imagine.

**Tolstoy:** Then you know something that your philosophy has not yet put into words. That is where I would begin.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether loving anything is enough

**Susan Wolf:** You have taken this course and survived it. Tell me what gives your life meaning.

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Wolf in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what she will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** My friends, my work, music. I'm not sure the universe cares about any of them, but I do.

**Wolf:** Good. And if you cared just as much about collecting rubber bands, would your life be equally meaningful?

**You:** If I loved it as much, maybe. Who's to say rubber bands are worse than music?

**Wolf:** You are. Do you think they are?

**You:** Honestly, yes. Music can express things, move people, connect them. A rubber band collection can't.

**Wolf:** Then you accept that some things are more worth loving than others. That is the objective half of my view. It does not require that the universe care, only that there are better and worse reasons to love things.

**You:** But aren't those reasons just my preferences in disguise? A Brassier would say "music moves people" is a fact about neurons, not about value.

**Wolf:** It is a fact about neurons *and* a reason. When you explain to a friend why a piece of music is great, you point to features of it. You don't point to your preference. You could be wrong about it, and you could be persuaded.

**You:** So you're a realist about value?

**Wolf:** I am careful about that word. I am confident that friendship is a better object of love than rubber bands, and I don't need a metaphysical theory to be confident of that. If a theory told me otherwise, I would doubt the theory.

**You:** That's the Moorean move from Chapter 12.

**Wolf:** It is. Does it bother you?

**You:** A bit. It feels like it ends the argument without winning it.

**Wolf:** Perhaps the argument with nihilism is not one that is won. It is one that is outgrown. You do not refute the person who says nothing matters. You show him, by living, what it looks like when things do.

**You:** And if he says that's just my anchor?

**Wolf:** Then you agree that it is an anchor, and add that some anchors hold in better ground than others. Now: what will you do tomorrow?

### Dialogue 3 — On whether emptiness is just nothing

**Keiji Nishitani:** You have followed nihilism from Silenus to the cosmic horror of Lovecraft. In Europe it seemed to end in nothingness. Tell me: is nothingness the bottom?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Nishitani in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It feels like the bottom. Once you see that no value is built into the universe, there's nothing underneath. That's what nihilism means.

**Nishitani:** When you look into that nothing, who is looking?

**You:** I am.

**Nishitani:** And this "I," does it stand outside the nothing, like a spectator at the edge of a pit?

**You:** I suppose it does. I'm the one who's disappointed that there's no meaning.

**Nishitani:** Then the nothing you see is still an object for a self. It is the shadow of the self's demand. Europe discovered nihility, but it kept the self that demanded meaning, and so the nihility appeared as a threat: a void where purpose should be.

**You:** What's the alternative? To stop demanding meaning?

**Nishitani:** Not to stop by an act of will. Nietzsche tried that. He tried to overcome nihilism by willing, and the will remained at the center. The alternative is to let the nothing go all the way through, even through the one who looks. Then it is no longer a void opposed to things. It is the openness in which each thing appears as itself.

**You:** That sounds like a mystical experience, not an argument.

**Nishitani:** It is a practice before it is a doctrine, yes. But consider a simpler case. When you are absorbed in music, or in a conversation with a friend, do you ask whether it has a cosmic purpose?

**You:** No. The question doesn't come up.

**Nishitani:** Is that because you have forgotten something, as Zapffe would say, or because there was nothing missing?

**You:** In the moment, nothing seems missing. But afterward the question comes back.

**Nishitani:** It returns with the self that steps back to evaluate. The Buddha called this clinging. Emptiness does not deny the music or the friend. It denies only that they need something beyond themselves to be what they are.

**You:** So the problem of meaning comes from asking for a foundation that nothing could have.

**Nishitani:** You have just restated the nihilist's discovery. The question is what you do with it. You can stand at the edge of the pit, or you can notice that you were never outside it, and that it holds everything you love.

**You:** I'm not sure I can do the second.

**Nishitani:** Nobody can do it by being sure. Sit for twenty minutes tomorrow, and watch who it is that wants an answer.

</details>

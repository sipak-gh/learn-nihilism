# Learn Nihilism: From Ecclesiastes to the Absurd and Beyond

Nihilism is the suspicion that nothing really matters: that life has no given meaning, that morality has no foundation, that the universe neither knows nor cares that we are here. Most people meet it as a mood, at three in the morning or in a philosophy class, and try to get away from it as fast as they can. This course does the opposite. It takes the suspicion seriously, follows it through 3,000 years of argument, and asks what is left standing.

A great deal is left standing, and that is the fun of it. The people who stared hardest into the void were not dull or gloomy writers. A Hebrew sage called everything "vapor" and still told his readers to eat their bread with joy. A Russian medical student who "believed in nothing" dissected frogs for the good of humanity. Nietzsche announced the death of God and then spent his sane life asking how to say yes to existence without him. Camus said that the only serious philosophical problem is suicide, and answered it with a man pushing a rock uphill, whom we must imagine happy. Following them, you learn exactly which of your beliefs rest on what, which of the nihilist's arguments are good, and where they stop working. Everyone builds values on some foundation. This course lets you inspect yours before you build on it.

## Who it's for

Anyone who has wondered whether anything matters and wants a better answer than a shrug or a slogan. No background in philosophy is assumed: the early chapters define every term and walk through every argument. The course ramps up steadily, and by the last chapters you will be reading the current scholarly debates (error theory, antinatalism, the meaning-in-life literature) at roughly a graduate-seminar level, with the secondary literature named so you can go further.

## What you need

- **No software** beyond an EPUB reader (Apple Books, Calibre, KOReader, Thorium, Foliate, or any e-reader).
- The **bundled readings**: 52 public-domain books from Project Gutenberg in [readings/](readings/README.md), from Ecclesiastes and Lucretius to Nietzsche, Dostoevsky and Kafka.
- A few **procure-yourself books**: the twentieth-century authors are still in copyright, so you will need to buy or borrow them. The essential ones are Camus's *The Myth of Sisyphus*, Sartre's *Nausea*, Beauvoir's *The Ethics of Ambiguity*, Heidegger's "What Is Metaphysics?", Mackie's *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* and Nolen Gertz's short *Nihilism*. Each chapter's Required reading block names the edition; [Appendix C](appendix-c-reading-list.md) collects them all.
- A notebook. Several exercises ask you to write.

## How to use this course

1. **Read the chapters in order.** Each one builds on the vocabulary and arguments of the ones before, and the course's argument has an arc: from the first ancient intimations, through the word's birth and its nineteenth-century crisis, to the twentieth-century answers and today's debates.
2. **Do the Required reading.** Each chapter opens with a short list: sections of the bundled EPUBs (linked directly) and, where needed, procure-yourself books with full details. The chapter quotes and explains the key passages, but nothing replaces reading the originals. Optional "Further reading" follows.
3. **Try the Living it section.** Every chapter ends its main text with a concrete practice: a way to test the idea against your own life, or to use it in building your own values. These are tied to the texts, not generic self-help.
4. **Use the Summary** to review before moving on.
5. **Work through the Dialogues.** Each chapter closes with two to four imagined conversations in the tradition of Plato's dialogues. A questioner probes someone holding a view from the chapter; the questioner may be Socrates or another philosopher in character (Nietzsche, Camus, Kierkegaard, a Pyrrhonist, Zarathustra). The dialogues are invented teaching exercises, not quotations, though they stay true to each thinker's positions. Each one stops at a *Your turn* prompt: write your answer, then open **Continue the dialogue** to see how the conversation might go on.

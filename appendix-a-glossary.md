# Appendix A — Glossary

Key terms of the course in alphabetical order, with the original-language term where one matters and the chapter where the term is treated most fully. Use it to check a word you have forgotten, or as a review list: if you can explain each entry in two sentences and say who would disagree, you have the course's vocabulary. Previous: [Chapter 15 — Beyond Nihilism](15-beyond-nihilism.md). Next: [Appendix B — Timeline](appendix-b-timeline.md).

| Term | Original | Meaning | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Absurd, the** | French *l'absurde* | For Camus, not a property of the world or of us but of the relation between them: the confrontation between the human demand for clarity and meaning and "the unreasonable silence of the world." | [10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md) |
| **Active nihilism** | German *aktiver Nihilismus* | Nietzsche's term for nihilism as "a sign of *enhanced spiritual strength*": the strength that destroys values it no longer believes in, as a prelude to creating new ones. Opposed to passive nihilism. | [6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) |
| **Amor fati** | Latin, "love of fate" | Nietzsche's "formula for greatness in man" (*Ecce Homo*): that "a man wishes nothing to be different, either in front of him or behind him, or for all eternity"; not merely bearing what is necessary but loving it. | [7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md) |
| **Amoralism** | — | The practical stance of someone unmoved by moral considerations. A type of person, not a metaethical theory: a realist can be an amoralist, and an error theorist can be kind. | [12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) |
| **Anguish** | French *angoisse*; German *Angst* | In Heidegger, the mood in which beings as a whole slip away and the nothing is revealed; in Sartre, the consciousness of one's own freedom and responsibility. Distinguished from fear (*Furcht*), which has a definite object. | [9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md), [11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md) |
| **Antinatalism** | from Latin *natalis*, "of birth" | The view that it is wrong, or always a harm to the person created, to bring new sentient beings into existence. Defended by Benatar's asymmetry argument and, differently, from consent (Shiffrin). | [13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md) |
| **Asymmetry argument** | — | Benatar's claim that the absence of pain is good even if no one exists to enjoy that absence, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived of it; hence coming into existence is always a harm. | [13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md) |
| **Ataraxia** | Greek *ataraxia*, "untroubledness" | The tranquility that, for the Pyrrhonists and Epicureans, follows suspension of judgment or freedom from fear of death and the gods. | [2](02-ancient-roots.md) |
| **Bad faith** | French *mauvaise foi* | Sartre's term for lying to oneself about one's freedom: pretending to be a thing with a fixed nature (the waiter who plays at being a waiter), or pretending to be pure freedom with no facts. | [11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md) |
| **Being-toward-death** | German *Sein zum Tode* | Heidegger's name for the authentic relation to one's own death: not brooding, but "running ahead" (*Vorlaufen*) to death as the horizon that makes a finite life a whole. | [9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md) |
| **Cosmic nihilism** | — | The view that the universe has no purpose, and is indifferent to human beings and their values. Pascal's "infinite spaces"; Lovecraft's and Thacker's "world-without-us." | [1](01-what-is-nihilism.md), [13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md) |
| **Cosmic pessimism** | — | The thesis that the universe is not merely indifferent but makes human purposes absurd, unthinkable or horrifying (Lovecraft, Ligotti, Thacker). | [13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md) |
| **Death of God** | German *Gott ist tot* | Nietzsche's phrase (*The Joyful Wisdom*, also called *The Gay Science*, §§108, 125, 343) for a cultural event, not a metaphysical proof: the collapse of belief in the Christian God as the ground of truth and value, and the long shadow that collapse casts. | [6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) |
| **Debunking argument** | — | An argument that a belief is unjustified because its origin (evolution, class interest, ressentiment) would have produced it whether or not it was true. Street's Darwinian dilemma is the leading example for morality. | [7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md), [12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) |
| **Despair** | Danish *Fortvivlelse* | Kierkegaard's "sickness unto death": a misrelation in the self, which fails to be itself before God. It includes the despair of not knowing that one is in despair. | [8](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md) |
| **Diversion** | French *divertissement* | Pascal's word for the hunting, gambling and chatter by which we avoid thinking about our condition. Ancestor of Zapffe's "distraction." | [3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md), [13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md) |
| **Emotivism** | — | A. J. Ayer's (and C. L. Stevenson's) view that moral sentences do not state facts but express feelings ("Boo!", "Hurrah!") and seek to influence others. A form of non-cognitivism. | [12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) |
| **Emptiness** | Sanskrit *śūnyatā* | In Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka, the lack of intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*) in all things, which arise only in dependence on causes, parts and concepts. Presented as a middle way between eternalism and annihilationism (*uccheda*), not as nihilism. | [2](02-ancient-roots.md), [15](15-beyond-nihilism.md) |
| **Epistemological nihilism** | — | The claim that nothing can be known, or that there is no justified belief. Ancient forms appear in Gorgias; the Pyrrhonists refused even that negative dogma. | [1](01-what-is-nihilism.md), [2](02-ancient-roots.md) |
| **Error theory** | — | J. L. Mackie's metaethical view that moral judgments purport to state objective, action-guiding ("objectively prescriptive") facts, but that there are no such facts, so all positive moral judgments are false. The strict sense of "moral nihilism." | [12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) |
| **Eternal recurrence** | German *ewige Wiederkunft* | Nietzsche's thought of "the heaviest burden" (*The Joyful Wisdom* §341, tr. Thomas Common; "the greatest weight" in Kaufmann): would you will to live your life again, exactly the same, innumerable times? Read as a cosmological hypothesis, a test of affirmation, or both. | [7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md) |
| **Existence precedes essence** | French *l'existence précède l'essence* | Sartre's slogan: human beings have no fixed nature given in advance; they first exist and then define themselves by what they do. | [11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md) |
| **Existential nihilism** | — | The view that human life has no meaning or point. The question of Ecclesiastes ("What profit hath a man?") and of Camus. | [1](01-what-is-nihilism.md) |
| **Fictionalism (moral)** | — | Richard Joyce's proposal that error theorists keep using moral language as a useful fiction, "accepting" moral claims without believing them. | [12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) |
| **For-itself / in-itself** | French *l'être-pour-soi* / *l'être-en-soi* | Sartre's two regions of being: consciousness, which is a kind of nothingness, always beyond what it is; and things, which simply are what they are. | [11](11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md) |
| **Leap** | Danish *Spring* | Kierkegaard's term for the qualitative transition between existence-spheres, above all into faith, which cannot be reached by reasoning. | [8](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md) |
| **Last man** | German *der letzte Mensch* | Nietzsche's figure, in *Zarathustra*'s Prologue, of a humanity that has given up striving for anything great: comfortable, equal and pleased with itself: "'We have discovered happiness'—say the last men, and blink thereby" (tr. Thomas Common). | [6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) |
| **Leveling** | Danish *Nivellering* | Kierkegaard's term (*The Present Age*) for the anonymous process by which "the public" presses every distinction of quality into the average. | [8](08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md) |
| **Metaphysical nihilism** | — | The thesis that there might have been nothing at all (Baldwin's subtraction argument), or, in mereology, that composite objects such as tables do not exist. | [1](01-what-is-nihilism.md) |
| **Moral nihilism** | — | The view that there are no moral facts, so nothing is morally right or wrong. It does not say "everything is permitted," since "permitted" is itself a moral term. | [1](01-what-is-nihilism.md), [12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) |
| **Nihilism** | Latin *nihil*, "nothing"; German *Nihilismus* | Used in many senses. In this course: the view or experience that some fundamental domain (meaning, value, knowledge, reality, political authority) is empty. For most of its history the word was an accusation, not a self-description. | [1](01-what-is-nihilism.md), [3](03-the-word-and-its-birth.md) |
| **Non-cognitivism** | — | The family of views (emotivism, prescriptivism, expressivism) on which moral sentences do not aim to state facts. Its modern defenders deny that it is nihilism. | [12](12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) |
| **Nothing, the** | German *das Nichts* | In Heidegger's "What Is Metaphysics?", not the logical negation of beings but what anxiety reveals when beings as a whole slip away: "the nothing itself nihilates" (*das Nichts selbst nichtet*). Carnap called the sentence meaningless. | [9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md) |
| **Onto-theology** | — | Heidegger's name for the structure of Western metaphysics, which grounds all beings in a highest being and so forgets Being itself. Its culmination, on his reading, is Nietzsche and the nihilism of technology. | [9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md) |
| **Passive nihilism** | German *passiver Nihilismus* | Nietzsche's term for nihilism as a sign of "the *collapse* and *decline* of spiritual *strength*": exhaustion that no longer believes in any values and seeks only anesthesia. | [6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) |
| **Pessimism** | from Latin *pessimus*, "worst" | The view that the bad in existence outweighs the good. Unlike nihilism, it affirms that value exists and finds the balance negative. | [5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md), [13](13-pessimism-and-antinatalism.md) |
| **Pessimism controversy** | German *Pessimismusstreit* | The German debate, from the 1860s to the 1890s, over whether life is worth living, sparked by Schopenhauer, Hartmann and Mainländer (Frederick Beiser's term). | [5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md) |
| **Political nihilism** | Russian *nigilizm* | The 1860s Russian refusal of every inherited authority (Church, Tsar, family, custom) in the name of science and utility, later a label for revolutionary terrorism. | [4](04-russian-nihilism.md) |
| **Ressentiment** | French, used by Nietzsche | The vengefulness of the powerless, which, in Nietzsche's genealogy, ends by "becoming creative and giving birth to values" (*Genealogy* I §10, tr. Horace B. Samuel): the slave revolt in morality. | [7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md) |
| **Revaluation of all values** | German *Umwertung aller Werte* | Nietzsche's project of re-examining and re-ranking inherited values by asking what kind of life they serve. | [7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md) |
| **Revolt** | French *la révolte* | Camus's response to the absurd: refusing both hope and resignation, living in full awareness of the absurd. In *The Rebel*, revolt discovers a shared limit and a solidarity, summed up in a parody of Descartes: *Je me révolte, donc nous sommes*. | [10](10-camus-and-the-absurd.md) |
| **Suspension of judgment** | Greek *epochē* | The Pyrrhonist's response to the equal force (*isostheneia*) of opposing arguments: neither affirming nor denying, which is followed by *ataraxia*. | [2](02-ancient-roots.md) |
| **The "they"** | German *das Man* | Heidegger's name for the anonymous "one" of everyday life ("one doesn't do that"), which relieves each person of choosing. | [9](09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md) |
| **Übermensch** | German, "overman" or "superman" | Nietzsche's figure, introduced in *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, of a human being who creates values and affirms life after the death of God. Its meaning is much disputed. | [7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md) |
| **Vanity** | Hebrew *hevel*, "vapor, breath" | The key word of Ecclesiastes ("vanity of vanities"): what is fleeting, insubstantial and beyond grasping. | [2](02-ancient-roots.md) |
| **Weak thought** | Italian *pensiero debole* | Gianni Vattimo's program of embracing "accomplished nihilism": the weakening of strong metaphysical structures as an opportunity for interpretation, tolerance and charity. | [14](14-nihilism-in-modern-culture.md) |
| **Will** | German *Wille* | For Schopenhauer, the thing-in-itself behind the world as representation: a blind, endless striving that makes life oscillate between pain and boredom. | [5](05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md) |
| **Will to power** | German *Wille zur Macht* | Nietzsche's hypothesis that living things seek above all to discharge and increase their strength; also the title of a posthumous compilation of his notes. | [6](06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md), [7](07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md) |
